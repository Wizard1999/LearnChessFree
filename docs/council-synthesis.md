## 2. The deliberation

### Round 1. What should a beginner learn first?

- **Capablanca** favours starting with simple endings so piece power is seen undistracted, then middlegame, then openings [R].
- **Brunia** (Steps) favours very small steps with the same motifs returning at greater complexity [R/S].
- **Polgár** favours early, heavy tactical exposure; the child solves mates before theory [R].
- **Heisman** insists the first habit is not hanging pieces; "safety" precedes cleverness [R].
- **Lasker** and **Chernev** (witness) both lean on explaining *why* in plain language, one move at a time [R].
- **Sweller** warns that novices need worked examples and short steps; problem-solving alone overloads them [S]. **Chi** adds that prompting learners to explain each step beats just reading it [R].
- **Silman** (via *The Amateur's Mind*, witness) notes that many beginners' real errors are not missing theory but missing awareness [R].

**Collective:** Nobody disputes that rules and safety come first; they dispute *flavour*. Capablanca's endgame-first ordering is a teaching *device* (simplicity), not a requirement. We can take its benefit by making early mates and pawn races part of Tier 1, without delaying real play. Polgár's volume is right in kind (patterns need many exposures) but not for the first hour. A learner who has played three short games has a reason to care about the next lesson, which none of the printed curricula can provide but software can.

**Decision CD-1.** Rules through action → check/mate/stalemate → safety → first full games within about two hours; endgame flavour (K+Q vs K, pawn races) lives in Tier 1; tactics begin in session 5; text cards under ~60 words, each followed by a board task. *Provenance:* Capablanca (simplicity, adapted), Brunia (small steps), Heisman (safety first), Sweller/Chi (worked examples, self-explanation); Lichess Learn's structure was inspected and shows the same shape [V]. *Test:* does completion of sessions 1–2 predict success on unlabeled session-3 items?

### Round 2. How do tactics become usable in a real game?

- **Polgár** and **Smith/Tikkanen** (witness, the Woodpecker Method) argue for repetition: the same set, several cycles, each faster; the first cycle about four weeks, each later cycle about half as long [V, secondary]. Final cycles emphasise spotting the idea at speed [V, secondary].
- **Brunia** argues for a systematic motif order [R/S]; **Yusupov** returns to tactical themes across all nine volumes [V, secondary].
- **Kasparov** and **Carlsen** are widely understood to rely on pattern recognition learned from enormous game exposure [I].
- **Gobet** explains the mechanism: expertise is stored patterns (chunks and larger templates) [R]. **Bilalić** shows the danger: a familiar solution can block a better one, an effect measurable even in strong players and strongest in weaker ones [V].
- **Rohrer / Bjork**: mixed (interleaved), spaced, effortful practice transfers better than blocked repetition, even though it feels harder [V].
- **The Tactics Detection Workbook** (Schlepütz & Emms, witness) is built on the fact that real games do not promise a tactic exists [S].
- **Chesscademy users** (record, not a seat): "tactics are 90% of this website," and bad tactics are the main frustration [V].

**Collective:** The Woodpecker people and the interleaving people are answering different questions. Repetition of a fixed set buys **fluency**: speed and automaticity on known patterns. Interleaving fresh items buys **transfer**: choosing the right idea when nobody tells you. Training only the first can teach diagrams, which is exactly the failure our falsification list names. Bilalić tells us fluency can also cause set effects, so every fluency set must be paired with items where the familiar answer is *not* best.

**Decision CD-2.** Two separate tactic tracks with separate scoring: **fluency sets** (fixed verified sets, repeated at increasing speed, Woodpecker-style) and **transfer sets** (fresh instances, interleaved, unlabeled, including no-tactic positions and "familiar answer is not best" items). Only transfer performance counts toward mastery. *Provenance:* Smith/Tikkanen, Polgár (fluency); Rohrer, Bjork, Bilalić, Detection Workbook (transfer). *Test:* fluency scores rising while transfer is flat is a failure we must detect.

### Round 3. How should people think about moves?

- **Kotov** (witness) built the candidate-list and analysis-tree discipline: list candidates, analyse each branch once, decide [R]. **Aagaard**: candidates, comparison, elimination, and avoid repeated rechecking [R].
- **Dvoretsky**: always search for the opponent's best resource; analysis is not finished until the defender has had his say [R].
- **Heisman**: a practical process for adults, starting with the opponent's last move and safety, then checks/captures/threats [R].
- **Ramesh**: calculation is trainable with structured, graded exercises and written variations [S].
- **Rowson** (witness): the "sins" are largely thinking failures, such as inertia and wishful ("hope") play, not knowledge gaps [R].
- **Carlsen / Nakamura** (as seats/lens): fast intuition for most moves, deep calculation selectively [I].
- **Ericsson**: improvement needs feedback on the *process*; **Kalyuga**: scaffolds fade as skill grows [S].

**Collective:** These are one idea at different resolutions. The beginner's version is "is anything attacked?"; the master's is selective deep search; the *skill* is allocating depth. Nobody printed a way to train that allocation, but a platform can: it can log time and depth per decision and ask "was that move worth the thinking?" We should version the routine rather than present a different method at each level.

**Decision CD-3.** One **Thinking Loop**, versioned by tier: T1 safety scan → T2 checks/captures/threats and forcing replies → T3 candidate list, short lines → T4 compare resulting positions → T5 depth allocation and best-defence checking → T6+ elimination and verification. Calculation exercises require entering a **variation**, not only a first move, with step-level feedback; "when to stop" is an explicit node. *Provenance:* Kotov, Aagaard, Dvoretsky, Heisman, Ramesh, Rowson, Silman. *Test:* does unprompted loop use in games rise and then stay when prompts fade?

### Round 4. Positional play and planning: rules or concrete thinking?

- **Nimzowitsch** (witness): named positional concepts: prophylaxis, blockade, over-protection, restraint, the open file and the seventh rank [R]. **Steinitz**: positional accumulation justifies attack [R].
- **Silman**: evaluate *imbalances* before planning [R]. **Stean** (witness): simple recurring ideas such as weak squares and open files [R].
- **Watson** (witness): classical rules have exceptions; modern play is rule-independent [R]. **Flores Rios** and **Soltis** (witnesses): plans follow pawn structures; learn structures as families [R].
- **Karpov / Petrosian-style play** [I]: prophylaxis and restraint, taught by games more than rules.
- **Rowson** (Zebras, witness): chess is complex; avoid false certainty [R].
- **Schwartz & Bransford** (contrasting cases) [R]: learners understand a concept better when they compare cases that differ in one feature.

**Collective:** Beginners need rules, and rules are cheap to teach and expensive to unlearn. The reconciliation is to teach every positional idea as a **conditioned default**: what it says, when it holds, a failing look-alike, and an irrelevant case. That is exactly the contrast-pair family our spike generates. It also fits Chesscademy's own path names: "Basic Planning and Awareness" then "Advanced Strategy" with imbalances and prophylaxis [V].

**Decision CD-4.** Positional ideas are taught as **contrast families** (works / fails / irrelevant), with explicit conditions; imbalances checklist at Tier 4; structure families at Tier 4–5; prophylaxis as a named node at Tier 5; exceptions and dynamic play from Tier 3. *Provenance:* Nimzowitsch, Silman, Watson, Flores Rios, Soltis, Stean, Schwartz [R]. *Test:* can learners predict whether an idea survives a one-feature change?

### Round 5. Attack and sacrifice

- **Vuković** (witness): attacks work when specific conditions line up (development lead, open lines, superiority at the point of attack, the king's cover) [R]. **Spielmann** (witness): sacrifices come in types; sound ones have reasons [R].
- **Tal**: complication and practical chances; some sacrifices are objectively unsound but practically strong [R/I]. **Kasparov**: the initiative has a price and a clock [R]. **Alekhine**: long preparation of the decisive blow [R].
- **Aagaard / Dvoretsky**: calculate before you sacrifice; defend by counter-sacrifice [R].
- **User memory:** the Greek gift appears in Lichess's Practice list [V] and was a Chesscademy memory; its conditions are the standard teaching example.

**Collective:** An attack lesson that says "sacrifice on h7" without its conditions teaches losing games. We have a machine that can tell the difference, which Spielmann and Vuković did not.

**Decision CD-5.** Attack lessons list their **conditions**, include declined lines, and verify claims at the level "wins ≥ X or mates"; items flagged **"practical, not sound"** (Tal-style) are labelled as such and never used as unqualified correct answers. *Provenance:* Vuković, Spielmann, Tal, Kasparov, Aagaard; our claim-level verification finding [V]. *Test:* items where the greedy "famous sacrifice" fails must exist in every attack family.

### Round 6. Endgames

- **Capablanca**: elementary endings first; economy [R]. **Dvoretsky**: exact technique, resources, theory depth [R]. **Müller & Lamprecht** (witness): comprehensive reference [R]. **de la Villa** (witness): a curated list of 100 essential endings [R]. **Shereshevsky**: strategic principles of endgames (king activity, weaknesses, exchanges) [R]. **Silman's** *Complete Endgame Course*: content organised by playing class [R].
- **Carlsen**: wins technically equal endings by relentless small improvements [I].
- **Tablebases**: exact results for 7 or fewer pieces [V, public service used in this project].

**Collective:** The "must-know" list and the strategic principles are not rivals: principles tell you what to try; verified positions tell you what is true. Our advantage is a perfect opponent: a tablebase-backed bot that never misses a draw in drawn endings.

**Decision CD-6.** Endgames are a spine from Tier 1: principle → verified family → variations (including failing look-alikes) → play it out against a tablebase-backed opponent (Tier 4+). The "must-know" list is mapped to nodes with provisional tiers (G0). *Provenance:* Capablanca, Dvoretsky, Shereshevsky, de la Villa, Silman, Müller. *Test:* conversion/hold rate against the tablebase bot, measured separately from recognition.

### Round 7. Openings: how much, when?

- **Capablanca / Lasker**: principles, minimal theory early [R]. **Fischer / Kasparov**: deep, concrete preparation as a weapon [R]. **Carlsen**: avoid heavy theory early and outplay in the middlegame [I].
- **Yusupov / Steps**: opening ideas introduced alongside other themes, not as memorisation [R]. **Flores Rios**: openings lead to structures, structures to plans [R]. **Seirawan / Alburt** (witnesses): beginner-friendly repertoire guidance [R].
- **Chesscademy**: one lesson overview of openings, a Sicilian Dragon path later [V].
- **Education:** retrieval and spacing suit forced lines [V]; understanding must precede memorising.

**Collective:** The user's own account of why Chesscademy worked is that it did *not* demand memorised history. Memory should be reserved for forcing lines where one lapse loses, and the repertoire should be built from the openings the learner actually faces.

**Decision CD-7.** Opening ladder (principles → reasons-first small repertoire → structures → preparation → research) and a **"they left the book"** node family from Tier 1; every line ends at a model position with plans and a resulting endgame link; spaced review only for forcing lines; repertoire driven by the learner's own opponent frequencies (the CC0 Lichess games data can supply base rates [V: licence]). *Provenance:* Capablanca, Lasker, Fischer, Kasparov, Flores Rios, Yusupov, Steps, Chesscademy history.

### Round 8. Studying master games

- **Chernev** (witness): explain every move in plain language [R]; *Most Instructive Games* groups games by lesson [R]. **Fischer**: precise, concrete annotations [R]. **Bronstein** (witness, Zurich 1953): the human story of decisions under uncertainty [R]. **Tal**: the story of calculation and nerves [R]. **Alekhine / Kasparov**: planning and historical depth [R]. **Kotov** (witness): "guess the move" thinking [R].
- **Chi / Sweller**: explaining steps and studying worked examples teach [R/S]. **Dunlosky**: rereading is low utility, practice testing is high [V].

**Collective:** Reading a great game is rereading unless you commit to something first. Prediction turns a worked example into a test.

**Decision CD-8.** Master-game study = **guess-the-move**, scored not by "engine top move" but by agreement on the *plan class* and by whether the choice is sound; each game carries a "plan for each side?" prompt; games are curated into families by structure. Text commentary is original; the moves of a game are facts and may be used. *Provenance:* Chernev, Kotov, Fischer, Bronstein, Tal, Kasparov; Chi, Dunlosky.

### Round 9. Repetition, spacing, volume

- **Dunlosky**: practice testing and distributed practice are high utility; interleaving moderate [V]. **Rohrer**: strong delayed-test advantage for interleaving [V]. **Woodpecker / Polgár**: volume and repeated passes [V, secondary]. **Yusupov**: test every chapter, revisit themes [V, secondary].
- **Gobet & Campitelli**: practice is necessary but not sufficient, and hours to master vary eight-fold [V, secondary]. **Hambrick**: practice explains around a third of variance [S]. **Koedinger**: use learning curves to find over- and under-practised skills [V].

**Collective:** We cannot promise a number of hours or a rating. We can promise *measurement*: for each skill, the learner's own curve says whether more practice of that kind is still paying.

**Decision CD-9.** Sessions start with retrieval; review is scheduled at the **concept** level using fresh instances (not the same diagram); interleave after an initial block; cap daily reviews; track a learning curve per knowledge component; **never promise outcomes or timelines**. *Provenance:* Dunlosky, Rohrer, Bjork, Koedinger, Gobet & Campitelli, Hambrick.

### Round 10. Feedback, hints and explanation

- **VanLehn**: tutoring at the *step* level approaches the effect of human tutoring [V, secondary]. **Chi**: self-explanation. **Sweller / Kalyuga**: worked examples for novices, faded as expertise grows [S].
- **Dvoretsky**: analysis is judged by the quality of its argument [R].
- **Chesscademy record [V]**: single-answer exercises that rejected valid moves; hints that repeated and never escalated; no "why was I wrong"; some tactics looked auto-generated. The founder planned to fix these.

**Collective:** The failures are all failures of step-level feedback and answer-key correctness, which is what the validator and the detectors exist for.

**Decision CD-10.** Step-level checking of variations; escalating hint ladder (attention → relation → partial line → solution); every reason is produced by detectors or reviewed text; accepted-answer *sets* verified by engine; support fades by evidence, and returns on failure. *Provenance:* VanLehn, Chi, Sweller/Kalyuga, Koedinger, Chesscademy post-mortem.

### Round 11. Self-direction: when does the student take the wheel?

- **Botvinnik**: analyse your own games rigorously; regular self-examination and a training regime [R]. **Dvoretsky**: self-assessment and analysing your own games are the first topics of his training course [V: *Secrets of Chess Training* contents, secondary]. **Heisman**: adults need a process they can follow alone [R]. **Rowson**: know your own thinking failures [R].
- **Zimmerman**: self-regulated learners plan, act, and reflect in a cycle [R]. **Kalyuga**: guidance must fade, but only after competence appears [S].
- **Ericsson**: good practice is individualised and feedback-rich [R]; **Gobet & Campitelli** found group practice mattered and individual practice less so [V, secondary], a caution that solitary self-directed work is not automatically good.
- **The Guardian study** (the user's document): four roles (care → demonstration → coached practice → independent), success = declining assistance with maintained performance, silent "rehearsal of absence," and the finding that unguarded AI help can raise practice scores while lowering later unaided scores [V: user's document, finding not independently checked].

**Collective:** Botvinnik's independence and Dvoretsky's coached analysis are both right for different people at different times. A rating is a poor guide to which. Evidence about *unprompted* behaviour is a better one.

**Decision CD-11.** A self-direction strand built on a plan–act–review cycle from Tier 0 (one-line reflection after the first game); role fading by **evidence, not rating**; silent sessions; confidence calibration; own-plan experiments from stage 4; a portable training manual export. Novices start guided. *Provenance:* Botvinnik, Dvoretsky, Heisman, Rowson, Zimmerman, Kalyuga, the Guardian study.

### Round 12. Own games and error diagnosis

- **Silman**: classify amateur errors; patterns repeat [R]. **Rowson**: name your recurring thinking sin [R]. **Botvinnik / Dvoretsky**: annotate before consulting an engine; find the critical moments [R]. **Heisman**: separate errors of process from errors of knowledge [R].
- **Kasparov / Aagaard** (via the engine era) [I]: an engine's best move is not an explanation.

**Collective:** The engine tells us *what*; the learner's annotation tells us *what they saw*; the gap between them is the diagnosis.

**Decision CD-12.** Own-game review protocol: learner marks critical moments and predicted plans **before** engine output; deterministic detectors tag concept-opportunities; a small error taxonomy (not seen / seen-but-miscalculated / misjudged / time-pressure / out of book), assigned *tentatively* with questions to the learner. *Provenance:* Botvinnik, Dvoretsky, Silman, Rowson, Heisman.

### Round 13. Assessment and ratings

- **Glicko-2** is Lichess's system [V] and Chesscademy's tactic rating [V]. **Klinkenberg et al.**: Elo-style, on-the-fly estimation of learner ability and item difficulty without pretesting [S]. **Koedinger**: model knowledge components and read learning curves [V].
- **Yusupov / Steps**: per-chapter tests [V, secondary]. **Chesscademy**: game sets and checkpoints to test out [V].
- **Hambrick / Gobet**: huge individual variance; do not predict [V].

**Collective:** A rating can describe an item or a person on one scale; it cannot be attached to a concept. We keep the draft's four-attribute separation and evidence grades.

**Decision CD-13.** Cue-weighted evidence; item and learner co-estimation with uncertainty; **test-out checkpoints** (Chesscademy) by performance at cue 3–4; evidence grades G0–G3 on every rating claim; Lichess-anchored internal scale. *Provenance:* Glicko-2, Klinkenberg, Koedinger, Yusupov, Steps, Chesscademy.

### Round 14. Practical performance, mind, and motivation

- **Rowson**: thinking inertia and psychological error; **Heisman**: time use, slow play; **Smith** (witness, *Pump Up Your Rating*): practical training habits [R]. **Kasparov**: intensity and preparation; **Carlsen**: composure over a long event [I].
- **Self-determination research** (autonomy, competence, relatedness) [R] suggests motivation lasts when learners feel in control and see real progress.
- **Chesscademy**: badges and streaks existed; there is no evidence here that they improved learning [V: features; no outcomes data].

**Collective:** Streaks reward showing up, not learning. We want the reward to be *evidence of capability*.

**Decision CD-14.** Clock literacy at Tier 1 and budgeting at Tier 2; error taxonomy under time; loss-handling and workload modules; no punitive streaks; a visible evidence-based progress statement instead of points. *Provenance:* Rowson, Heisman, Smith, Chesscademy, self-determination research.

### Round 15. Engines and AI

- **Kasparov**: engines make human-machine cooperation possible but demand human judgment [R/I]. **Aagaard**: engines can mislead [I]. **Maia** projects: models of human-like play at stated Lichess ratings, trained on Lichess games [V].
- **VanLehn**: tutor effectiveness depends on the *step-level* design, not on how "AI" it is [V, secondary]. **Guardian study**: independent unaided performance is what counts [V: user's document].

**Collective:** The engine is an excellent *judge* and a poor *teacher*. A language model is a good *author's assistant* and a bad *oracle*.

**Decision CD-15.** Engines verify and spar (including a tablebase-perfect opponent); learners analyse **engine-last**; AI drafts content offline; **no live AI in the learner's loop in v1** (restating D3); an optional grounded dialogue later, fed only verified facts. *Provenance:* Kasparov, Aagaard, VanLehn, Maia, the Guardian study.

### Round 16. Rights, sources and originality

- All instructional text is **original**; moves of games and bare positions are facts, but annotations, translations, diagrams, and curated sets are protected [S, earlier U.S. Copyright Office source]. The Lichess puzzle and game data are CC0 [V]. Lichess's software is copyleft (Chessground GPL-3.0, the tablebase server AGPL-3.0 [V]); I did not verify the licence of its lesson module, so we take ideas and structure only, never its code or lesson text.

**Decision CD-16.** Every source gets a **synthesis sheet** (definitions, agreements, disagreements, exceptions, examples, engine/tablebase checks, original explanation, graded items, rights record). Public-domain classics (Lasker, Capablanca, Nimzowitsch, Alekhine and others, depending on edition/translation) supply games and ideas; no wholesale reuse of protected annotations.

## 3. The Collective's final deliberation

*(Synthetic voice. Every claim carries the decision it rests on.)*

"Here is where the many agreed.

**One:** a skill is not learned until it works **when nobody tells you which skill applies** (CD-2, CD-4; Bilalić, Rohrer, the Detection Workbook, Chesscademy's 'game set' idea). **Two:** every claim shown to a learner is machine-checked or human-reviewed (CD-10; Chesscademy's failures, VanLehn). **Three:** ideas are taught by contrast and with their conditions (CD-4, CD-5; Watson, Schwartz, Silman). **Four:** the thinking process is a single routine that grows (CD-3; Kotov, Aagaard, Heisman, Dvoretsky). **Five:** games feed the curriculum and the learner's own annotations come before the engine (CD-12; Botvinnik, Silman). **Six:** control passes to the learner by evidence of unprompted competence, not by rating (CD-11; Botvinnik, Dvoretsky, Zimmerman, the Guardian study).

Here is where we did **not** agree, and what would settle it:

1. **Endgames first (Capablanca) or tactics early (Polgár)?** We chose a hybrid; test: sessions 1–5 completion and unlabeled transfer.
2. **Repetition volume (Woodpecker) vs fresh transfer (Rohrer):** we run both, separated; test: do fluency gains transfer?
3. **How much opening memorisation?** Fischer/Kasparov say deep; Capablanca/Lasker say little. We say: as much as the learner's real games demand, no earlier; test: opening-related losses by cause.
4. **Sound vs practical sacrifices (Tal vs engine):** labelled, never mixed; test: reviewer agreement.
5. **Coached or independent analysis (Dvoretsky vs Botvinnik):** fade by evidence; test: whether learners who self-direct earlier do worse on unaided tasks.
6. **Whether a rating per concept can mean anything:** we say no; per item and per learner only; test: held-out prediction.

Here is what we **cannot know yet:** whether this particular combination outperforms a good book plus a coach, or any competitor. That is a measurement problem, and the curriculum is built to produce the measurements."
