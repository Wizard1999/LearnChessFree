# Research review: teaching beginners fast and deeply (8 Nov 2026)

Purpose: the evidence behind the design (hook, short early lessons, unlabeled testing, contrast lab, games feeding the graph, fading support). Original wording; one short quote at most per source.

## Method and honesty notes
- Tools available: web search summaries and a page fetcher. PubMed pages were blocked (cookie wall) and one full text returned 403, so **most claims below are [S-snip]: bibliographic details and numbers come from search summaries of abstracts or repository pages, not from my own reading of the paper.** Where a number is marked "unverified" it appeared only in a secondary source. Nothing here is [V] in the project sense (verified by running something here).
- Items marked [R] are recalled from memory and were not looked up in this session; treat as leads to confirm, with no effect sizes stated.
- Grades: G0 guess, G1 single weak study, G2 several consistent studies or a meta-analysis, G3 replicated. The grade is for the *finding as a design basis*, not for the paper's quality. Effect sizes are as reported by the sources (g = Hedges, d = Cohen, r = correlation); they come from different outcome types and are not comparable with each other.

## A. Chess instruction and transfer

**A1. Sala and Gobet (2016), "Do the benefits of chess instruction transfer to academic and cognitive skills? A meta-analysis", Educational Research Review.** https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/102237 [S-snip]
Measured: 24 studies (40 effect sizes), about 2,788 children taught chess vs 2,433 controls; outcomes in maths, reading, general cognitive skills. Result as reported: overall g = 0.338 (CI .242 to .435), maths g = 0.382, reading g = 0.248, cognitive skills about 0.33; studies with more than 25 hours of chess gave 0.43. Authors stress that almost no study had an active control group. Grade: G2 for "small to moderate in weak designs". Consequence: do not sell chess as a general brain booster; our claim is chess skill, plus the metacognitive habits we teach explicitly (see A4).

**A2. Sala and Gobet (2017), "Does far transfer exist? Negative evidence from chess, music, and working memory training", Curr Dir Psychol Sci. DOI 10.1177/0963721417712760** [S-snip, Europe PMC abstract summary]
Measured: meta-analyses of chess, music and working-memory training on children's cognitive and academic skills. Result: small to moderate effects whose size falls as design quality rises (active controls); the authors conclude far transfer rarely occurs. Numbers not seen in the abstract. Grade: G2. Consequence: same as A1; any "learning how to learn" claim must be tested by us (open question Q1), not borrowed from chess-transfer studies.

**A3. Sala and Gobet (2017), "Does chess instruction improve mathematical problem-solving ability? Two experimental studies with an active control group", Learning & Behavior. DOI 10.3758/s13420-017-0280-3** [S-snip]
Measured: Exp. 1 N = 233 (grades 3-4), 25 hours of chess vs checkers vs passive control; Exp. 2 N = 52, chess vs Go vs passive. Result: no significant differences in maths problem solving or metacognition; effect sizes not seen. Grade: G1-G2 (two experiments, consistent with A1-A2). Consequence: same; also shows what "chess instruction" studies measure: school maths, not chess strength.

**A4. Sala, Foley and Gobet (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8:238, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00238** [S-snip, title/keywords only; abstract not seen]
Review of the state of the art and the placebo problem in chess-instruction studies. Use only as a pointer; open access via Europe PMC PMC5322219. Not graded.

**What chess-instruction studies did NOT measure:** how fast novices reach playable chess, retention of chess knowledge, or comparisons of teaching methods for chess itself. This is a gap in the literature, not a finding (see Q2).

**A5. Burgoyne, Sala, Gobet, Macnamara, Campitelli and Hambrick (2016), "The relationship between cognitive ability and chess skill: a comprehensive meta-analysis", Intelligence 59, 72-83. DOI 10.1016/j.intell.2016.08.002; corrigendum Intelligence 71 (2018) 92-96.** [S-snip]
Measured: 19 studies, about 1,800 players. Result: chess skill correlates with fluid reasoning r = .24, short-term memory r = .25, numerical ability r = .35 (the processing-speed and crystallised figures .24/.22 were weakened by the 2018 correction); the link is stronger in young and lower-skilled players. Grade: G2. Consequence: general ability matters most early; this supports making early steps low in working-memory demand and not assuming a beginner can hold a board plus a paragraph in mind (see C1).

## B. Expertise research

**B1. Chase and Simon (1973), "Perception in chess", Cognitive Psychology 4.** [S-snip: only a secondary description seen] Proposed that expertise rests on a large store of recognised piece patterns (chunks); a first estimate of about 50,000 chunks. Experts recall real positions far better than random ones [R: the well-known result; not re-read here]. Grade: G3 for the basic real-vs-random recall advantage [R]; the chunk count is an estimate (G1). Consequence: teach recurring patterns (mates, forks, pawn structures) as named, repeatedly seen units, and test with positions from real game contexts, not only random puzzles.

**B2. Gobet and Simon (1996), "Templates in chess memory: a mechanism for recalling several boards", Cognitive Psychology 31.** [S-snip] Argues that expert recall exceeds short-term-memory limits because large chunks grow into templates with slots, stored in long-term memory. Grade: G2 (computer-model plus several experiments, still debated). Consequence: repeated exposure to the *same family of structures with varying details* is what builds slots; supports our contrast-lab and interleaved-variant design (see C4).

**B3. Charness, Tuffiash, Krampe, Reingold and Vasyukova (2005), "The role of deliberate practice in chess expertise", Applied Cognitive Psychology 19, 151-165.** [S-snip] Two samples of rated players estimated their chess activities; serious solitary study was the best practice predictor of rating. A later reanalysis (Burgoyne, Nye, Macnamara, Charness and Hambrick, 2019) reports that deliberate-practice estimates explain 34% of variance in chess skill after correcting for unreliability (and serious chess activity plus instruction plus starting age explain 63% of peak rating variance, as relayed by the search summary). Grade: G2 (retrospective self-report, correlational). Consequence: practice that is serious, solitary and tested matters; but it is an association from self-reports. Our "decks and unlabeled problems" fit, and games alone are not enough.

**B4. Campitelli and Gobet (2008), "The role of practice in chess: a longitudinal study", Learning and Individual Differences 18(4), 446-458.** [S-snip] Strong link between practice hours and skill; group practice predicted high performance better than individual practice; masters outrated experts after about three years of serious play despite similar practice totals. The related 2007 Developmental Psychology paper (Gobet and Campitelli) is the source of the "about 11,000 hours to reach 2200 on average, with large individual variability" figure, per a ChessBase write-up of it (secondary; not seen in the 2008 abstract). Grade: G2. Consequence: individual differences in hours needed are large, so any promise of a fixed time to a rating is unsupported; show progress by evidence, not by hours. Group/discussion practice has some support, which backs a future social layer but is not v1.

## C. Learning science relevant to design

**C1. Split attention and spatial contiguity.** Schroeder and Cenkci (2018), Educational Psychology Review 30, 679-701, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9435-9 [S-snip]: meta-analysis, 58 comparisons, 2,426 participants, overall g = 0.63 favouring text integrated with, or next to, the relevant part of a diagram over separated text. Grade: G2. Consequence: put the explanation as labels, arrows, or a caption touching the board square or piece, not in a paragraph below; keep the board visible while the text changes. Test on a phone where scrolling separates them.

**C2. Worked examples and expertise reversal.** Barbieri et al. (2023), Educational Psychology Review 35, art. 11 [S-snip]: meta-analysis of worked examples in mathematics, g = 0.48; self-explanation prompts moderated the effect (the snippet's direction is unclear; do not rely on it); correct examples beat incorrect-only sets. Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003), Educational Psychologist 38, 23-31 [S-snip, metadata and secondary summary only]: guidance that helps novices can stop helping or hurt as expertise grows; critics note much of the support uses subjective load ratings. Grades: worked examples G2 (maths, not chess); expertise reversal G2 but with measurement caveats. Consequence: early lessons should show-then-ask (matches "show it, then say it"); support must fade by evidence of competence (hints and demos off for strong learners; placement or quick-skip), which is already design principle D-fade. Chess has no meta-analysis of its own here (Q3).

**C3. Retrieval practice (testing effect).** Rowland (2014), Psychological Bulletin 140, 1432-1463, PMID 25150680 [S-snip]: meta-analysis; testing beats restudy; recall tests beat recognition tests; larger when feedback follows. A blog gives overall d = 0.50 (unverified, do not cite). Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017), Review of Educational Research 87, 659-701, DOI 10.3102/0034654316689306 [S-snip]: 118 articles; practice tests beat restudy and other comparisons; no pooled number seen. Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Psychological Science, PMID 16507066 [S-snip: bibliographic record only]. Dunlosky et al. (2013), Psychological Science in the Public Interest, DOI 10.1177/1529100612453266 [S-snip]: practice testing and distributed practice rated high utility; interleaving, self-explanation and elaborative interrogation moderate; rereading and highlighting low. Grade: G3 (replicated for verbal and text material; chess-position retrieval is untested, Q4). Consequence: lessons should end each step with the learner producing the move, not reading it; reread-style recap screens should be minimal; feedback after each attempt.

**C4. Spacing and interleaving.** Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006), Psychological Bulletin 132, 354-380, PMID 16719566 [S-snip]: 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments; the best gap grows with the retention interval (no single magic interval). Brunmair and Richter (2019), Psychological Bulletin 145, 1029-1052, PMID 31556629 [S-snip]: 59 studies, 238 effect sizes, overall g = 0.42 for interleaving; paintings and visual material g = 0.67; word lists favoured blocking (g = -0.39); benefit larger when categories are similar to each other. Grades: spacing G3 (verbal), interleaving G2. Consequence: chess positions are visual and category-confusable (fork vs pin vs skewer), the kind of material where interleaving helped; use blocked practice first for a new idea, then interleaved mixed sets. Schedule reviews with a growing gap. Both are supported for general learning, not yet for chess tactics (Q4).

**C5. Self-explanation.** Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi and Winne (2018), Educational Psychology Review, "Inducing self-explanation: a meta-analysis" [S-snip]: 64 reports, 69 effect sizes, weighted mean g = .55. Larsen, Butler and Roediger (2013), Medical Education [S-snip]: testing beat self-explanation alone after six months in 47 medical students, with self-explanation helping but less. Grade: G2. Consequence: ask "why does this fail?" with choices (no free text needed, and no live AI per rule 7) after a wrong attempt; do not substitute explanation for retrieval.

**C6. Feedback.** Wisniewski, Zierer and Hattie (2020), Frontiers in Psychology 10:3087, DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087 [S-snip]: random-effects mean d = 0.48 (435 studies), high heterogeneity; information content was the strongest moderator; larger for cognitive and motor than motivational outcomes. Grade: G2. Consequence: feedback must say what the move allowed (the refutation shown on the board), not just right/wrong or a score. Timing (immediate vs delayed) is not settled by anything I saw (Q5).

**C7. Problem-solving before instruction (productive failure).** Sinha and Kapur (2021), Review of Educational Research [S-snip]: 53 studies, 166 comparisons, bias-corrected g = 0.87 favouring problem-first; reported moderator that younger learners (grades 2-5) fared better with instruction-first (from a secondary Chinese-language page, unverified). Grade: G1-G2 (the strong figure is contested in the literature; Kirschner, Sweller and Clark 2006 argue minimal guidance fails [R]; not looked up). Consequence: for an adult mid-level player, "try first, then see" works for concepts they partly know; for true novices and children, demonstrate first. Our hook (guided first game) is guided, which is the safe middle.

**C8. Mastery learning.** Kulik, Kulik and Bangert-Drowns (1990), Review of Educational Research 60, 265-299, DOI 10.3102/00346543060002265 [S-snip]: 108 evaluations, positive effects; a secondary AI summary gives about 0.52 SD and larger gains for weaker students (unverified). Hattie 2009 synthesis d = 0.58 via a secondary source. Grade: G2 (mostly classroom courses, effects shrink in later critiques [R]). Consequence: gate on demonstrated competence with retries; this supports the graph with "next skills glow", but avoid hard locks that stop a strong player from skipping.

**C9. Gamification, stars, streaks.** Sailer and Homner (2020), Educational Psychology Review 32, 77-112 [S-snip]: pooled r = .31 (CI .11 to .47) for gamified vs not on learning outcomes; cognitive effect stable in rigorous studies, motivational and behavioural effects less stable; game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes. Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999), Psychological Bulletin, PMID 10589297 [S-snip]: 128 experiments; tangible rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation, d = -0.40 (engagement-contingent), -0.36 (completion-contingent), -0.28 (performance-contingent); self-reported interest -0.15/-0.17; worse for children; Cameron and Pierce (1994) dispute how widespread the harm is. Grade: gamification benefit G2 but unstable; reward harm G2 with active dispute. Consequence: use visible progress (the map filling) as information, not as paid-out points or streak penalties; no loss framing for missed days; avoid tangible-style rewards for merely "doing" a lesson; make competence feedback (you solved a class of positions) the reward. Streak mechanics specifically have no study found here (G0, Q6).

**C10. Optimal difficulty and the 85 percent rule.** Wilson, Shenhav, Straccia and Cohen (2019), Nature Communications 10:4646, DOI 10.1038/s41467-019-12552-4 [S-snip]: derives about 15.87% error as optimal for gradient-based learners on binary classification with Gaussian noise; press notes it was checked against animal learning and perceptual tasks, and the authors' scope is simple tasks with clear right/wrong. Grade: G1 for humans learning chess (theory; not tested on chess). Consequence: aim for a high but not perfect success rate in practice sets (our default target about 80-85% first-try) as a tunable, logged parameter, not a law; do not treat it as established for strategy concepts.

**C11. Desirable difficulties, hint timing, onboarding and time-to-first-success.**
- Desirable difficulties (Bjork and Bjork) [R]: spacing, interleaving, retrieval and generation slow initial performance but help retention. Not looked up here; grade rests on C3/C4. No separate claim.
- Hint timing: nothing verified in this session. Grade G0. Treat our hint ladder (try first, escalate) as a design choice to measure.
- Onboarding and activation: I found no peer-reviewed evidence that a short time to first success increases retention in learning apps in this session. The "get them playing within about 10 minutes" hook is a product judgement (G0 here), supported only indirectly by C7 and D below. We should log it (Q7).

## D. Play versus instruction

**D1. Weisberg, Hirsh-Pasek, Golinkoff, Kittredge and Klahr (2016), "Guided play: principles and practices", Curr Dir Psychol Sci 25, 177-182, DOI 10.1177/0963721416645512** [S-snip]. Argues for a middle path between free play and direct instruction: the adult sets up a goal-directed setting and the child explores. Outcome comparisons not seen in what I retrieved. Grade: G1-G2 (preschool domains such as shapes, not chess). Consequence: our guided first game (constrained, hinted, real play) matches the guided-play pattern.

**D2. Chess-specific play vs instruction.** I found no study comparing learning chess by playing with learning by instruction (adult or child) in this session. The deliberate-practice results (B3, B4) suggest serious study plus play both matter, with solitary study stronger in self-reports. Grade: G0 for causal claims. Consequence: keep the dual loop (guided play, then post-game review that feeds the graph) and measure it ourselves.

## E. Mobile learning of board-game skills

**E1. Sung, Chang and Liu (2016), "The effects of integrating mobile devices with teaching and learning on students' learning performance: a meta-analysis", Computers & Education 94, 252-275, DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2015.11.008** [S-snip]: 110 studies from 1993-2013; overall effect 0.523; a language-learning meta-analysis (Sung, Chang and Yang 2015) reports 0.55 over 44 studies (9,154 participants). Grade: G2 for mobile versus no-mobile, but these compare technology-supported with conventional teaching, not phone versus desktop and not board skills. Consequence: phone delivery is not disqualifying; the real risks are small boards and the split between text and board (C1).
**E2. Board-game skill on mobile:** no study found. Duolingo efficacy study (Vesselinov and Grego) was searched and not found. Grade: G0. Consequence: test touch targets, board size and one-handed play on real phones; the evidence will have to be our own.

## Design consequences
1. Claim chess skill only, never "makes you smarter" (A1-A3). Any learning-to-learn claim is a hypothesis we test (rule 6).
2. Early steps carry a low working-memory load and use direct show-then-ask; general ability matters most for beginners (A5, C2).
3. Place explanations on the board (labels, arrows, caption touching the square); never stack paragraph text away from the board (C1). Check on a phone.
4. End each step with the learner producing the move or choosing among options (retrieval), with feedback that shows the consequence (C3, C6).
5. Teach one pattern blocked first, then mix patterns in interleaved sets, using the contrast lab for look-alike ideas (C4, B2).
6. Space reviews with growing gaps; log first-try correctness and delay (C4).
7. After a wrong attempt, offer a "why" choice (self-explanation) but keep retrieval primary (C5).
8. Fade support by evidence: hints, demos and wording simplify as the learner succeeds; allow skip via placement (C2).
9. Gate on competence with retry, but soft-gate (suggest, do not lock) so strong players can skip (C8).
10. Progress shown as the map filling and competence statements; no streak-loss mechanics, no points for just showing up; avoid tangible-style rewards (C9, Deci 1999).
11. Practice target about 80-85% first-try success, adjustable and logged, not treated as a law (C10).
12. Keep the guided first game as the hook; treat the 10-minute figure as a product bet and instrument it (C7, D1, C11).
13. Keep a dual loop: guided play and serious solitary study of positions; do not rely on games alone (B3, B4, D2).
14. Do not promise a time to a rating; individual differences in required hours are large (B4).
15. Teach recurring patterns as named, repeatedly seen chunks in game-like contexts (B1, B2).
16. Phone is acceptable but test board size and touch targets ourselves (E1, E2).

## Open questions (weak evidence)
- Q1. Does explicit "learning how to learn" instruction transfer anywhere? Chess-transfer results are negative or weak when controlled (A1-A3); needs our own evaluation.
- Q2. No study compares ways of teaching novices chess itself (speed to first legal game, retention).
- Q3. Worked examples and expertise reversal in chess specifically: no meta-analysis found.
- Q4. Does retrieval, spacing and interleaving work as strongly for chess tactics as for words/maths/paintings? Likely, but unproven.
- Q5. Hint and feedback timing for board problems: unverified (G0).
- Q6. Streaks and badges specifically: no study found; the Deci evidence is about tangible rewards.
- Q7. Does a short time to first success raise retention in a learning app? Only industry lore found; instrument it.
- Q8. Is 85% correct right for strategy concepts and for adults vs children? Theory only.
- Q9. Phone vs desktop for chess learning: nothing found.
- Q10. Items to verify by reading: pooled effects for Adesope 2017, Rowland 2014, Kulik 1990; Gobet and Clarkson (2004), Kirschner et al. (2006) and Bjork and Bjork were not looked up.
