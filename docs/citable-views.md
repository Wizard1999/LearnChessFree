# Citable views: what great players and coaches said, for lesson authors

Written 2026-10-30 (task R3). Purpose: when a lesson says "Capablanca advised..." or "Dvoretsky taught...", the attribution must be true, specific and honestly labelled. Draw on this file; do not invent beyond it. Ideas are paraphrased in our own words; facts and ideas are free to restate, wording is not.

## How to use an entry (author rules)

1. Say the idea in your own sentence, name the person, and (for books) the title. Do not present a paraphrase in quotation marks.
2. Only the entries marked **[V-quote]** carry a verbatim quotation, each under 15 words, each checked by searching the stored book text this session. Use at most one quotation per page.
3. Never put words in a person's mouth ("Nimzowitsch would say..."). Write "Nimzowitsch argued..." only where an entry supports it.
4. Entries from old books (1800s to 1920s) are views of their time. Where an engine-age caveat exists it is given as [I]. Add it to learner text when the claim is absolute.
5. Where a lesson shows a concrete line, the board shows it; the prose carries the idea (owner's review item 1). This file holds ideas, not move strings.

## 1. Opening principles and development

- **1.1** Bring your forces into play before anything else; neglecting this lets the opponent seize a key point and finish the game quickly. Emanuel Lasker, *Common Sense in Chess*, Lecture 1 and applied in Lectures 3-6. [V-sheet] (4.2). Caveat: the sheet notes pages of Lecture 1 are missing from the OCR, so the "about six moves" figure is second-hand within the book.
- **1.2** Every opening move should add working power to some piece; knights before bishops is a default, and rook-pawn moves made out of fear of a threat that does not yet exist waste time. E. Lasker, Lectures 1 and 6. [V-sheet] (4.3).
- **1.3** The development rule has exceptions once a game has "acquired a character": a quiet pawn move that protects a key bishop can beat one more developing move. E. Lasker, Lecture 3 and the amendment at the end of Lecture 6. [V-sheet] (4.5). Good contrast-lab material: same rule, one position where it holds and one where it fails.
- **1.4** A gambit is a trade of a pawn for time and open lines, and is judged by whether the compensation survives the first accurate reply, not by slogan. E. Lasker, Lectures 1, 4, 5. [V-sheet] (4.4).
- **1.5** Develop fast, knights before bishops, do not move a piece twice before development ends unless it wins something, move pieces rather than pawns, and do not trade bishop for knight early without compensation. Capablanca, *Chess Fundamentals*, Part II opening sections (Sec. 6-7). [V-sheet] (CI-45, CI-46).
- **1.6** Development is collective: a side is developed when all its pieces are out, not when a few are, and the side ahead in development holds the initiative. Nimzowitsch, *My System*, I.i §1 and §3. [V-sheet] (idea 1). [I] The tempo-counting habit is a heuristic; do not teach "any piece moved twice is bad".
- **1.7** Warning against pawn-grabbing: wing pawns won while development is unfinished cost more than they gain. Nimzowitsch, I.i §7. [V-quote] "Never play to win a pawn while your development is yet unfinished!" The same section gives one exception, a centre pawn taken without great danger. Teach the rule and its exception together.
- **1.8** When development is stuck, exchange wholeheartedly to relieve the tension instead of patching with a protective or flight move; swapping a much-moved piece for an undeveloped one is the classic beginner error. Nimzowitsch, I.i §4-5. [V-sheet] (idea 3).
- **1.9** White's first move gives a "moral right" to attack; a lost move by White hands Black the initiative. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*, opening chapters. [V-sheet] (K8). [I] Present as a 1915 heuristic, not a law; modern theory treats Black's equalising counterplay as normal.
- **1.10** Choose simple development over a messy pawn grab, and learn principles rather than memorising lines, so irregular openings hold no fear. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*, opening chapters. [V-sheet] (K13, K14).
- **1.11** Keep the option to castle open; delay it unless the opponent's central plan is known, and let full development come first. Capablanca, Sec. 7, Ex. 18 (CI-48); Edward Lasker, Game 12 note (K32). [V-sheet]
- **1.12** First moves leave lasting marks: 1...e6 in the French blocks Black's own bishop but also blocks White's bishop diagonal toward f7, and those two facts give the French its permanent character. E. Lasker, Lecture 6. [V-sheet] (4.21). Use for "why does this opening feel the way it does" lessons.
- **1.13** An opening is judged by the concessions it forces; a natural developing move that breaks no principle is rarely bad, and a line that forces an ugly move condemns itself. Capablanca, Ex. 18, Ex. 67, Games 9 and 12. [V-sheet] (CI-50). [I] A rule of thumb that engines sometimes overturn; pair with a verified counterexample.

## 2. The centre

- **2.1** The four central squares matter because pieces there reach everything; control of at least two or three of them is needed for a violent attack to succeed. Capablanca, Sec. 7 and 31. [V-sheet] (CI-47).
- **2.2** Open with e4 or d4 because they free a bishop and the queen at once; two pawns side by side in the centre are the strongest pair; advancing either to the fifth rank is a major decision. Capablanca, Sec. 6-7, 31; Ed. Lasker opening chapters (K6). [V-sheet] (CI-38, CI-45).
- **2.3** Play e4, then d4 supported by c3, so two pawns stand abreast; do not rush them, and if one is attacked, advance rather than allow a separating exchange. Philidor, *Studies of Chess*, general maxims. [V-sheet/skim] (P1).
- **2.4** A free, mobile enemy centre pawn is the main enemy: either remove it with a prepared pawn break or restrain it until it is harmless. Nimzowitsch, I.i §6. [V-sheet] (idea 4). Link to restraint, section 7.
- **2.5** The centre can be controlled from afar by pieces; the enemy pawn centre then becomes a target rather than a strength. The hypermodern school, led by Nimzowitsch, with Réti, Tartakower, Breyer and others. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypermodernism_(chess). The same page says hypermodernism extended classical theory rather than replacing it.
- **2.6** "Surrender of the centre" is about squares, not pawns; a defender may yield the pawns and still hold the key squares. Nimzowitsch, II.i. [V-sheet] (Part II.i summary in sheet section 3). [I] Check the exact subsection before citing a section number; the sheet gives only the chapter.
- **2.7** Lure the opponent's central pawn away by a pawn exchange, but do not give up your own central pawn without a gain such as open lines or a commanding piece. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K10).
- **2.8** In open games, keep ...d5 available as Black's equalising break; do not play early moves that let White prevent it. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy* comparison tables. [V-sheet] (K11).
- **2.9** Central squares touch more of the board, so a piece there exerts more; ranks and files outrank diagonals; reaching the fifth rank raises a piece's activity. Znosko-Borovsky, *The Middle Game in Chess*, Part I (board geometry). [V-sheet] (C25).
- **2.10** Tarrasch took control of the centre, the bishop pair and space from Steinitz and made them easier to apply, but stressed piece mobility more than Steinitz did. Siegbert Tarrasch. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegbert_Tarrasch. Hypermoderns called his teaching dogmatic; the same page notes many modern masters disagree.
- **2.11** A knight advanced to a square no enemy pawn can attack (an outpost), ideally on the fifth or third rank, supported by a pawn, can outweigh a bishop. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_strategy (general statement, no single author).

## 3. Pawn structure and pawn breaks

- **3.1** Pieces can retreat, pawns cannot, so the pawn skeleton is the lasting element: arrange the pieces around it and change it only after thought. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K9).
- **3.2** Philidor's emphasis: pawns are central to the game. [V-quote] "The Pawns are the soul of Chess" (Philidor, as quoted in Young, *Chess Generalship*). The quotation is Philidor's words as printed in Young's book, not a page of Philidor's own *Studies* that I checked.
- **3.3** Keep pawns united, and when a recapture lets you choose, join the smaller group to the larger; two abreast on the fourth rank beat the same pair pushed to the sixth. Philidor, general maxims. [V-sheet/skim] (P2, P4).
- **3.4** Doubled pawns are not a defect when neighbours support them; they matter mostly when no pawn stands on an adjoining file. Philidor (P3); Edward Lasker (K26) adds that if no exchange can create a passed pawn, doubled pawns remain a handicap. [V-sheet]
- **3.5** Attack separated pawns, even just to tie down pieces; between equal groups the side that first breaks the larger group tends to win. Philidor (P5). [V-sheet/skim]
- **3.6** Place your pawns on the colour of the enemy bishop (so they are safe from it) and, if you hold a bishop, on the colour opposite your bishop so it is not hemmed in. Capablanca, Sec. 14 around Ex. 33. [V-sheet] (CI-18); Philidor makes a related point (P6).
- **3.7** Pawns that "work" (active or full of future action) separate the better position from one whose pawns only need protecting. E. Lasker, Lecture 3 and Lecture 10-12. [V-sheet] (4.7). [I] In engine play, piece activity can outweigh a damaged structure; show both.
- **3.8** A pawn chain splits the board into two fronts; attack the base, not the head; the side with the head plans a lever on the other wing. Nimzowitsch, I.ix §1-3. [V-sheet] (idea 30).
- **3.9** Against a chain, choose a war of movement (force the base pawn to capture) or a siege (fix the base and pile on); the base is mainly an endgame weakness, so in the middlegame the siege should lead to a second weakness. Nimzowitsch, I.ix §4-6. [V-sheet] (idea 31).
- **3.10** A backward pawn on an open file plus a hole in front of it gives the enemy a permanent post; Capablanca says such formations invariably lead to disaster. Capablanca, Sec. 31, Ex. 63. [V-sheet] (CI-37). [I] "Invariably" is his absolute; verify any example you show.
- **3.11** To win a backward pawn: stop it advancing, then out-count the defence along the file with rooks, queen and bishop. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K16).
- **3.12** Isolated queen's pawn: the side playing against it aims at the isolation from the opening, with knights manoeuvring and the defending bishop passive, so minor-piece trades lead to a won ending. Capablanca, Game 9. [V-sheet] (CI-44).
- **3.13** A missing pawn is a different problem in each phase: a missing g-pawn hurts king safety in the middlegame, a missing b-pawn hurts mostly in the ending, when a- and c-pawns are far from the king. Edward Lasker, Diagrams 97-98. [V-sheet] (K19).
- **3.14** Decide where the rooks will work from the pawn structure: after 1.e4 e5 the e- and d-files open by exchange; in French or Caro-Kann chains each side advances a wing pawn; in closed d-pawn games the c-file is the target. Edward Lasker. [V-sheet] (K12).
- **3.15** A real pawn majority must be able to create a passed pawn; the candidate (the pawn with no enemy pawn in front) leads and the others support, while the weaker player pushes the middle pawn first. Nimzowitsch, I.iv §1. [V-sheet] (idea 14).
- **3.16** Pawn weaknesses (isolated, doubled, backward, holes) are usually permanent, so avoid them unless something compensates, such as an attack. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_strategy.

## 4. Attack on the king and sacrifices

- **4.1** Do not attack without a tangible superiority; if you do, the reaction leaves your army disordered and the counterattack finds it. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.8).
- **4.2** The first aim of an attack is to establish strong points near the enemy camp, occupied by pieces with a large field of action. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.8, 4.9).
- **4.3** Pace should match the margin: a big superiority against real weaknesses calls for quick forcing play; a vague one calls for a moderate pace and a methodical plan. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.10).
- **4.4** To mate, you must control up to nine squares (the king's and its eight neighbours), and the checking piece must be safe. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.14). A good "count the squares, not the attackers" exercise.
- **4.5** Pick the wing by where the opponent has defects, not where your own pieces point; Lasker shows a kingside attack rejected because that wing offered no targets. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.12).
- **4.6** Once you begin a direct attack, bring in every piece (often the last piece decides) and do not break off halfway. Capablanca, Sec. 21. [V-sheet] (CI-32).
- **4.7** A threat held in hand ties down the opponent; carried out, it is gone. So execute a threat only if it gains something at once, and consider a feint on one wing before switching to the other. Capablanca, Sec. 22. [V-sheet] (CI-33).
- **4.8** Sacrifices on and around a castled king work through coordination: a sacrifice strips the cover, then queen, a lifted rook and a minor piece finish. Capablanca, Ex. 11-16. [V-sheet] (CI-27, CI-28). He also warns that pushing the knight's pawn one square after castling loosens the cover.
- **4.9** A development lead licenses a direct attack and even sacrifices to open lines; a development deficit voids it, however tempting the attack. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K7).
- **4.10** Aim attacks at objectives that cannot run away (a pinned piece, a pawn held in place); chasing a movable piece with pawns loses time. Edward Lasker (K15). [V-sheet]
- **4.11** Pawn storms need overwhelming force behind them; when kings castle on opposite wings, pawns are cheap and speed decides. Edward Lasker (K18); Philidor says the same of opposite-wing castling (P7). [V-sheet], Philidor [V-sheet/skim]
- **4.12** Every pawn move leaves something behind: before pushing a pawn near your own castled king, ask which squares it stops guarding. Edward Lasker (K17). [V-sheet]
- **4.13** The revolutionary attack: when a quiet build-up fails, give material to clear the way, for the entry square not for the material, and only after the converging attack has been tried. Nimzowitsch, I.ii §4 and I.iii §2. [V-sheet] (idea 8).
- **4.14** A sacrifice is a swap of one element for another (a gambit swaps force for time); judge it by whether the new element lasts. Znosko-Borovsky, Part I. [V-sheet] (C01, C16).
- **4.15** Eleven attack types are described, among them a centre attack on a weak point such as f7, the king left in the centre (target its sheltering pawn and stop castling), and a kingside-castled king. Znosko-Borovsky, Part III Ch. III. [V-sheet] (C33). Mine this for contrast families.
- **4.16** Count attackers needed against defenders available; if both are exactly enough, the move order decides. Znosko-Borovsky (C32). [V-sheet]
- **4.17** Pawns are the weakest piece but the most threatening, since every piece must retreat from them; storm only when the camp is already weakened or when the collapse coincides with the push. Znosko-Borovsky (C30). [V-sheet]

## 5. Defence

- **5.1** When you are sound and the opponent is only preparing, you may ignore the preparation, bring up reserves, make the smallest evasive move and counterattack. When you are genuinely compromised, find the weak points, vacate them of key pieces, and support them. E. Lasker, Lecture 9. [V-sheet] (4.15).
- **5.2** Defence is economical, not passive: do not change your own position in the threatened quarter without forcible reasons. E. Lasker, Lecture 9. [V-quote] "Abstain from changing it voluntarily, except for most forcible reasons."
- **5.3** Find the only defence by listing the natural defences and refuting all but one, and never assume a draw by perpetual check without checking the king's escape squares; Lasker analyses his own game against Steinitz where he misjudged one. E. Lasker, Lecture 9. [V-sheet] (4.16).
- **5.4** Diagnose before defending: find your chief weakness, the opponent's chief threat, and whether his edge decides in the middlegame or the ending; defend with a long plan, not one threat at a time. Znosko-Borovsky, Part III. [V-sheet] (C21).
- **5.5** Defence differs by the element lost: against a space bind, make play on the opposite wing; against a time lead, keep making small threats; against a material deficit, choose passive defence or counterattack. Znosko-Borovsky (C22). [V-sheet]
- **5.6** Counter-attack works only if your own defence is complete and the enemy camp has weak points; the worst course is the half-hearted third option, neither defending nor attacking. Znosko-Borovsky (C23). [V-sheet]
- **5.7** If you win material, expect to defend for a while, then strike with everything when his attack runs out. Capablanca, Sec. 23, Ex. 53 (Capablanca-Janowski). [V-sheet] (CI-34).
- **5.8** A defender can be an illusion: it may be capturable by a cheaper piece, pulled away by a bigger threat, or pinned; check before trusting the count. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K3).
- **5.9** Real and fictitious threats: some threats would gain nothing if carried out, yet still pay off because the opponent must respond. A quiet preparatory move can turn the opponent's real threat into a fictitious one. Znosko-Borovsky (C14, C15). [V-sheet] He cites the proverb that the threat is stronger than its execution (the proverb quoted in his Part II; not claimed as his original).
- **5.10** When defending, offer exchanges; Philidor lists this among his maxims. Philidor, general maxims. [V-sheet/skim]
- **5.11** Petrosian, called "Iron Tigran", was a defence-first player who neutralised the opponent's attacking chances before building his own, and who used the positional exchange sacrifice. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigran_Petrosian. Kramnik is quoted on that page calling him "the first defender with a capital D" (his words as reported there; do not reuse without re-reading).
- **5.12** Steinitz often accepted "unnecessarily difficult defensive positions" to demonstrate his theories, and could still build crushing attacks from them (a summary of the page's account). Wilhelm Steinitz. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Steinitz.

## 6. Positional play and plans

- **6.1** Every advantage is one of three elements: force (material), space, or time (useful moves made); a plan converts between them and the conversion moment is where games are often decided. Znosko-Borovsky, Part I and II. [V-sheet] (C01, C16). Useful as the organising frame for the "Plans" course.
- **6.2** Evaluate in steps: a formal count (material, moves made), then a qualitative audit piece by piece, then the character of the position, the one or two features that make it unlike its neighbours. Znosko-Borovsky (C02, C05, C06). [V-sheet]
- **6.3** The difference method: list the differences between the rival positions (which pawn stands elsewhere, which wing holds the king); each difference suggests a possibility. Znosko-Borovsky (C07). [V-sheet]
- **6.4** Stand better: play actively. Stand worse: defend and restore freedom. Stand equal: manoeuvre. Playing actively from a worse position is the most dangerous choice and passively from a better one the most wasteful. Znosko-Borovsky (C08). [V-sheet]
- **6.5** Revisit the valuation from scratch every so often, like a spectator, to escape the pull of earlier forced moves; he cites the proverb about the looker-on. Znosko-Borovsky, Part II Ch. I. [V-sheet] (C09).
- **6.6** A lead in time fades and must be cashed early; a lead in space does not fade and can be squeezed slowly, but watch for the opponent's liberating sacrifice. Znosko-Borovsky (C19, C20). [V-sheet]
- **6.7** Analyse by plans, not trees: list the full set of plans each side has and count the moves each needs. E. Lasker, Lectures 4 and 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.13). [I] The "no other plan" claims are strong; engines often find extras. Teach the method, not his conclusions.
- **6.8** Three kinds of move: bring new force into play, make pieces do something, or undo the opponent's work. Name the purpose of every move. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.11).
- **6.9** Keep a plan that fits your means; Capablanca criticises a plan-less side that pushed a pawn blocking its own bishop. Capablanca, Sec. 25, Ex. 55. [V-sheet] (CI-36). Edward Lasker goes further and says an unsound plan is better than none (K33), which is contested; [I] modern coaches would prefer an adaptive plan.
- **6.10** Harass the opponent: if he has a weakness, make it worse or add a second one, and keep forcing his heavy pieces to defend pawns. Capablanca, Sec. 26, Ex. 57. [V-sheet] (CI-23). Same idea as Steinitz's later reputation for accumulating small edges.
- **6.11** Steinitz's approach, as summarised by Lasker on the Wikipedia page: aim for small advantages in the opening, accumulate them, and only then look for a combination. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Steinitz.
- **6.12** Silman's imbalances: list the differences between the two sides (minor pieces, pawn structure, space, material, key files, holes, lead in development, initiative, king safety, statics vs dynamics), then build the plan around the imbalances that favour you. Jeremy Silman, *How to Reassess Your Chess*. [S] https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-evaluate-a-position and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Silman (the list varies by edition; the Wikipedia page gives seven, the chess.com article ten).
- **6.13** Silman's five-step process after a tactical check: identify the imbalances on both sides, pick the side of the board where you can gain an edge, imagine an ideal target position without calculating, test whether it is reachable, and only then calculate candidate moves toward it. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Silman.
- **6.14** Bishop for knight and the like should be decided by what the trade buys; Silman's example is a trade that removes White's poor bishop for Black's strong one. [S] https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-evaluate-a-position.
- **6.15** Allocate pieces: decide where the main blow falls, which pieces can be spared and which are the minimum for defence; keep defenders also useful elsewhere. Znosko-Borovsky (C10). [V-sheet]
- **6.16** Young's "Grand Reconnaissance": before acting, compare the two armies in six respects (time, numbers, position, organization, mobility, topography) and do it yourself; do not act on guesses. Franklin K. Young, *Chess Generalship*. [V-sheet/skim] (item 2). [I] The quantitative tables in Young are idiosyncratic; cite the habit, not the numbers.
- **6.17** Edward Lasker's counter-view: strategy sets the tasks and tactics decides whether they are possible, so strategy cannot be taught without tactics; he rejects military analogies as useless. *Chess Strategy*, Ch. III. [V-sheet] (K30). Note that Young (6.16) embraces them; a good pair for "do analogies help?".

## 7. Prophylaxis and restraint

- **7.1** The deepest positional skill is blunting the opponent's useful possibilities: outer prophylaxis stops his real freeing move, inner prophylaxis brings your pieces into contact with your own key points. Nimzowitsch, II.i §3. [V-sheet] (idea 33).
- **7.2** Two misconceptions to uproot: that every move must threaten or parry, and that position play is only slow accumulation of small gains. Quiet moves secure the position and place pieces on key points. Nimzowitsch, II.i §2. [V-sheet] (idea 32).
- **7.3** The sequence "first restrain, then blockade, and finally destroy" is how he describes his game against Johner. Nimzowitsch. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Nimzowitsch (the page's account; I did not read the game record).
- **7.4** Overprotection: defending a strongpoint more times than seems needed, even when not attacked, so the pieces gain contact with it; the page ties the idea to Nimzowitsch. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Nimzowitsch and https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_strategy; the stored book's Part II.v covers "which points, which pawns to shed" [V-sheet] (section 3 of the sheet).
- **7.5** The blockade of a passed pawn is obligatory, for three reasons: it wants to expand, the blockader sits safe in front, and the blockading square tends to become a weak square. [V-quote] "The passed pawn is a criminal, who should be kept under lock and key." Nimzowitsch, I.iv §2a. Reasons paraphrased from [V-sheet] (idea 15).
- **7.6** The best blockaders are minor pieces; the queen leaves at the first attack, the king is a poor blockader in the middlegame; if the blockader is strong, exchange it so a weaker one replaces it. Nimzowitsch, I.iv §3-4. [V-sheet] (ideas 16, 17).
- **7.7** Squeeze: take away useful piece moves until only pawn moves remain and one of them opens an entry. Edward Lasker, Games 10, 14, 18. [V-sheet] (K31). Lasker does not name it prophylaxis; [I] it is the same family.
- **7.8** Do not provoke a weakness by pawn moves of your own; a pawn move without a clear purpose takes a good square from a knight and leaves a strong point for enemy knights. E. Lasker, Lecture 10-12, Hastings game commentary. [V-sheet] (4.7).
- **7.9** Petrosian, strongly influenced by Nimzowitsch's prophylaxis, spent more effort neutralising the opponent's play than building his own and waited for an over-ambitious opponent to slip. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigran_Petrosian.
- **7.10** Dvoretsky regarded prophylaxis as a cornerstone of chess understanding and drew many examples from Karpov's games; Artur Yusupov's chess.com course "Prophylactic Thinking" contrasts prophylaxis with merely improving the position. [S-search] Lichess blog by Lizard1 and the chess.com lessons page (neither opened; the blog returned 403). Recheck before use.
- **7.11** The habit of asking what the opponent wants before choosing a plan is the practical core of prophylactic thinking, as coaches in the Dvoretsky tradition teach it. [R] not re-read. [I] The exact question wording is mine; do not attribute it as a quotation.
- **7.12** Aagaard's calculation book has a chapter on prophylaxis among its tools, alongside candidate moves, comparison and elimination. Jacob Aagaard, *Grandmaster Preparation: Calculation*. [S-search] excerpt table of contents via a search result (https://www.scribd.com/document/403099204/CALCULATION-excerpt-pdf, not opened).
- **7.13** Restraint of a doubled-pawn complex, a qualitative majority or a mobile centre pawn is a recurring plan in the book; Nimzowitsch also describes the "mysterious rook move" as a prophylactic rook shift. Nimzowitsch, II.ii. [V-sheet] (section 3 of the sheet lists the chapter; details unverified).

## 8. Piece activity and weak squares

- **8.1** Mobility is the value of a piece and the measure of the balance of forces; raw numerical equality is not balance. Edward Lasker, Ch. III. [V-quote] "mobility is the first and foremost consideration"
- **8.2** A weak point is a square only heavy pieces can attack; what is a weak point for one side is a strong point for the other, and occupying it with a long-range piece often half decides the game. E. Lasker, Lectures 7-8. [V-sheet] (4.9).
- **8.3** Capablanca's piece comparisons: bishop and knight roughly equal in general, two bishops nearly always beat two knights, rook worth a minor piece plus two pawns; the point is a set of pairwise comparisons, not a table. Capablanca, Sec. 5. [V-sheet] (CI-16).
- **8.4** The bishop usually beats the knight because it can cover both wings and can stalemate a knight; the knight wins mostly in blocked positions with pawns on one wing. Capablanca, Sec. 14-15, Ex. 34-38. [V-sheet] (CI-17).
- **8.5** An outpost: a protected piece (usually a knight) on an open file in the enemy half; its strength comes from the open file behind and the protecting pawn; remove either and it loses its point. Nimzowitsch, I.ii §6. [V-sheet] (idea 10).
- **8.6** Hole: a knight on an unassailable post, with the cramped side better off trading pieces; trade the bishop that guards holes. Capablanca, Sec. 33, Ex. 67 and Game 4. [V-sheet] (CI-41, CI-42).
- **8.7** A bishop shut behind its own pawns is effectively a piece down; a master sometimes plays only to put one enemy piece out of play. Capablanca, Sec. 24, Ex. 54. [V-sheet] (CI-35).
- **8.8** The knight controls a ring of squares and no line: weaker than the bishop in open positions, stronger in crowded ones; a piece attacking a knight cannot be attacked back by it, so a knight attack on a major piece has special force. Znosko-Borovsky (C26). [V-sheet]
- **8.9** Before opening a line, ask who benefits; closing lines is useful when the enemy is stronger or the line leads to your weak points. Znosko-Borovsky (C27). [V-sheet]
- **8.10** A badly placed piece can serve a new purpose where it stands; if it must move, move it with a threat; plans often include trading your worst piece for the opponent's best. Znosko-Borovsky (C13). [V-sheet]
- **8.11** An aggressive rook with freedom on both wings beats a passive rook tied to a pawn, and giving up the pawn for activity is often right. Nimzowitsch, I.vi §2. [V-sheet] (idea 24).
- **8.12** The superior minor piece is the first imbalance in Silman's list, and with two bishops against two knights you should try to open the position. Silman. [S-search] summary of the book's chapter headings (https://www.chess.com/clubs/forum/how-to-reassess-your-chess-third-edition appears in the results; not opened); the chess.com article above confirms the list [S].
- **8.13** Tarrasch disliked cramped positions and judged them close to lost from the start (the page paraphrases a remark that cramp has "the germ of defeat"); Soltis's description of his chess as "all about piece mobility" is the page's report. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegbert_Tarrasch (do not quote the phrases; they are the page's, not read in the original).

## 9. Endgames: king activity, opposition, rook endings

- **9.1** The endgame is where middlegame gains are cashed in; the king becomes a principal actor, so centralise it as soon as the endgame begins. Nimzowitsch, I.vi intro and §1. [V-sheet] (idea 22).
- **9.2** In the ending the same rules persist but the king changes from target to fighter: weaknesses are mainly blocked pawns that no other pawn can defend. E. Lasker, Lectures 10-12. [V-sheet] (4.17).
- **9.3** The king is a fighting piece even sometimes earlier; Capablanca's Game 12 shows a king march while queens are still on. Capablanca, Sec. 5 end, Game 12. [V-sheet] (CI-26).
- **9.4** Study endings first: weak endgame play wastes a middlegame edge, and the champions of the previous sixty years were all strong in endings (his claim). Capablanca, Ch. V intro. [V-sheet] (CI-20). Caveat: the claim of "all champions" is his, unchecked.
- **9.5** King and pawn against king: the attacker walks the king forward with the pawn and pushes the pawn only to keep it safe; if the defending king stands directly in front of the pawn, it cannot be won. Capablanca, Sec. 1-2, Ex. 4-6. [V-sheet] (CI-06, CI-07).
- **9.6** Opposition: kings facing on a line with one square between and the other side to move; Capablanca gives close and distant forms. Capablanca, Sec. 14, Ex. 30-31 (CI-13); Edward Lasker adds that the critical moment comes when the pawn reaches its sixth rank (K23). [V-sheet]
- **9.7** Counter-view: Nimzowitsch called the standard theory of the opposition unclear and offered instead the king's frontal approach, forcing the enemy king to yield by zugzwang and winning a square by a turning move. I.iv §5. [V-sheet] (idea 18). [I] Teach opposition normally; mention this as a different explanation, verified with tablebase positions before use.
- **9.8** Exhaustion: when both sides are so well placed that neither can improve, having the move is no privilege. [V-quote] "the move ceases to be a privilege" (E. Lasker, Lectures 10-12). The sheet adds corresponding squares as his tool. [V-sheet] (4.19).
- **9.9** Spare moves decide tight pawn endings: the side with a spare pawn move in hand keeps the decisive tempo, and a knight cannot lose a move while a bishop can. Edward Lasker, Master ending 1, Diagrams 73-74. [V-sheet] (K25).
- **9.10** A distant passed pawn wins because it drags the opposing king away; count the moves of each side separately in races. Edward Lasker (K24); Capablanca adds that the first queen may be able to capture the other, so choose landing squares, not just speed (Sec. 10-12, CI-11). [V-sheet]
- **9.11** The rook belongs behind a passed pawn, its own or the enemy's, and a rook should not become passive. Tarrasch's formula, as given by Nimzowitsch, I.vi §2 [V-sheet] (idea 24); [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegbert_Tarrasch.
- **9.12** Rook endings are the commonest and look easier than they are; the winning side keeps one rook free to swing, fixes enemy pawns and forces heavy pieces to defend them. Capablanca, Sec. 26-30, Ex. 56-62. [V-sheet] (CI-21, CI-22). In the same passage the maxim: [V-quote] "Keep freedom of manoeuvre while hampering your opponent."
- **9.13** Rook endings: reduce the enemy rook's scope, and a rook behind the opponent's passed pawn freezes his rook; with one pawn left the defending king on the queening square draws. Edward Lasker, Diagram 78. [V-sheet] (K28).
- **9.14** Exchange into a known win: if ahead, trade into a typical theoretical ending, avoid structures that lose endings; beginners dislike queen exchanges because they think games become dull. Edward Lasker (K21). [V-sheet]
- **9.15** Wrong-coloured rook pawn: the bishop cannot win if the defending king reaches the corner; opposite-coloured bishops are mostly drawn even two pawns up. Capablanca (CI-14); Edward Lasker (K27). [V-sheet]
- **9.16** Passed pawns need "points of vantage" analysis: the squares from which the enemy can stop the pawn; if the pawn's path is one long strong point, it ties down a piece or the king. E. Lasker, Lectures 10-12. [V-sheet] (4.18).
- **9.17** When a passed pawn should advance: only if it nears promotion against a weak blockade, wins ground for the king or another piece, or serves as a diverting sacrifice. Nimzowitsch, I.iv §7. [V-sheet] (idea 20).
- **9.18** Minimum mating force: queen or rook; two bishops; bishop and knight in the corner of the bishop's colour; two knights cannot force it. Edward Lasker (K22); Capablanca, Sec. 1-4 (CI-01..05). [V-sheet]
- **9.19** Piece values in the ending are positional, not fixed: they depend on structure, openness and king position. E. Lasker, Lectures 10-12. [V-sheet] (4.20).

## 10. Calculation and the thinking process

- **10.1** Kotov's method: before each move, identify the candidate moves and examine each in an organised analysis tree. Alexander Kotov, *Think Like a Grandmaster* (1971). [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Kotov.
- **10.2** "Kotov syndrome": thinking hard in a complicated position without finding a clear path, then running short of time and playing a weak move quickly. Kotov (1971). [S] same page. Name the failure so learners can notice it in their own games.
- **10.3** Kotov also urged examining each variation once only and not jumping between lines. [R] not re-read. [I] This is the practical form of the tree idea.
- **10.4** Before moving, list every forcing reply your opponent has (checks, captures, threats) and confirm you have a safe answer to each. Dan Heisman. [S] https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess.
- **10.5** "Hope Chess": playing a move without analysing the opponent's forcing replies and hoping a safe answer exists. Basic Hope Chess skips the check; Passive Hope Chess only visualises one's own move and scans for familiar patterns. Heisman. [S] same page.
- **10.6** Heisman's "Looking for Trouble": look for the opponent's winning or damaging moves before you move; check the opponent's last move for the threat it made and your candidate move for the chance it gives. A good exercise form is two steps, identify the threat, then find the prevention. Dan Heisman, *Looking for Trouble*. [S] https://forwardchess.com/blog/review-looking-for-trouble-recognizing-and-meeting-threats-in-chess/.
- **10.7** Aagaard: the most serious errors often arise at the very start of calculation, when a key candidate is overlooked, so finding candidates matters more than calculating long lines accurately. Jacob Aagaard, *Excelling at Chess Calculation* (2004). [S] https://www.chess.com/blog/sputnick/calculation-in-the-engine-age-a-review-of-jacob-aagaards-excelling-at-chess-calculation (a reviewer's account). The same review says other parts are less new and lean on Dvoretsky.
- **10.8** Aagaard treats candidate moves as a way to uncover new ideas, with later chapters on comparison, elimination, intermediate moves, imagination and difficult positions. *Grandmaster Preparation: Calculation*. [S-search] excerpt contents via a search result (not opened).
- **10.9** Count attackers and defenders on one square, weighting by value, and do not play the sequence out in your head piece by piece, because beginners forget which pieces they used. Edward Lasker, Ch. II. [V-sheet] (K2). Also count what can arrive, not only what is there (K4).
- **10.10** Capablanca names his own thinking failures: not seeing a threat at all (G1), seeing a combination, judging it unsound and then losing to it, and distortion by fear of a future ending. Capablanca, Part II games. [V-sheet] (CI-51). A rare honest-expert source for a "learn from errors" lesson.
- **10.11** A sure small gain is worth taking for a beginner; traps in Ex. 20-21 are the kind beginners fall into. Capablanca, Ex. 20-21. [V-sheet] (CI-49).
- **10.12** Distant threats are more dangerous than direct ones because they are hidden; a chain of direct threats can make the opponent unable to deal with the distant one. Znosko-Borovsky (C14), Tarrasch-Janowski. [V-sheet]
- **10.13** Silman puts a tactical check before the imbalance analysis, and says engine analysis shows what to play but not why. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Silman and https://www.chess.com/article/view/how-to-evaluate-a-position.
- **10.14** A lasting rule of the Kotov/Heisman school is to treat the opponent as someone who will find his best move. Heisman's article says strong players assume this. [S] https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess.

## 11. How to study and improve

- **11.1** Study master games and work the variations yourself; Capablanca's book leaves about a dozen variations to the reader on purpose. Capablanca, Sec. 3, 12, 29, 33 and passim. [V-sheet] (CI-52). Reading without trying the variation is the failure.
- **11.2** Understanding openings beats memorising; Edward Lasker thought opening analysis rated far too highly as a study method. *Chess Strategy*. [V-sheet] (K14).
- **11.3** Beginners pass three stages: chaotic pawn pushing and queen raids; seeing one-to-two-move threats; and, after years, positional play. Learning stalls at stage 2 unless one studies how to open and how to treat the opening. Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*, introduction. [V-sheet] (K1). Diagnostic framework for placement.
- **11.4** Endgame knowledge steers the middlegame: you cannot judge exchanges without knowing which endings are won. Edward Lasker (K21); Capablanca (CI-20). [V-sheet]
- **11.5** Demand proofs: Lasker invites the reader to be sceptical about claims. E. Lasker, Preface. [V-quote] "reasonably sceptical and exacting in the matter of proofs"
- **11.6** Show the inferior alternative: truth gets its strength from contrast with what only seems true, and deep variations are shown to demonstrate that the better side wins by any route, not to burden memory. E. Lasker, Lectures 2, 3, 4. [V-sheet] (4.22). This is the case for our contrast lab.
- **11.7** Botvinnik: analyse your own and others' games and publish the notes so errors get found; know a narrow set of openings deeply; study opponents and your own weaknesses honestly; train under simulated conditions with short matches; set homework (Karpov credited it). [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Botvinnik.
- **11.8** Dvoretsky had a reputation for turning hard-working 2200 players into grandmasters, and Yusupov credits his success largely to Dvoretsky's methods; the school he founded with Yusupov produced many grandmasters. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Dvoretsky. The page does not describe his methods in detail.
- **11.9** Dvoretsky's *Secrets of Chess Training* and his school's books stress analysis of your own games and sound technique. [R] not re-read; the book title and award year are [S] from the Dvoretsky page, the emphasis is from memory.
- **11.10** The Steps Method (Rob Brunia, an educator, and IM Cor van Wijgerden) dates from 1987: six steps, each with a trainer's manual and a student workbook; it is the official method of the Royal Dutch Chess Federation. [S-search] summaries in search results (https://lichess.org/@/HanSchut/blog/the-steps-method-for-learning-and-teaching-chess/L74I4Ihq returned 403, so unopened); partly confirmed [S] at https://www.chessdom.com/steps-method-for-quality-chess-action-in-chessity/, which says the six-step system has been used since 1987 across the Netherlands, Belgium, Germany, Austria and Switzerland, and that its puzzles are calibrated to the step levels.
- **11.11** The Steps Method's central teaching idea is heavy repetition of the same themes in workbooks at one level before moving on, with a diploma for each step. [R] not re-read; neither page I opened confirmed repetition or diplomas. Do not state it as fact in learner text without a re-read.
- **11.12** Aagaard stresses confidence and motivation in training, and warns that very hard exercises undermine them. [S] https://www.chess.com/blog/sputnick/calculation-in-the-engine-age-a-review-of-jacob-aagaards-excelling-at-chess-calculation (reviewer's account).
- **11.13** Heisman: analysing candidate moves this way will not make you strong by itself, but relying on Hope Chess will keep you from improving. [S] https://www.chess.com/article/view/passive-vs-basic-hope-chess.
- **11.14** [H] Learners would retain more from a Steps-style pattern: a short theory page, a worksheet of ten positions in one theme, then a mixed review the next day. Test against our spaced review data when Andrew's baseline exists.

## 12. Psychology and practical play

- **12.1** Chess is a fight in which intellect decides; economy of force and time are the general's concerns. E. Lasker, Preface and Lecture 1. [V-sheet] (4.1).
- **12.2** The danger of a safe position: a player ahead stops thinking and gives the win away; Capablanca's Havana example shows three consecutive inaccuracies from a pawn-up side. Capablanca, Sec. 27-28, Ex. 58. [V-sheet] (CI-24).
- **12.3** The side ahead in material may defend more freely and take small risks; Znosko-Borovsky says this runs against the folk view that the leader must be more careful. Znosko-Borovsky (C18). [V-sheet]
- **12.4** Hide your plan with innocuous moves, or choose forcing moves so the distant aim goes unnoticed; the price is the time that slow moves give. Znosko-Borovsky, Showalter-Lasker. [V-sheet] (C12).
- **12.5** Fake gains: a rook-pawn run that develops nothing is false time; a wing advance while the game is decided elsewhere is false space; a pawn won just before being mated is false force. Znosko-Borovsky (C04). [V-sheet]
- **12.6** Do not stop thinking at the first good-looking move: Znosko-Borovsky's Black led in force, space and time and still lost quickly against Morphy. Znosko-Borovsky (C04, McConnell-Morphy). [V-sheet]
- **12.7** Strong players keep the initiative until they can trade it for something better, since only the initiative wins games. Capablanca, Sec. 20. [V-sheet] (CI-31).
- **12.8** The urge to be always doing something is an amateur error. Nimzowitsch, II.i §2, with the Riga 1910 game against an amateur. [V-sheet] (idea 32). [I] A good refutation of "attack means threatening every move".
- **12.9** In equal positions the main mistakes are strategic: a wrong valuation then a wrong plan pursued stubbornly, or constantly changing plans. Znosko-Borovsky (C24). [V-sheet]
- **12.10** Petrosian attacked only when his position felt fully secure and waited for an over-ambitious opponent to slip, giving him the nickname "Iron Tigran". [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tigran_Petrosian.
- **12.11** Self-honesty and preparation: Botvinnik held training matches in noisy, smoky rooms if he expected such conditions. [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Botvinnik.
- **12.12** Time trouble breeds blunders; Kotov's syndrome is the textbook example. Alexander Kotov (1971). [S] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Kotov.
- **12.13** [H] Naming a failure ("Hope Chess", "Kotov syndrome", "false time") and asking learners to tag their own mistakes with these names may speed self-diagnosis. Hypothesis for the Games review page; not tested.
