# Learner voice: what real beginners and returning adults say (8 Nov 2026)

Purpose: the benchmark we must beat, seen from the learner's side. Companion to `docs/research-competition-wide.md`, `docs/competition-chesscom.md`, `docs/misconceptions-by-level.md`.

## Method and evidence labels
- **[S]** secondary: the page was opened with the fetch tool and its content summarised by a small model. I did not read these pages line by line myself, so every point is a summary of what the page says, not a quotation check.
- **[S-snip]** a search-result snippet or search summary only; the page was not opened (or the open failed). Treat as a lead, not evidence.
- **[I]** my inference. **[H]** hypothesis for us to test.
- Reddit was not used (blocked in our tools; no workaround tried). Where a search returned a Reddit mirror I ignored it.
- Failed opens (HTTP 403): two Lichess blog posts (adult-beginner diary, "psychological struggle"), so those are [S-snip] only. `nextlevelchess` redirected and was not re-fetched. App-store review text was not reachable; store pages and aggregator "review analyses" (unstar, marlvel, stork, justuseapp) are AI-written or aggregated and are weighted low.
- Paraphrase throughout. Handles appear only where a forum post is the evidence. Counts below are mine from what I read; they are not a statistical sample. Most sources are forum threads, which over-represent people who write forum posts.
- Not covered well: Chessly (no real hits for an app by that name), Lichess Learn complaints (almost no negative threads found, which may itself mean the Learn page is rarely discussed rather than loved), YouTube comments (none readable via fetch), books.

## 3. When learners say they got hooked
Honest finding: few sources state a timing in minutes. What exists:
- **Beating someone they know** for the first time (a parent or a friend in a garage) pushed several people to look for chess online [S-snip] search results for chess.com blogs and a chess-start thread. Timing: the first win, not the first lesson.
- **Losing to colleagues, then logging in and playing rapid games** and getting hooked quickly [S-snip] Lichess adult-improver blog (page 403 on open).
- **A cultural spark, then the first weeks of online play**: several adult beginners started after *The Queen's Gambit* or a streamer video, using the free tier, and ramped up study after early progress. One reported a quick climb in the first months, though another member doubted a specific figure [S] src 14.
- **Early visible rating gain** from learning a few basic openings and not hanging pieces, then reviewing own losses (about 500 to 800 in the HN example; 800 to 1100 in six months in a diary) [S] src 10; [S-snip] diary.
- **Short stress-free games** (one 5-minute game a day as a break from work) keep people playing without study [S] src 10.
- **Enjoyment as the engine**: "you won't work hard unless you're enjoying yourself" type statements [S] src 12, 13.
- [I] Pattern: the hook is a first win against a real opponent plus a visible number or milestone. No source reports being hooked by a lesson. This supports the project hook (guided real game in about 10 minutes) but is not proof; the sources skew to people who already stayed.

## 4. Rules and notation confusions
- **Stalemate** (the most written-about): beginners corner the king without check and draw games they thought they had won; they know each piece's move but not how to finish [S] src 7. One beginner mistook a stalemate for threefold repetition because the opponent's king only had one shuffle [S] src 8. Helpful fixes people gave: "the king can never move into check", leave the opponent a pawn or a spare move, before each move check that the opponent has a legal move, keep queens and rooks a knight's distance away [S] src 7, 8.
- **En passant**: beginners treat it as a general diagonal capture and think it applies to pawns that have not moved; they think the game has a bug [S] src 9; [S-snip] Lichess "not a bug" thread. The explanation that worked: pawns always capture diagonally; en passant is the one special case that only exists on the move right after a two-square advance [S] src 9.
- **Castling**: forgetting the king may not cross an attacked square, wrongly believing an attacked rook blocks castling, and wrongly believing a past check removes the right [S-snip] chess.com/ChessKid guides and a forum thread.
- **Check / "move into check"** confusion appears inside the stalemate threads [S] src 7.
- **Notation**: one adult says he is still getting a handle on it after months [S] src 15; forum snippets say beginners forget capital letters for pieces, find Black's view awkward, and many skip notating [S-snip]. One poster said they learned algebraic in under an hour after weeks of the old style [S-snip]. [I] Notation is a reading skill for later; nobody reported it as the reason they quit.
- **Openings**: chosen because they "sounded cool"; beginners do not know which to study [S] src 14, 15.

## 5. Why adults plateau around 600-1000 and what they say fixed it
Causes named:
- **Plateau of pain** where study seems not to raise the rating, and many quit; the author reframes it as consolidation [S] src 11 (the claim that "most quit" is the author's, unsourced).
- **Hanging pieces and missing one- or two-move tactics** are the main loss cause; players rarely check the opponent's threats before moving [S] src 13, 11.
- **Speed**: 10-minute and faster games leave no time to calculate; one poster noted about 30% of the thread starter's losses were on time [S] src 13. HN commenters advise avoiding blitz and bullet until about 1500 [S] src 10.
- **Playing mostly bots** or lots of games without reviewing [S] src 13.
- **Tilt and rating focus**: chasing lost points with more fast games [S] src 11, 10.
- **Quick-fix openings and traps** that stop working [S] src 11.
- **Study turning into memorisation** and losing joy [S] src 10.
- **Puzzles detached from real games** (puzzle positions announce that a tactic exists) [S-snip] search summary only.
What they say fixed it:
- Tactics practice and mating patterns (a few hundred points) [S] src 10, 12.
- Reviewing own games, wins as well as losses, first without the engine [S] src 10, 11, 13.
- A pre-move checklist: opponent's threats, then own captures, checks, threats [S] src 13.
- Slower time controls [S] src 10, 13.
- Simple opening principles and a small repertoire [S] src 13, 10.
- Basic endgames and general strategy, not obscure theory [S] src 12.
- A coach or stronger friend to see blind spots [S] src 11, 13.
- Playing unrated or just many games, treating losses as lessons, stopping after a losing run [S] src 10, 14.
- Progress comes as plateau then jump, and improvement lags effort by months [S] src 12.

## 6. Paywalls, lives, limits, ads, streaks and nags
What feels unfair:
- **Capping basic learning** (lessons per week, daily puzzles) while the sales pitch targets newcomers; proposals: a daily free lesson like the daily puzzle, or at least five a week [S] src 2.
- **Shrinking free allowances without notice** [S] src 3.
- **Paying and still being sold to**: ads/promos inside paid tiers and a mascot prompting upgrade on nearly every review move [S] src 4; promo replacing a Game Review button [S-snip].
- **Interstitial ads between games** reported by free users [S-snip] review analysis (third-party).
- **Annual commitments after a bad first year** (a beginner reconsidering a year-long subscription) [S] src 1.
- **Cancellation and billing difficulty** (ChessKid, from UK Trustpilot snippet) [S-snip].
- **Constant reward noise**: streaks, badges and rubies on Chessable felt like social media to one reviewer, less useful than the actual scoring [S] src 21.
- A fairer pattern people suggested: a weekly quota with a plain "limit reached" message rather than nagging [S] src 4.
- Streaks: no source I read complained that a streak itself was unfair; one HN-adjacent commenter recommended Chessable for its streak [S] src 20 (one data point). I found no evidence on lives in chess apps. Duolingo's chess review did not discuss streaks, lives or ads [S] src 16. These stay open questions.

## 7. Accessibility and mobile
- Screen-reader users say most chess apps are unusable and boards are often one unlabelled image; Lichess has a blind mode with double-tap moves and a visual display for low vision; chess.com improved but a user said it still gave little value to screen-reader users [S-snip] TalkChess, Lichess changelog, chess.com forum.
- Mobile: drag-to-move problems, intrusive pop-ups on mobile web, premove limits, no in-game chat on mobile, and a missing retry control on iPad [S] src 5, 23.
- Chessable: no phone app at the time of one review (may be stale), though studying on a phone was a main praise [S] src 21.
- ChessKid: Android bugs [S-snip].
- Colour-blind, tiny-piece and mis-tap complaints: **not found**. A targeted search returned only screen-reader material.
- Limited internet time was a barrier for one learner [S] src 15.

## 8. Design consequences for Scholarmate
Each point is [I] or [H] derived from the above; none has been tested with players.
1. **Show the path.** A visible "you are here, next is this" map is the most repeated unmet want (complaints 1-3). Our radial Map and Today card already do this; make sure the first screen shows one next step and the whole route to "first full game", not a library.
2. **Keep levels separate and labelled.** Never show a beginner advanced items; gate with prerequisites and say why a skill is locked.
3. **Track concepts, not only a number.** Show mastered, shaky and untried skills, and recurring tactical misses from the learner's own games (complaint 3, 4).
4. **Teach the bridge to real games.** Every lesson ends with "look for this in your next game"; add lessons on how to review a game, how to use puzzles, and a pre-move checklist (opponent threats, then my checks, captures, threats). This is the most cited plateau fix.
5. **Hook with a first win.** The sources point to a first win against a real opponent, not a lesson, as the sticking moment. Keep the guided first game and make sure the opponent can be beaten but not for free; play a bot that makes human-looking errors, not random giveaways.
6. **Bots with a ladder that behaves.** Weakest bot should still develop and mate; mid bots should not alternate giveaways with traps. Show what the bot is "like" (careless, greedy, tricky). Verify bot strength against a reference.
7. **Rating humility.** Do not start a novice at an adult-sounding 1500; use ranges, unrated early games, or no number until placement. Keep ratings off concepts (rule 6).
8. **Rule traps as first-class lessons, taught when the game needs them:** stalemate (including "check the opponent has a legal move" and "leave a spare move"), en passant (always a special capture, only on the very next move), castling (cannot cross an attacked square; an attacked rook is fine; a past check is fine), "king may never move into check". Use the learner's own near-miss ("you almost drew a won game") as the trigger. Add detectors for stalemate blunders in games review.
9. **Defer notation.** Teach reading algebraic notation later and in small steps, with a click-to-see board; never make it a gate to playing.
10. **Explain why, in words.** Automated review that only says "eval changed" is called generic. Our reviewed-text approach (hard rule 7) should say why a move is a mistake in one sentence, from detectors, and ask the learner to find it first before showing the engine line (analyse-without-engine-first).
11. **Puzzles at the learner's level, with a skip and a retry.** Easier-than-your-rating mode by default for novices; one-tap retry on missed items; avoid the "puzzle announces itself" effect by mixing in positions from the learner's own games.
12. **Slow down, on purpose.** Default play and review prompts toward longer time controls for beginners; nudge after a losing run ("take a break or play unrated") instead of rewarding rapid replays.
13. **Free means free, and say so.** No daily caps, no lives, no upsell inside learning, no interstitials. Position against the specific grievances (capped lessons, shrinking free tiers, nags in paid tiers). If we ever add a supporter option, never place it in the learning flow.
14. **Rewards: light.** Reward noise annoyed one reviewer, and I found no evidence either way on streaks for chess. Test a quiet streak (opt-in, never lost with a loud message) before building anything heavier. [H]
15. **Short sessions on a phone.** Praise for 10-15 minute sessions and complaints about long study sessions point to a one-card "today" flow that works offline and resumes. Make drag-and-tap both work, with large hit targets and an undo for mis-taps.
16. **Accessibility from the start.** Labelled squares and moves for screen readers (a text move list and square-by-square readout), a colour-blind-safe board theme, selectable piece sets, large pieces, and reduced motion. Few chess apps do this, so it is a distinguishing feature, not a nicety. Test with a real screen reader before claiming support (rule 1, evidence labels).
17. **Keep the human voice in practice.** Learners recommend YouTube and annotated games because they hear reasoning. Our lessons should show a thought process ("what I notice first"), not just the answer, and give links to free human-made material only where rights allow.
18. **Do not oversell.** Several complaints are about marketing mismatch ("not designed for beginners" stated honestly was a request). State plainly who Scholarmate is for and what level it takes you to.
19. **Open questions to measure after release (feedback-tagged):** where learners stop in the first hour; whether a first win is the retention driver in our data; whether streaks help or annoy; whether stalemate lessons reduce stalemate blunders; how many learners use the review step. These need real data and sit under "After release".
