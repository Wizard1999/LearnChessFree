# Research: curriculum orders used by coaches, books and courses (8 Nov 2026)

Purpose: collect the *order* in which established sources teach topics, so we can compare when each topic is introduced and how long it takes, against our path (`content/path.json`, stages S0 to S7; titles in `docs/curriculum-map-v1.md`). Facts only: chapter titles, counts, order. No book text was downloaded or copied; contents lists come from retailer and publisher listings (rule 4). Wording is original.

Evidence labels: **[V]** I read the named page (WebFetch, 8 Nov 2026) and the item is stated there. **[S]** secondary (a page that reports on the source). **[S-snip]** search-result snippet only, page not opened. **[R]** recalled, not checked. **[I]** my inference. **[H]** hypothesis.

Caveat on method: WebFetch summarises pages with a small model, so lists marked [V] were read through that summariser, not by eye. Page numbers are given only where a listing gave them; I use them as a rough size signal, not as a measure of study time.

## 1. Headline findings (read this first)

1. **No source in this set puts tactics before the learner has seen a whole game or at least all the piece moves, but most put the first tactic or mate pattern very early.** Yusupov's first fundamentals book opens with two chapters on mating motifs before anything else [V]. Polgar's 5334 is built from mate-in-one, then mate-in-two problems as its bulk [S-snip]. Chess Steps puts "attack techniques and winning material" in Step 2 [S, from `docs/competition-chess-steps.md`].
2. **Checkmate patterns are the most common "first topic after the moves".** Yusupov (chapters 1 and 2), Chess.com's Dane guide (endgame mates in "Winning the Game", item 9 of 20, before tactics lessons), Silman's endgame course (part 1 is basic mates), CSC (simple checkmates in term 2 of 3) all do this.
3. **Openings split into two camps.** *Principles* come early (Yusupov Build Up 1 chapter 3 of 24; Dane item 14 of 20, "do not memorise openings"). *Repertoire and named openings* come in the second book or later (Build Up 2 chapters 15 and 17 of 24; Seirawan Openings is a separate volume). One exception is the Rozman beginner course, which puts openings for White and for Black right after "basics" and "piece interactions", before tactics [S-snip].
4. **Endgames enter in two waves.** Basic mates and the simplest pawn endings are early (Yusupov Build Up 1 chapter 4, "simple pawn endings", and chapter 10, "the opposition"; Silman parts 1 and 2). Rook-ending theory (Lucena and Philidor) is placed at the 1400 to 1599 band by Silman [S], and "elementary endgames" sits at chapter 16 of 24 in Boost Your Chess 1 [V].
5. **Positional play is later than tactics in every graded course read**, but it is not absent early: Yusupov Build Up 1 has "centralizing the pieces" (8), "open files and outposts" (14), "weak points" (20). Chess Steps starts positional work mainly at Step 4 [S]. Silman's *Reassess* is aimed at roughly 1400 to 2100 [S].
6. **Almost nobody states hours.** Only CSC (about 30 weekly lessons, one page says 45 to 60 minutes each [S-snip], so roughly 22 to 30 hours) and the Rozman course (about 9 hours of video [S-snip]) give a figure I could find. Dane's guide gives no hour target, only a 40 minute example session [V]. Chess Steps hours: not found. Yusupov hours: not stated; each chapter has a theme, worked examples and a set of test positions [S-snip].
7. **Stated principle most shared across sources:** play and review your own games alongside the lessons (Dane: playing games is "the most important ingredient" [V]; CSC: about half of each lesson is mini-games or full games [V]). Our path puts the first guided game in the first 10 minutes, which is earlier than any source here; the nearest is Dane, whose first game (item 3) follows two interactive courses.

### 2.1 Yusupov, Build Up Your Chess 1: The Fundamentals (Quality Chess, 2008; 261 pages)

- **Audience:** "club players" who need the basics; the series runs Fundamentals, Beyond the Basics, Mastery [S: shop page text]. A publisher-style blurb says each chapter introduces a theme, then examples of rising difficulty, then exercises with full solutions [S-snip, schachversand listing]. - **Endgames:** chapter 4 (simple pawn endings), 10 (opposition), 16 (queen against pawn), 17 (stalemate), 22 (wrong bishop). **Openings:** principles chapter 3; gambits chapter 24.
- **Hours:** not stated.

### 2.5 Silman, Silman's Complete Endgame Course (2007)
Nine parts, each tied to a rating class [S: schachversand listing summary, page returned a German review; https://www.schachversand.de/silman-s-complete-endgame-course.html]:
1 Unrated to 999: overkill mates ("staircase", "box") · 2 Class E, 1000 to 1199: basic mates (queen vs bishop or knight), first stalemate problems, king activity, opposition, rook-pawn endings · 3 Class D, 1200 to 1399: deeper opposition and pawn endings · 4 Class C, 1400 to 1599: further pawn endings, minor-piece endings, rook endings with Lucena and Philidor, queen vs pawn on the 6th or 7th · 5 Class B, 1600 to 1799: triangulation, outflanking, deeper rook and minor-piece endings · 6 Class A, 1800 to 1999 · 7 Experts, 2000 to 2199: "flowchart" technique, critical Philidor positions · 8 Masters, 2200 to 2399 · 9 "Pure Pleasure": tactics in endgames, dominated minor pieces, five greatest endgame players.
- The concept names (opposition, Lucena, Philidor, triangulation, square of the pawn, principle of two weaknesses) are confirmed on Silman's page [V: https://www.jeremysilman.com/?p=2356], but that page does not assign them to classes.

### 2.6 Silman, How to Reassess Your Chess (4th ed.)
Parts [V: https://www.schachversand.de/how-to-reassess-your-chess.html]: 1 The Concept of Imbalances · 2 Minor Pieces · 3 Rooks · 4 Psychological Meanderings · 5 Target Consciousness · 6 Statics vs. Dynamics · 7 Space · 8 Passed Pawns · 9 Other Imbalances; answers to tests; appendix of articles.

### 2.7 Dvoretsky and Yusupov, Positional Play (Batsford, 1996; 272 pages)
Contents [V: https://www.schachversand.de/positional-play-8747.html]: Part 1 Methods of improving positional play (1 improving one's positional skill, 2 manoeuvring, 3 competition in solving positional exercises) · Part 2 Methods of seeking positional solutions (4 prophylactic thinking, 5 the key to a position, 6 planning, 7 sensing the tempo, 8 positional transformations) · Part 3 Typical positions (9 opposite-coloured bishops in the middlegame, 10 "You cannot manage without combinations!", 11 modern treatment of the Dutch Defence) · Part 4 Complicated strategy in practical play (12 grandmaster strategy, 13 whose strategy will triumph) · Part 5 pupils' work.
- **Order logic:** training method first (how to practise positional thinking), then techniques (prophylaxis, key move, planning, tempo, transformation), then typical positions, then practice. Audience: strong club players and up; no beginner material. Tactics enter only as chapter 10, as a reminder that positional play needs combinations.

### 2.8 Dvoretsky and Yusupov, Attack and Defence (Batsford, 1998; 288 pages)
Contents [V: https://www.schachversand.de/attack-and-defence.html]: Part 1 Calculating variations (analysis technique and decision-making; navigating complex lines; visualizing positions during analysis) · Part 2 Intuitive decisions (how intuition develops; improvisational play) · Part 3 Practical elements (practical winning chances; when complicating pays; reflections on a book) · Part 4 Attack (missed brilliancies; a disputed position; attacks after castling on opposite wings) · Part 5 Defence (training session on difficult choices; masterful defence; errors and their causes) · Part 6 Further examples (a full game analysis; pupils' achievements).
- **Order logic:** calculation and visualisation first, then intuition, then practical decision-making, then attack and defence. Our map puts calculation at tier 3 to 5 (`S4`), attack and defence at tiers 3 to 6; the same relative order.

### 2.9 Heisman, Elements of Positional Evaluation (Russell, 4th ed. 2023; 216 pages)
Chapters [V: https://www.schachversand.de/elements-of-positional-evaluation.html]: 1 The Background of Positional Knowledge · 2 The Elements (mobility, flexibility, vulnerability, centre control, piece coordination, time, speed [S]) · 3 Pseudo-Elements (material, space, king safety, development [S]) · 4 The Pieces in Relation to the Elements · 5 Static Features and the Elements · 6 Miscellaneous Applications · 7 Epilogue; appendix of illustrative games.
- **Audience:** improving amateurs; it defines a small set of "real" elements and treats space, material, king safety and development as derived [S]. A theory book, not a graded course.
- **Heisman's Novice Nook (column archive):** the archive is now mostly behind a subscription and Dan removed most links; no ordered topic list available [V: https://www.danheisman.com/articles-by-subject.html]. His subject index lists: thought process/analysis; time management; general improvement; shorter lesson/tip material; skills and psychology; endgame; openings; tactics; strategy/positional play; winning won games; game analysis; rules; books and media; quizzes [V]. Note that "Thought Process" is first in his index. Article titles on his best-of page include "The Three 'Times' for Checks, Captures, and Threats" and "The Most Important Tactic" [V: https://www.chess.com/blog/danheisman/quick-guide-to-my-best-articles]. No sequence, so no order data.

### 2.10 Capablanca, Chess Fundamentals (1921; public domain)
Chapter order [V: Project Gutenberg HTML, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/33870/33870-h/33870-h.htm; the model read the contents list and preface]:
Part I: I First Principles: Endings, Middle-game and Openings · II Further Principles in End-game Play · III Planning a Win in Middle-game Play · IV General Theory · V End-game Strategy · VI Further Openings and Middle-games. Part II: 14 illustrative games.
- **Order logic:** endings first, then middlegame, then openings. This is the clearest historical statement that **endgame knowledge precedes opening knowledge**. The preface argues strategic fundamentals do not date, unlike tactics and fashions [V]. I did not read the chapter bodies, so what Capablanca covers *inside* chapter I (which endings, how many positions) is **not read**.

### 2.11 Lasker, Common Sense in Chess (1895 lectures)
Twelve lectures, as printed on the Internet Archive text [V partial: https://archive.org/stream/commonsenseinche00laskrich/commonsenseinche00laskrich_djvu.txt; only the first ~140k characters were summarised; the summariser did not find a contents page, so headings are inferred from running text]:
I opening principles (quick development) · II to VI openings: Ruy Lopez defences, Evans Gambit, King's Bishop's Gambit, French Defence · VII and VIII principles of attack · IX principles of defence · X to XII the endgame (passed pawns, king as an active piece, changing piece values) and the "principle of exhaustion".
- **Order logic:** opening principles, then concrete openings, then attack, then defence, then endgame last. The opposite of Capablanca on endgames. For us: shows a 19th-century order with openings first and endgames last. Treat the lecture-by-lecture mapping as [I]; the lecture numbering was partly confused in the summary.

### 2.12 Polgar, Chess: 5334 Problems, Combinations and Games
Retailer and library listings give counts: 306 mates in one, 3,412 mates in two, 744 mates in three, 600 miniature games, 144 simple endgames [S-snip: AbeBooks-style bookseller text]; a publisher excerpt shows the last parts as simple endgames (problems 5063 to 5206) and the Polgar sisters' tournament combinations (5207 to 5334) [S-snip]. ### 2.15 Chess in Schools and Communities (CSC), UK
Curriculum page [V: https://www.chessinschools.co.uk/resources]: about 30 weeks; assumes no prior chess; three terms: **Term 1** basic rules; **Term 2** what makes a good move and simple checkmates; **Term 3** basic tactics (forks, pins, skewers) and checkmating techniques. Pieces are introduced one per week through mini-games: pawn ("capture the flag", eight pawns a side), then rook, bishop, queen, knight, then king, check and checkmate. Each lesson: recap, new material, workbook exercises, then roughly half the lesson playing mini-games or full games with a tutor.
- **Hours:** 30 lessons; another CSC page says enrichment lessons are 45 to 60 minutes [S-snip], so about 22 to 30 hours [I].
- **When tactics start:** term 3, the last third (week 21 or later) [I from the term structure]. **First full game:** mini-games from week 1; full game within the first term [I: "half the lesson playing mini-games or full games"]. Opening and endgame topics are not listed on that page.
- **Principle:** learn the pieces through play, one at a time, with maths content built in.

### 2.16 Chess.com, "Coach Dane" (NM Dane Mattson), Starting Out Study Guide (0 to 600)
Ordered goals [V: https://www.chess.com/article/view/coach-danes-beginner-improvement-guide]: (Beginner basics) 1 course "How to Move the Pieces", 2 "Playing the Game" (check, en passant, castling early) · (Play) 3 first game against the Beginner bot, 4 beat it three times, 5 beat every Beginner bot, 6 a Daily Game, 7 a 15+10 or 10 minute game, 8 weekly routine · (Learning) 9 "Winning the Game" (endgame mates: two rooks, queen, rook vs king), 10 stretch "Understanding Endgames", 11 "Capturing the Pieces", 12 a puzzle each study day, 13 stretch "Winning with Tactics" · (Strategy) 14 "Opening Principles", 15 "Finding Checkmate", 16 "The Four Move Checkmate", 17 "Make the Most of Your Pieces" · (Openings) 18 do not memorise, 19 beat-your-first-bot article, 20 "Intro to Book Openings".
- **Where things sit:** first game at item 3; basic mates at item 9 (**before** the tactics course, which is optional at 13); opening principles at 14; piece activity ("Make the Most of Your Pieces") at 17 of 20, the last strategy item; book openings last.
- **Hours:** none stated; the only example is a 40 minute session; alternating "Game Day" and "Learn and Solve" days [V].
- **Principle (his words, paraphrased):** active learning, play, solve, review; playing games is the most important ingredient; do not follow advice word for word; improvement at 0 to 600 comes from keeping pieces safe, taking free material and finishing with mate [V].
- Chess.com's wider study-plan directory is banded: below 600 (Mattson), 600 to 1000 (Ramirez), 1000 to 1400 (Belenkaya), 1400 to 1800 (Kabadayi), over 1800 (Neiksans), plus a 1-1-1 plan for busy players [V: https://www.chess.com/article/view/study-plan-directory]. The contents of the bands other than the first were not opened.

## 4. Where our path differs (S0 to S7 in `content/path.json`)

Our stages [V: read from `content/path.json`]: S0 Moves and the board (8 lessons) · S1 Legal play (7) · S2 First complete games (21) · S3 First wins: mate in one, simple forks and pins, K+Q vs K (46) · S4 Becoming independent: test move against reply, rook mate, explain why (49) · S5 Developing strength: tactics, plans, pawn structure unlabelled (161) · S6 Advanced craft: longer calculation, choosing plans, converting (179) · S7 Specialization (37). I compared at the level of stage titles and milestones; I did not re-read each lesson, so lesson-level position is not claimed.

1. **First game timing: we are earlier than every source.** Ours is in the first minutes; Dane puts it at item 3 after two interactive courses; CSC at the first term. [V/S]. This is by design (the hook, CLAUDE.md). No source argues for later.
2. **Mating patterns: broadly in line.** Sources put mating motifs in the first third (Yusupov, Dane, Silman part 1, CSC term 2-3); ours is S3. Our S3 is also where K+Q vs K lands, matching Silman part 1 and Dane item 9. Rook mate in S4 matches Dane (rook vs king in item 9, together with the queen mate, so possibly one step earlier than ours) [I].
3. **Fork and pin in S3: earlier than Yusupov (ch.11-12 of 24), in line with Seirawan Tactics (ch.2-3), Steps 2, CSC term 3 and ChessKid.** The previous finding that our first fork was about 25 hours in (`docs/chess-steps-comparison.md`, D57) is still the main gap against Steps; this review adds no new evidence against it.
4. **Discovered attack, double check, back-rank mate:** Yusupov puts them in the first third; our map files these in tiers 1 to 3 (`TAC.DISCOVER`, `TAC.DOUBLE-CHECK`, `TAC.BACKRANK`). If our path puts them in S5 or later, they are later than Yusupov [H: needs a lesson-level check].
5. **Simple pawn endings and opposition: Yusupov (ch.4, 10) and Silman (1000 to 1399) place them early.** Our curriculum map has endgames as a spine "from Tier 1" with simple pawn endings at tier 3 (`docs/curriculum-map-v1.md` strand S7 summary: "simple pawn endings" at tier 3) [V: from the map's tier gloss]. This is **later than both sources** and is a candidate to pull forward.
6. **Rook endings (Lucena, Philidor):** Silman puts them at 1400 to 1599; our map's practical rook endings are tier 4 [V: tier gloss]. Aligned, since tier 4 is a similar level [I].
7. **Positional play: our S5 is "developing strength" and includes plans and structure.** Yusupov BU1 has a centralising chapter at 8 of 24 and open files/outposts at 14; Boost 1 has the 7th rank at 5 of 24 and outposts at 14. We put `POS.ACTIVITY`, `OPENFILE`, `SEVENTH` at tier 2 (`curriculum-map-v1.md` S5 line) [V], which is earlier than Steps (Step 4) and similar to Yusupov. This matches the earlier "reject the lateness" decision.
8. **Calculation and the thinking loop:** Yusupov holds candidate moves to the second book and Steps puts "thinking ahead" workbooks at Steps 2 and 3 (about 1400 to 1600). Our `LOOP.V1` safety scan at tier 1 and calculation at tier 3 are not contradicted. A "safety check before each move" appears explicitly only in Dane (as the 0 to 600 principle) and Heisman (the three times for checks, captures and threats), not in the graded books; so our early loop is an addition, not a catch-up [I].
9. **Openings: our "simple opening habits" in S2 corresponds to the principles chapters (Yusupov ch.3 of 24, Dane 14 of 20, Seirawan ch.2 of 9, Lasker I).** Named openings in our map are strand S8 (not an early stage). That is later than the Rozman beginner order but equal to most others. Capablanca's argument that endgames come before openings is consistent with the curriculum map's endgame spine and not with Lasker's.
10. **Hours:** only CSC (about 22 to 30 hours for the beginner year [I]) and Rozman (about 9 hours of video, plus drills [S-snip]) give a number. We have no benchmark for hours of the other sources. `tools/check_path_time.py` estimates ours; a direct comparison would need Steps hours, which I could not find.
11. **Rating labels:** Silman and Chess.com attach rating bands to material; those are expert guesses (G0). Our rule 6 forbids attaching a rating to a concept, so these should remain a *comparison column only*.
