# Comparative analysis: Scholarmate against the field (12 Nov 2026)

Andrew asked for an unbiased comparison of what we have, what we plan, what chess.com and Lichess offer for learning, and what other beginner curricula and books offer, with no effectiveness data to settle it, and for an honest answer to: *how far are we from a better and all-encompassing experience, one a beginner would choose over any other source?*

## 0. How to read this (method, bias and limits)

- **Labels:** [V] verified here from files, our own measurements or pages read in the research files; [S] secondary; [R] recalled and not rechecked in this session; [I] inference; [H] hypothesis. Rule 3 applies: a competitor claim that is [R] or [S] is marked, and nothing below is quoted from a person.
- **Simulated board.** The "board of elite educators, players and coaches" in section 3 is a *simulation* (rule 5): seven viewpoints I wrote to stress-test the comparison. They are not real people, make no endorsements and carry no quotations. Treat them as a structured way to argue against myself, not as evidence.
- **No outcome data exists.** I found no trustworthy head-to-head trial of any of these products [V for what I read]. So this compares *design properties* (what the product does and how well made it looks), not results. A product with fewer features could teach better. That uncertainty cuts in every direction, including ours.
- **Builder's bias, and how it is handled.** I built one of the five contenders. Every score has a stated reason; competitor scores use only what the research files verified; the "end goal" column is scored only on what the roadmap plans; and the dimensions where we are weakest (human coaching, evidence, polish, live play) are on the table.
- **Scores are judgments (grade G0).** 0 = absent, 1 = thin, 2 = solid, 3 = best seen among those compared. Totals are unweighted sums for orientation. A gap of one or two points means nothing.
- **Not read (so not claimed):** most of chess.com's roughly 395 library courses beyond what was sampled (282 sampled, mostly to the first challenge) [V, `docs/research-chesscom-synthesis.md`]; Lichess's Practice and Study contents beyond the source of its Learn module [V for Learn, R for the rest]; ChessKid's lesson list; Chess Steps workbooks (descriptions only) [S]; the full text of Yusupov, Silman and the others (chapter lists only) [S]. Sources: `docs/research-competition-wide.md`, `docs/research-curriculum-orders.md`, `docs/research-chesscom-*.md`, `docs/chess-steps-comparison.md`, `docs/benchmark.md`.
- **Reading coverage is itself thin.** The research behind this note rests on listings and summaries for the in-copyright books, and on 26 public-domain books we hold. The Internet Archive alone lists about 300 pre-1931 English chess texts marked not in copyright [V, search of 12 Nov 2026] and we hold 26. The library is being widened (see `library/REGISTRY.md`).

### Why each score

- **Cost and access (free, no account, offline):** chess.com gates lessons, bots and review behind daily limits and a paid tier [V]; Lichess is free and open source [V]; books and Chess Steps cost money [S].
- **Time to first move:** Lichess Learn: first move within seconds, no reading [V]. Ours: first guided game in about 3 minutes by `check_path_time.py` [V]. chess.com: first opposing piece around minute 11 on its beginner path [V].
- **First real game early in the path:** Lichess Learn has no game at all and sends you to play [V]. chess.com reaches a free game after a block of lessons [V]. Books: none inside the book.
- **Rules and basics, taught as the game needs them:** Lichess's 18-stage Learn is a well-made rules ramp [V]. Ours is shorter and game-first but unreviewed by people.
- **Breadth of curriculum (to roughly club level):** 206 skills and 617 lessons [V] against chess.com's 395 library courses [V]; Lichess has no curriculum beyond Learn, only tools [V, R]; Chess Steps reaches about 2100 in six steps [S].
- **Sequencing: a defensible order, with reasons:** Our order is checked against 17 published orders and is a graph with prerequisites [V], but no coach has signed it. chess.com's library is large and loosely ordered [V]. Expert-authored books and Steps are the gold standard here.
- **Lesson design and feedback quality:** chess.com's best lessons have outstanding feedback and its worst have flat or wrong grading [V]. Ours are machine-checked but their prose is unreviewed by people.
- **Practice volume:** Ours: 10,506 items, median 40 per skill, 50 thin skills [V]. Lichess's puzzle database holds about six million puzzles [V]. Chess Steps workbooks hold about 500 positions per step [S].
- **Spacing and retention built in:** Ours: spaced review and evidence-weighted mastery [V]. chess.com leaves spacing to a separate course product (MoveTrainer) [S]. Books and Lichess leave it to the learner.
- **Testing without the answer's label (transfer):** Unlabelled mixes and stage checks are central to our design; the contrast lab is a doubtful extra (Q26) [V]; chess.com mostly names the theme in titles and prompts [V]; Chess Steps has unlabelled mix books [S].
- **Tactics:** Everyone teaches tactics early; ours is complete in coverage but late in places (D57) and unreviewed.
- **Positional play and plans:** Ours is present from stage 3; strong books (Yusupov, Heisman, Silman) set the bar [S].
- **Endgames:** Ours is tablebase-checked up to 7 pieces [V]; Silman's course is the benchmark [S]; Lichess has a Practice section on basic endings [R].
- **Openings:** Ours: 36 deep-dive lessons, an explorer on 329,379 master games [V]; chess.com's library is far larger [V].
- **Playing opponents of the right strength:** Ours: Stockfish at gentle levels, human-like Maia bots rolling out [V]. chess.com and Lichess have live human opponents and large bot ladders [V, R]. We do not plan to match this.
- **Game review and analysis:** Ours has a full analysis board and a learn-from-mistakes loop [V]; chess.com and Lichess are mature here [V, R].
- **Diagnosis and a personal plan:** Ours: placement, Plan, Today, Games review patterns [V]; chess.com Insights is paywalled [R]; a human teacher is the best diagnostic in books-and-club learning [I].
- **Teaching how to learn (habits, self-checking, reflection):** A design strand of ours (LOOP, SELF, PRA) [V]; nothing comparable found at chess.com or Lichess [V for chess.com]; one beginner guide mentions it [V]. Untested with learners.
- **Correctness and trust of the claims:** Ours: every position and result machine-checked, but wording unreviewed by a strong player (0 of 617 reviewed) [V]. chess.com defects found in sampling: illegal boards, rejected sound moves, words that contradict numbers [V]. Edited books carry human authority.
- **Production values (voice, video, art, polish):** We chose text and board; art and audio are partial [V]. chess.com and ChessKid lead on video and polish [V].
- **Human coaching and community:** We have none by decision (no accounts, no live AI) [V]. Clubs, coaches and forums are where chess.com, Lichess and school programmes win.
- **Evidence of effectiveness:** No independent trial of any product here was found [V for what I read]; vendor and user claims only [S]. We have no learners yet [V].
- **Openness and rights (reusable, no lock-in):** Ours: free forever, rights-checked sources, export/import of progress [V]. Lichess: open source and CC0 data [V].

## 3. What the simulated board says

Seven viewpoints, each arguing from its own corner. These are my own syntheses (rule 5), not anyone's words.

1. **The scholastic coach (teaches children and adult beginners in groups).** A beginner would be delighted by the speed to a first game and the lack of walls. The risk is the middle: Stage 3 to 5 lessons were long and are now cut into parts, and the path is about 133 estimated hours, a school year of weekly sessions [V, `check_depth` minutes]. A coach would want a visible "you can now do this in a real game" milestone at the end of each stage. We have stage checks but have not made them feel like achievements [I].
2. **The club-level trainer (about 1800 to 2000).** Coverage beats most single sources and the skill graph is the right idea. They would test the *explanations* first, because that is where an unreviewed text fails: false generalisations, soft claims, moves that are fine but not the lesson's point. Our machine checks catch wrong facts, not misleading emphasis [I]. This is the biggest trust gap and only a strong player reading the lessons (G3) closes it.
3. **The master-level trainer.** Cares less about breadth and more about whether training builds calculation and pattern recognition at a useful depth. The calculation and thinking-loop strands are unusual and credible on paper; they would want those exercise sets at higher volume. Fifty skills hold fewer than 20 exercises [V].
4. **The learning scientist.** Approves the design choices general learning research supports: retrieval practice, spacing, interleaving, faded hints, game-based transfer [R; `docs/research-learning-science.md` records the sources]. Would add cautions: transfer to chess strength is not demonstrated for any product; "evidence-weighted mastery" is only as good as the difficulty estimates (priors from the Lichess puzzle database, not our own learners); some of our features, such as the standalone contrast lab, rest on plausible theory with no evidence (Q26); and a curriculum this large invites fragmentation, so fewer, better lessons beat more [H].
5. **The instructional designer.** Praises the lesson shape (short, one idea, task right after the explanation) and flags inconsistency: split part lessons with a mechanical bridge card; prose-heavy lessons remain; closing reminder cards are new and unreviewed. Wants a voice pass. Also: the number of destinations (Today, Curriculum, Map, Practice, Lab, Review, Library, Openings, Plan) is high for a beginner; the simplicity pass is still open [V, `docs/design-simplicity-pass.md`].
6. **The product skeptic.** "A beginner already has Lichess free and chess.com free-ish. Why switch?" Honest answer today: *only if they hit a reason.* Reasons we can defend: no limits and no account; a first game inside minutes; every position and result machine-checked; practice that tests without labels; support that fades. Reasons we cannot defend yet: better teaching outcomes, better production, a community, more content than chess.com. The pitch must lead with what is true today; "better than chess.com" is unproven until learners say so.
7. **The adult beginner advocate.** Adults learn on their own schedule, dislike childish art, ads and gamification, and want the *why* before the *what*. Scholarmate's tone and no-streak design suit them. They also want a measurable outcome; "you can now beat the 800-rated bot" motivates more than a percentage of a map. We have the pieces (placement, plan, Maia games) but not the promise stated crisply [I].

**Where the seven agree [I]:** the design is sound and distinctive; trust depends on human review that has not happened; the first hour is a real advantage; and a beginner's choice will be driven more by polish and community than by pedagogy unless Scholarmate makes a visible, testable difference early.

## 4. Where each contender really wins (the honest map)

- **First ten minutes:** Lichess Learn (instant, no reading) and Scholarmate (guided game in about 3 minutes). chess.com is close behind but asks more reading and has more walls [V].
- **Rules plus first checkmates:** a tie between Lichess Learn and Scholarmate; chess.com's rules course lacks set-up, notation, stalemate and draws that we include [V].
- **Practice volume and variety:** Lichess and chess.com puzzles, by orders of magnitude. Our edge is structure and spacing, not size [V].
- **Explanations a learner can trust:** edited books first, then (once reviewed) us; chess.com's best courses are excellent and its sampled worst are flawed [V].
- **A guided path from zero to a club player in one place:** nobody does this well for free. chess.com has the content but fragmented; Chess Steps has the sequence but needs a teacher and money; Lichess has tools, not a path. This is our open ground [I].
- **Live play, community, coaches:** chess.com and Lichess. Not our fight.
- **Production quality and video:** chess.com, ChessKid, paid courses. Not our fight either, but first impressions are partly cosmetic, so our polish debt matters [I].

## 5. How far are we from "better and all-encompassing"?

Defined carefully: *better* means a beginner who tries us beside the others would pick us for the same material; *all-encompassing* means they would not need to leave to continue to about 1400 to 1600.

By the scorecard we sit level with the field on design coverage today (48 against 46 and 44 of 69), because we lead on retention, testing, learning-to-learn and openness while trailing on trust, production, live play and community. The end goal (61) would lead every column. Distance, as hypotheses [H]:

| Area | Where we are | Distance to "better" | What blocks it |
|---|---|---|---|
| First ten hours (S0 to S3) | Built, game-first, checked; being tightened | Close (about 80 percent of the way) [H] | Human read of the lessons; polish on a few screens; a clear "you can now..." milestone |
| First wins to independence (S3 to S4) | Built; some lessons long, split into parts | About 60 percent [H] | Review; pool floor (50 thin skills); tactic coverage check against Chess Steps |
| Developing strength (S5 to S6) | Broad, about 350 lessons, mostly unreviewed | About 50 percent [H] | Review at scale; tactics-to-positional balance; exercise depth |
| Openings and master games | 36 deep dives, 24 famous games, explorer | About 45 percent [H] | Review; more openings; chess.com's library is far larger |
| Playing and review loop | Analysis, review, Stockfish; Maia rolling out | About 50 percent [H] | Maia finale games; a human-like opponent ladder |
| Trust and authority | Machine-checked, unreviewed by people | About 15 percent [H] | A strong player or coach reviewing; named reviewers (G3) |
| Evidence that it works | None | About 0 percent [V] | Real learners and time; arrives only after release |
| Polish and first impression | Partial art, audio, icons, covers | About 40 percent [H] | A design pass; consistency (video is out of scope) |
| Community and human coaching | None by design | Not in scope | Possibly a link to an open community |

A single "percent of the way" would mislead: the first ten hours are near parity, claims of correctness are ahead, and trust and evidence are far behind. The roadmap's "Complete core" figure (69 percent after the D92 correction) measures content-building only, not trust or evidence.

## 6. What would make the choice obvious

A beginner chooses on the first hour, on what they can see they will get, and on whether it feels safe to trust. Ranked by effect and by whether we control it:

**We control (do next):**
1. **State the true promise sharply:** "Play a real game in three minutes. Free. No account. Every position and result checked by an engine. A guided path from the first move to a club game." With the numbers, on the first screen [I].
2. **A visible milestone at each stage** ("you can now beat the 400-rated bot", "you can find a fork in a position nobody labels"), proved by Maia games rather than a percentage. Needs the planned Maia finale games.
3. **Close the thin-pool gap** (50 skills under 20 exercises) and the tactics-to-positional balance [V, `docs/chess-steps-comparison.md`].
4. **A short, honest "compared with" page** using only verified facts about the others, so a skeptic can check us [I]. (The product-page rule says do not name competitors without checking current facts; this note supplies the base.)
5. **Simplify the surface** for a beginner: one main card, fewer destinations until needed (simplicity pass), and drop weak features from the pitch (Q26).
6. **A style and consistency pass** on lessons: voice, bridge cards on split lessons, reminder cards reviewed.
7. **Widen the reading base.** More public-domain books in the library (Znosko-Borovsky, Lasker, Steinitz, Morphy's games and about 250 more candidates) feed both the library and our own research.

**We do not control (but can prepare for):**
8. **Human review.** One strong player reading the first ten hours would turn "machine-checked" into "machine-checked and coach-reviewed", the strongest trust claim available. Tagged `(feedback)`.
9. **Learner evidence.** Instrumentation is local and exportable; publish results honestly when they arrive, including null ones.
10. **A community link** (an open forum or Lichess study space) rather than building one [H].

## 7. Bottom line

- **Today:** Scholarmate is a distinctive, correct, free first hour and a broad curriculum whose design choices are better supported by learning research than most alternatives [R], but it is *not yet* a "why go anywhere else" choice for everyone. A beginner who values live opponents, video, community or an authoritative author will still go elsewhere, and a skeptic cannot yet verify that our teaching works.
- **Where we are already the better choice:** an adult or older beginner who wants to start immediately, wants no account, limits or ads, and wants to learn how to think, practise without labels and see why a move fails. For the first ten hours we are plausibly the best free option [H].
- **The end goal is credible** and would lead the field on design coverage, but it rests on three things outside the builder's hands: strong people reading the content, learners using it, and time. About half the distance is work we can do alone (pools, milestones, polish, simplification, Maia games, a wider reading base); the other half is trust and evidence that only other people supply [H].
- **What to say publicly:** what is verified (free, fast, checked, game-first, research-based design). Do not say "better than chess.com" or "more effective" until learners and a reviewer support it (rules 3 and 6).
