ON PROBLEMS, WITH EXAMPLSfi. 

In a game of such entjless variety as Chess, it 
must necessarily . happen that situations will occur 
where one party will be enabled to check-mate his 
adversary in a certain number of moves. In situa- 
tions like this, the game resolves itself into a 
problem; and the more intricate the moves, the 
more beautiful the problem. Those problems, there- 
fore, are most to be admired, that occur, or may occur, 
in actual play. 

The study of the best problems of the befet masters 
will amply repay the student, inasmuch as it will 
teach him the quickest and most brilliant method of 
playing the ends of games. It will also have the bene- 
ficial effect of teaching the tyro never to despair ; for

inferior may win. 

The Btudy of problems, too, is necessary for those 
who wish to gain a sound knowledge of the game. 
They show the innumerable resources of the game, 
and, what is more important, accustom you to cal- 
culate many moves in advance. 

In Chess, that player is the strongest who can 
foresee and calculate the greatest number of moves on 
each side; consequently, this delightful branch of 
Chess— namely, the solving of problems — ^goes a great 
way to make a tolerably good player. 

The learner will find more advantage in endea- 
vouring to solve the problem from the diagram alone.

advise him not to touch the pieces until he has found

the required number of moves. Moving the pieces ' 
about in every direction will be found not only a great 
loss of time, but no improvement can result from it
