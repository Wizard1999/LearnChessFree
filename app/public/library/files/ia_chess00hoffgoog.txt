Strictly speaking, this is not a Prologue, as inti- 
mated by the heading, but an Epilogue. 

Entrusted with the editorship of the Chess article 
for the "Book of Card and 'i'able Games," the 
article has assumed dimensions far beyond the scope 
and the space which the editor originally intended

In order to make the treatise on Chess more 
easily accessible to the Chess community an^ 
students, it was considered advisable to sever it 
from the "Book," and to publish it separately — 
as it forms a complete treatise on the game — in 
book form. 

The matter contained in this volume is necessarily 
compressed, as it had to form part of the Larger 
work; but if liberty had been given to extencJ 



the useful infonnation, which had to be frequently 
crowded into footnotes, it would form a bulky

The elementary principles of the game have been

of experience, for the use of those who wish to 
become practical players themselves, and also to 
supply some usefal information to the advanced 
student — the aim, therefore, has been 9^ juste milievL 
between too elementary and too advanced. 

The method adopted in treating the Openings is 
that of a teacher giving a lecture to the pupil over 
the chess-board. The spirit of the Opening is 
lucidly explained, a sound model game is generally 
selected for analysis, the positions are copiously 
illustrated by diagrams at short intervals, and the 
moves interspersed with notes, suitable to the 
understanding oi the student. The minor variations^ 
less exhaustively treated, follow with footnotes. 

To the recognized Openings a great deal of atten* 
tion has been devoted, whilst the Openings which

the game, the superiority of a player asserts itselj^ 
both players being here thrown upon their resourcee^ 
as the analysis does not reach beyond the opening.

CLOSE GAMES. 

All games which commence with any other move 
than 1. P to K 4, or in which Black replies witli 
any other move than 1. ... P to K 4, are called 
Close Games. These openings are resorted to eitlier 
by the first player, if he has to meet an opponent 
who is well versed in book knowledge, or by the 
lecond player, if he is afraid to trust himseU in the 
wide field uf the Open Games, with all the by-paths 
and ramifications of the numerous variations and 
combinations, which the player who has the move, 
knowing what opening he is to adopt, might have 
prepared. In Close Games both players are soon 
thrown upon their own resources, as, by the nature 
of the Close Game, it cannot be so thoroughly 
analysed as the Open Games. This is especially 



OHEas. 186 

the case with openings bep;inning with 1. P to Q 4, 
or 1. Kt to E B 3, the latter being only a trans- 
position of the first move, as the main features are 
the same as in the 1. P to Q 4 opening. 

The English Opening, 1. P to Q B 4, is a branch 
of the openings commencing with 1. P to Q 4, or

A good system for practice is to play the attack

The student should not get into the habit of

have his favourite colour. He must never touoli

The first principle is to develop the pieces 
quickly, and never to commence an attack with 
insufficient forces. If a player is able to bring 
more pieces into play than his opponent, it is 
obvious that he must be stronger. When the game 
la well developed, and the Middle game has com- 
manced, the player will frequently be embarrassed 
as to the best continuation. In such cases he 
•hould survey the board, and if he finds that any
