In an era distinguished by the influence of Science,

Such being the nature of the game, two important

of its most valuable qualities ; which, as Dr. Franklin

caution, circumspection, foresight, and perseverance

Notation, to meet the rapidity of thought, must 
unite brevity and precision. It must present the mind 
at once with the true idea, and no other. It must 
not require time to analvse it. 

For the purposes of the writer it must be practicable 
with rapidity. For the reader's use it must be vocaL 
For the solitary student it should be expressive to the

Letters alone unite these requisites, and will per-

due association of ideas. 

By the method explained in the following introduc- 
tion, every square on the board is denoted most pre- 
cisely by its peculiar syllable of two letters only, al- 
though only eight consonants and four vowels are 
employed : their combination, being systematic in strict 
conformity with the correct nomenclature, imposes no 
burden on the memory beyond retaining the order of 
the eight consonants in connexion with the files, and 
the rule for prefixing, or else affixing, the four vowels, 
to distinguish the ranks on each player's half of the 
board. The same consonants singly, stand for the 
pawns of the same files respectively. Small letters are 
employed for all the foregoing objects. Eight other 
consonants represent the Pieces : and for these, capi- 
tals are used, so that the eye at once perceives the 
class of the piece to be moved ; and each being repre- 
sented by a single letter which stands for no other 
piece, every move is indicated by three letters only, 
combining the utmost brevity with the precision of

THE PAWN. 

The pawns are not privileged like the Pieces to go in 
every direction ; but move only forward along their re- 
spective files towards the adversary's rank a. If a 
pawn live to reach it^ he is instantly invested with all 
the powers of whatever Piece the player may prefer, 
according to the state of the game ; and as he gene- 
rally chooses a Queen, this advancement is termed 
^' queening a pawn," and its last moves for this ob- 
ject, "pushing to Queen." This promotion is open 
to every pawn that can arrive at the home of either of 
the adversary's Pieces. Such being the ultimate hope 
and importance attached to the pawns, the greatest at- 
tention should be given to sustain and advance theci, 
especially those in the centre. Philidor's observation 
upon the King's and King's Bishop's pawns, I and Sj

the game :" which in many cases may be asserted, of 
any two central pawns well sustained on adjoining 
files: for although neither should become a Queen, 
the adversary may be pushed to the necessity of sacri- 
ficing a Piece to prevent it.

This difficult pawn-position differs from one by 
Greco, merely in one of the Kings being on the 
Queen's square instead of on his own : this equalizes 
the parties by placing success in the power of which-

him to win only with the move. 

There are two methods of winning from the above 
position. First, by pushing on the pawns so as to 
compel the adversary to stop them, as shown in the 
foregoing instances of convex and concave positions ; 
thereby laying him under the necessity of moving his 
pawns, which the first player must take care to nave 
his King so stationed as to disorder and pick up. 
Should the adverse King, in preference, retire before 
the pawns which beset him, they advance to queen by 
their co-operative power. The second method, is by 
playing the King to secure the opposition against 
the pawns, that not one of them may move without 
capture ; being careful to provide, that on his assuming 
that attitude, the adversary, having to move, shall 
have no alteyiative but retreating his King before the

PIECES AGAINST PIECES. 

The board is frequently cleared of all the pawns be- 
fore a game is finished ; and the Kings remain with 
Pieces only. Some of these endings are exceedingly 
difficult to win ; others only easy when the right me- 
thod of manoeuvring is known ; and all require tact 
for winning neatly. Many persons, after playing tole- 
rably well, and reducing the adversary to a decided 
disparity of force, fumble in the consummation, and

their superiority, and so risk having the game drawn, 
through their want of precision : for, as soon as their 
inability becomes apparent, the opponent on giving 
notice, may count fifty moves, at the termination of 
which, if not beaten, he is, by chess law, entitled to 
draw. This should ensure great attention to the pre- 
sent section.

124 BENEFICIAL EFFECT OF ODDS 

Chess derives all its interest from exciting the men- 
tal powers ; and these it exercises so agreeably, that 
even beginners, when equally matched, derive m>in it 
as much pleasure as good players do. No such excite- 
menty however, can be raised in a good player by one 
muck his inferior : therefore, while the latter may be 
fully amused, the former may be under a severe trial 
of his patience. Yet the progress of the young player 
would be slow, unless he strove with the more ad- 
vanced — while first-rate or second-rate players might 
rarely obtain any enjoyment from their favourite game. 
The remedy for this is easy and perfect. It consists 
in the stronger player giving odds ; which the weaker 
should always offer to accept : for on this point Mr. 
Lewis has justly observed, " it is surely not to be ex- 
pected that one person shall play merely for the 
amusement of anotner. The most improving games,'^ 
says the same eminent writer, *^ are those which are 
difficult for both parties, who consequently are obliged 
to play their best." He that is too conceited to ac- 
cept any Piece offered him, must foreso the best op- 
portunities of improvement and gratification at chess. 
On the other hand, the player who desires to keep up 
his attainments, must insist on giving to an opponent, 
for whom he feels too strong, such odds as will insure
