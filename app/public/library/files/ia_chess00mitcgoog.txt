INTRODUCTORY NOTE 



Among the famous chess players mentioned in this 
book are Dr. Emanuel Lasker, William Steinitz, Frank J. 
Marshall, the American master, and Jose R. Capablanca, 
the expert Cuban player. By winning the world's chess 
championship in 1894 from William Steinitz, who held the 
title for twenty-eight years, Lasker accomplished a feat 
which not only surprised the chess world, but it had a 
serious effect upon his adversary, who it is said never 
played as well after he lost the title. The score of the 
match was Lasker, 10; Steinitz, 5, while three games were 
drawn. 

Frank J. Marshall, the Brooklyn master, won the 
American championship in 1909 from Jackson W. Sho- 
walter of Kentucky. Marshall won seven, Showalter 
scored two, and twelve games were declared drawn. 

Jose R. Capablanca, the brilliant Cuban player, has 
established a remarkable record. He has participated in 
tournaments wherein the strongest masters in the world 
were entered, and in the majority of these he has emerged 
at the top of the ladder.

THE BOAfiD 

The game of chess is played on a board of sixty-four 
squares of black and white alternate colors. It is similar 
to the board used in checkers, but in chess all of the 
squares are played on. The board must be placed so 
that the white square is in the lower right-hand corner. 

Chess is a mimic battle between two armies. There are 
sixteen chessmen on each side, viz.: eight Pawns, two 
Knights, two Bishops, two Rooks, one Sling and one 
Queen are allotted each player at the start of the game. 
The thirty-two chessmen are placed upon the board as 
indicated on Diagram A. 

Care should be taken by the student to note that in the 
printed diagrams White always occupies the foot of the 
board, playing up the board from bottom to top. Black, 
on the other hand, occupies the top and plays downward. 
Hence you will see the word "Black" printed at the top 
of each position pictured in this book, indicating Black's 
side of the board, and the word "White" at the bottom 
of each diagram. 

The student should be careful in actual play, however, 
to become accustomed to playing with both the white 
and black men alternately. Do not accustom yourself to 
one color. This is a very bad habit and, once formed, is 
hard to break.

THE GERMAN NOTATION 

The German notation is another system of numbering 
the squares of the chessboard. It is much simpler than 
our notation and is used in many of the principal countries 
in the world. Most of the leading chess critics have adopted 
it for recording problems and positions. See Diagram C. 

The learner will note that in the German notation the 
board is lettered on the files from A to H, and the squares 
are numbered up the board from 1 to 8. These numbers 
and letters do not change, as in the English notation, and 
the squares retain their original numbers and letters, that 
is, A 1 remains A 1 for both Black and White; the numbers 
do not reverse when we look at the board from the differ- 
ent sides.

MOVEMENTS OF THE PIECES 

Turn again to Diagram A. Here the pieces are placed 
ready to start the game. Take the piece in the lower left- 
hand corner. It is the QR (Queen's Rook) because it is 
placed on the Queen's side of the board. All of the pieces 
counting from Queen's square are the Queen's pieces, and 
those to the right from King's square are the King's. 
Thus the White Queen has a QR, QKt, QB and four 
pawns, viz.: QRP, QKtP, QBP and QP; while the King 
has also a KR, KKt, KB and his four pawns, viz.: 
KP, KBP, KKtP and KRP. On the black side the pieces 
are named in the same manner, after the King or Queen 
according to the side of the board they occupy. 

What makes chess more complicated than checkers is 
the fact that the pieces do not all move alike. The Pawns 
or foot soldiers move slowly, the Knights or cavalry have 
a peculiar jump or leap, the Bishops sweep the diagonal 
squares, while the Rooks move in a straight line along the 
rank and file. Let us study each piece separately. 

THE PAWN 

There are eight Pawns on each side. They all move 
alike, that is, one square at a time, but on the first move, 
and only when the Pawn has not made a previous move, 
it may be played two squares. Consult Diagram D: 

Champion Lasker says the pawns are the soul of chess. 
"Struck by the power and scope of the larger pieces, the 
youthful student ofttimes overlooks the value of the 
humble Pawn." Remember that the Pawn is an im- 
portant member of your army, whose real value increases 
as the game draws to a close. The master who emerges 
with a Pawn ahead in a close struggle usually wins.

PAWN PROMOTION 

Like a single man in checkers, the Pawn which reaches 
its eighth square or any eighth square may be promoted to 
any other piece excepting a King. After it reaches the 
King row — as we may call it — the Pawn may become a 
Queen, a Bishop, a Rook or a Knight. In this way a 
player may have as many Queens, Bishops, Rooks and 
Knights as he has Pawns to promote. A Pawn cannot 
remain a Pawn after reaching the so-called King-row. 

In Diagram D the white Pawn marked (3) on KR7 has 
but one step to go in order to claim a piece of a higher 
denomination. The same applies to the Pawn marked (4) 
on Black's QR7.

THE KNIGHT 

The Knight is a horse, both in looks and in movement. 
His move makes him by far the most difficult of all the 
pieces to master. It is a peculiar sort of a maneuver, in 
which the piece has the power to go two squares in one 
direction and one square at right angles, or one square 
in one direction and two in the other. The Knight has the 
power of leaping or jumping over his own pieces or those 
of the opposite color. Diagram F shows the Knight. 

The Knight in the hands of an experienced player can 
be made to perform astonishingly. He leaps in and out 
amid the mass of pieces and Pawns of both colors, cap- 
turing, attacking, and defending with a movement pecu- 
liar to his kind. It frequently happens that you are en- 
abled to straddle two pieces of greater value with the 
Knight, and thus win what is called the "Exchange."

THE ROOK 

The Rook plays a most important part in the game. 
It is a powerful piece, second only to the Queen, which is 
the strongest piece on the board. The Rook moves at 
right angles any distance, captures as it moves, forward, 
backward or sideways, but never diagonally. On the ap-

Many players have given the Rook the name of Castle.

this book.

the sacrifice can safely be made. It all depends upon 
position and judgment, which you will acquire as your 
knowledge of the mental game and experience in chess 
combinations increases.

THE KING 

The King is virtually the commanding officer of your 
army of chess pieces. Keep that in mind, for a command- 
ing officer is usually made to surrender and is seldom cap- 
tured in actual warfare, and never in the game of chess. 
The King can never be captured. The object of the game 
is to checkmate the King. That is, to surround him on all 
sides with your pieces in such a way that he cannot 
legally capture your pieces, or take the attacking piece, or 
interpose a piece. Then he is checkmated and the game 
is over. (See Checkmate.) 

The King moves only one square at a time in any direc- 
tion forward, backward or sideways. He captures where- 
ever he can legally move. The King cannot, however, 
according to the laws of chess, move to a square com- 
manded by an adverse piece. Nor can the King capture a 
piece of the opposite color protected by another piece, for 
by so doing he would be subjecting himself to capture cr 
check, which is contrary to the rules of the game. Remem- 
ber, a King cannot move into check, that is, he cannot 
place himself open to capture. See Diagram K. 

Great care should be taken by the student to master 
the general rules pertaining to the King. Take the ad- 
vice of most authorities and keep your King under cover, 
away from the fire of the enemy, just as you would pro- 
tect the general of an army. Remember, that your King 
should be kept under cover in the early stages of the 
game, when the contest is young. When you have pro- 
gressed further with your chess studies you will learn that 
the King is used to great advantage in the end-game.

VALUE OF THE PIECES 

Summing up, we find that each piece has a value. 
That is, the Queen by virtue of its great freedom of 
movement is worth, say, 10 Pawns; the Rook is worth 5; 
the Bishop, 3§ and the Knight 3J Pawns. Of course, this 
table is merely to teach you how to estimate the value of 
your pieces. 

A Knight or a Bishop is often exchanged for three Pawns; 
whether it is worth while to make the exchange depends 
upon your position. 

Often the Queen is exchanged for three pieces. Two 
Rooks for a Queen in most instances is considered an even 
exchange, but again it depends entirely upon the state of 
your game. 

A Knight for a Bishop is considered an equal exchange. 
This is the most frequent exchange in chess, and is given 
in most of our chess openings at one stage or other. 

The King is a valuable piece, and may often be used to 
limit an adversary's moves, but since it cannot be cap- 
tured it is unnecessary to discuss its worth.

Hence we have determined that the Queen ranks first 
as the most powerful and valuable piece on the board; 
the Rook comes second; while the Bishop and Knight tie

Strictly speaking, the Pawn is not considered a piece, 
but it is so classed nowadays when counting the chessmen 
in problems, etc. Hence we say a chess set consists of 
thirty-two pieces. The Pawn is of least value, but it also 
is the only participant in the miniature battle which can 
gain promotion.

TERMS USED IN CHESS 

When you have mastered the movements of the chess- 
men, then you are ready to study the terms used in chess. 

CHECKMATE 

The object of the game of chess is unlike checkers in 
that you try to force the King to surrender. In checkers 
the player emerging with the greatest number of pieces 
usually wins, but in chess, while it is true that we must 
needs play for a superiority in force, yet the ultimate ob- 
ject of our game is to place our opponent's King under 
attack from our pieces so that he cannot escape. Then the 
game is won. 

The rule is: When your King is so situated that you 
cannot move out of check (*. e., cannot avoid capture), 
capture the checking piece or interpose one of your own 
force in the path of the attacking piece, then your King is 
checkmated and your adversary scores the game. When 
your King is checked by a Knight it is impossible to inter- 
pose a piece, as the Knight can jump over it. 

CHECK 

It is customary to call "check" when you subject your 
adversary's King to attack by one of your pieces. When 
the King is checked it must be moved, the checking piece 
captured or another piece interposed. The student 
should bear in mind that it is not necessary to call

It has now finally come to pass that another work of

LAWS FOR REGULATION OF GAMES PLAYED OVER THE BOARD 

From these laws the King-move penalty is omitted be- 
cause (1) illegal moves in games of any importance are 
now of rare occurrence and certainly not intentional; (2) 
the King-move penalty, involving possibly the loss of the 
game, is too severe; (3) where there is a time limit the loss 
of time in making an illegal move, retracting the same and 
substituting a legal move is sufficient punishment; (4) the 
King-move penalty may destroy the sequence of play, and 
(5) in European countries this penalty has been abolished 
for some years.

(b) In a series of games between the same two players,

are begun by the same player.

DEFINITIONS AMD FUNDAMENTAL LAWS 

1. The Chessboard and Its Position. 

(a) The "chessboard" is a square divided into sixty- 
four equal squares, of which thirty-two are colored light 
and thirty-two dark, and no two having a side in common 
are of the same color. The light squares are called " white " 
and the dark squares "black." 

(b) In a game between two players the chessboard — 
assuming that the players are on opposite sides of it and 
are facing each other — is properly placed when each 
player has a white square at his right-hand corner of the 
board. 

2. File, Rank and Diagonal. 

The chessboard being placed between the players, a 
continuous line of squares which crosses the board and is 
at right angles to each player's side of the board is called a 
"file," and a continuous line of squares which crosses the 
board and is at right angles to the other two sides of the 
board is called a "rank." 

A "diagonal" is a continuous line of squares which 
crosses the board and has no two squares of the same rank 
or file. 

3. Names of the Ranks. 

The rank nearest to a player is called his "first" rank, 
the rank next to his first rank is called his "second" rank, 
and so on to the " eighth " rank. Thus the first rank of one 
player is the eighth of the other, the second rank of one 
player is the seventh rank of the other, and so on.

4. The Chessmen. 

(a) There are sixteen " chessmen " for each player, 
namely, eight "pieces" and eight "Pawns." One player's 
chessmen are of a light color and his opponent's are of a 
dark color. The one color is called "white" and the other 
"black." 

(b) The eight pieces for each player are one "King," 
one "Queen," two "Rooks," two "Bishops" and two 
"Knights." 

(c) The word "man" is used as a general name for any 
piece or Pawn. 

(d) Throughout the game the white men belong to the 
same player and the black men to his opponent. 

5. Man Standing on a Square. Adjustment. 

(a) A man stands on a square if the center of the man's 
base is on some point within the boundary of the square. 

(b) To "adjust" a man — already standing on a square 
but not having the center of its base on the center of the 
square — is to place the man so that the center of its base 
is nearer to, or on, the center of the square. 

6. Initial Position. 

Before the commencement of an ordinary game the 
white pieces are placed one on each square of the first 
rank of the player of the white men, and in the following 
order, from this player's left to his right: Rook, Knight, 
Bishop, Queen, King, Bishop, Knight, Rook; and the 
black pieces are placed one on each square of the first rank 
of the player of the black men, and in the same order, 
from this player's right to his left. For each player his 
Pawns are placed one on each square of his second rank.

ordinary game stand nearer to his King are called his 
"King's Rook," "King's Bishop" and "King's Knight";

CHAPTER IV 

ADVICE TO THE STUDENT 

Study the chess openings if you wish to become a pro- 
ficient player. 

There is no sure method of victory, no certain way to 
place your pieces which will give you a won game. You 
must see the weak points in your opponent's game and 
proceed to take advantage of these. To know where the 
weak spots are and when they arise can be obtained only 
through a good general knowledge of the openings, 
coupled with over-the-board experience. 

There are a few general rules which may be followed to 
advantage, although there are times when even these are 
not practicable. Here are some of the general principles: 

Do not move P — KR3 or P — QR3 without a definite 
object in view. 

Cultivate a system of development, a free movement of 
your pieces, and avoid a cramped position. 

Play with strong players and you are bound to im- 
prove. Accept odds from them if necessary to balance 
the strength. 

Never allow your hand to hover over the board and 
thus confuse your adversary. 

Seize the open files with your Rooks. By so doing you 
add great strength to your game. This is a splendid gen- 
eral rule to follow. 

To take the adversary's QKtP with your Queen, when 
he has castled on the King's side, more often leads to a bad 

55 



56 CHESS 

game than otherwise. Why take the Queen out of play 
for the sake of a Pawn, which usually is recovered with an 
equal game at least? 

Play out your Knights before Bishops is the advice of 
the world's chess champion, Dr. E. Lasker. 

Avoid giving useless checks. We do not agree with the 
player who said, "Always check — it might be mate." 

Play your Pawns so that they will not interfere with 
the movements of your pieces and yet obstruct your op- 
ponent's as much as possible. 

A Knight posted on KB5, when your adversary's QB 
is off the board, is generally considered a good move.

CHAPTER V 

THE STANDARD CHESS OPENINGS 

All ckess players recognize certain standard openings. 
These appear below under the names always assigned to 
them. In each particular opening there is a principle 
which distinguishes it from other openings, and this 
principle is usually found in the first five moves. The 
openings have, nevertheless, in most instances, been 
carried here to the tenth move, selecting a series of moves 
in each case that should lead to a sound line of play, and 
give each side an equal chance in the game. 

Unless otherwise stated in a footnote, or followed by a 
question mark, the student may safely accept the series 
of moves (or "continuation") as, on the whole, the best for 
a beginner. 

It should be understood, however, that no two masters 
of the game are sure to develop an opening after the first 
five moves in exactly the same way. Each may have his 
own special style of development, and may often trans- 
pose moves to suit his plan of attack or defense. 

Remember, there is no sure way to win; no particular 
formation or method of placing the pieces to make your 
game impregnable. All we can do is to show you the best 
method of conducting your forces in order to give your 
pieces the greatest freedom of movement; practice and 
experience must do the rest. 

The student should become familiar with each opening, 
and if possible memorize the moves up to the tenth turn. 

The beginner should note that "gambit" is not neces- 
sarily a synonym for "opening." A gambit is an opening

establish a certain attacking position. 

Many beginners make the mistake of calling any open-

an opening which involves an early exchange of pieces, 
therefore, is referred to as a "gambit."

CHAPTER VIII 

THE ATTACK 

While I was engaged in writing this book I received a 
letter from a young man in Chicago asking me to explain 
to him what was meant by "the attack." "How am I to 
know when I have the attack? How do I get it? Please 
give me a position and state which side has the attack?" 
These were his queries. 

The attack in chess means the ability to strike, — to 
be in a position to launch an assault upon your opponent's 
forces. (See score and notes of game Helms vs. Smyth.) 
There is no special way to determine when you have the 
attack. In fact, it is left to the player himself. He may 
either decide to attack or remain on the defensive. Dr. 
Lasker says, "the best defense is a counter-attack." That 
is, parry your adversary's thrust with a counter-blow. 
In other words, if I bring my pieces to bear upon your 
King and the pieces surrounding him, you do the same 
to me, *. e., counter-attack. 

It is all a matter of judgment. Of course, there are 
times when it would be bad play to start an attack. Your 
pieces may not be placed properly. Your King may be 
exposed and several of your pieces placed badly. And 
then, again, your opponent may threaten to win in a 
few moves. Then you must be strictly on the defensive, 
and will have no time to attack. 

116 



CHESS 117 

When opening the game, the player with the White 
pieces is said to have the attack, because he has the choice 
of opening. He can select a method of opening his game 
which will, and in most openings does, place you on the 
defensive. You have no time to start a counter-attack 
at first, but if you play correctly, you gradually get your 
pieces into a position where they are of great value to 
you not only in the defense but also become threatening 
to your adversary. The best move is one which attacks 
and defends. An old slogan among chess players is, 
"Attack and defend." That is, move so that you de- 
fend or ward off your opponent's blows, and at the same 
time attack his forces. Remember this saying, "attack 
and defend," and when you move try to abide by it. 

In this book you will come across the phrases, "White 
has a splendid attack" or "Black has a good attack." 
Which means that the side with the attack has his pieces 
placed in such a way that he threatens to win the game 
within a few moves. The player with the attack has 
his pieces placed where they can move freely, he probably 
has more of his pieces developed, that means they control 
more of the board. Hence we define this state of game 
with the above phrases. ** 

In warfare the phrase is easily understood. When the 
enemy attacks, he has plenty of men and ammunition, 
and hence takes the aggressive. One side must defend, 
That's all there is to it. When you have plenty of men 
(meaning when your forces are well placed in the field, 
while your adversary has not yet brought his pieces out) 
then start your attack.

GENERAL RULES 

It is generally best to castle early in the game because it 
places the King in safety and allows more scope to the Rooks. 

Do not block your pieces. Moving your King's Pawn 
or Queen's Pawn one square before you have developed 
your Bishops is often bad play. 

The Knights control more of the board if placed in the 
center, therefore it is better play to bring the Knights out 
toward the center than to develop them on the Rooks' files. 

When a Knight attacks two pieces of greater value at 
the same time, such as two Rooks, or a Queen and Rook, 
this maneuver is called a "fork" or "straddle." Prob- 
ably more games are won by this play than by any other 
move. It is well to develop your Knights early in the 
game. Dr. Lasker says, "Develop your Knights before 
you bring out your Bishops." 

In playing the end-game and in the mid-game it is 
usually good play to obtain a passed Pawn, while doubled 
Pawns are not to be desired, because they interfere with 
and cannot be made protect each other.

CHAPTER X 

FOUR-HANDED CHESS 

There seems to be no limit to the varieties of chess. 
For many years attempts have been made to improve on 
or change the style of the American and European game; 
but while several interesting novelties have been created, 
none has served to take the place of the standard game, 
until now it begins to look as though the game could not 
be improved upon. * 

Through the courtesy of B. Rodgers, the Philadelphia 
checker expert, the writer has recently come into pos- 
session of a copy of Frere's Chess Hand Book, published in 
1858, and containing a most interesting description of four- 
handed chess. 

The game of chess for four persons is played on a board 
of 160 squares. From the sketch it will be seen that each 
of the four players has a set of men placed as indicated. 
We suppose the players to be A, B, C, and D, and that 
the four colors used are white, black, red, and green. It 
is right to observe that in all points, excepting such as are 
here indicated, the game for four is played similar to the 
game for two. 

A and B play with the white and black pieces, in part- 
nership against C and D, to whom are appropriated the 
green and red. The partners sit opposite each other. 

The pieces are the same in number, but their position 
is slightly different at the beginning to what it is in the 
ordinary game. The only difference, however, relates to

The move passes round in turn, always to the left. 
Each player supports and assists his partner to the ut- 
most, while he opposes the two adverse parties. 

The Kings of the partners may move on to adjoining 
squares, and, of course, can go freely into the range of 
any of their partners' pieces. A Queen or other piece 
cannot give check to the partner's King. 

No player is allowed to move a piece or Pawn the re- 
moval of which would open his partner's King to a check 
from either of the hostile powers, any more than he may 
uncover his own King to a similar check. 

Each player supports his partner in an attack; thus, 
should A put his Queen in take of D's King, unsupported, 
and should C be unable to capture her or otherwise pro- 
vide for the check, B may support the Queen, and even 
give mate in this compound manner. 

The Pawns can only move one square at a time and not 
two squares on the first move as in the ordinary game. 

It is the general rule not to permit castling in the 
game for four. With some persons, however, it is the 
custom to adopt it. 

The Pawns do not become pieces when they reach the 
eight squares, such squares being friendly squares, be- 
cause they are in the territory of your partner. But on 
a Pawn's attaining any one of the extreme line of squares 
right or left, it becomes a Queen. Thus, A can only 
Queen a Pawn by getting it to one of the end squares of 
D or C. It is evident from this that a Pawn can only 
Queen through making repeated captures. 

When a Pawn has attained either one of the ultimate 
squares of the board belonging to your partner, such 
Pawn remains there as a Pawn, and moves back again

as. a Pawn, one square at a time in the direction toward 
you. A Pawn which has reached either one of the eight 
ultimate friendly squares should be marked in some way 
to show that it has exchanged its own line of march for a 
power exclusively of backward motion. After the Pawn 
returns to the line from which it originally started, it 
moves forward again as it did at first. 

As the partners sit opposite, it sometimes happens that 
their Pawns meet on the board. In every such case they 
are allowed to leap*over the friendly Pawn and place them- 
selves on the move upon the square beyond, always pre- 
serving their forward or backward motion, but never 
leaving their file save to capture. 

The game is only won when the two partners are check- 
mated. Should one be checkmated and the other stale- 
mated, the game is drawn as if both were stalemated. 

Should a player be checkmated his pieces are not re- 
moved from the board, but must remain in the same posi- 
tion, his partner continuing the fight single-handed. 
Of course, while checkmated a player cannot move, and 
therefore misses his turn. His partner may at any time 
relieve him, if he can, from the checkmate, in which event 
his pieces regain life, and he again moves in his turn. 
While a player is in checkmate his pieces cannot be taken 
by the adversary, but they remain in the same position. 
In this case they present a species of " Caput mortuum," 
devoid of all offending properties; thus, should the squares 
be open the adverse Pawns or pieces may move between 
them, going into their check or range with impunity. 
They still, however, offer the inert resistance of a lifeless 
mass by blocking the squares they actually occupy. 



CHAPTER XI 

A BRIEF HISTORY OF THE GAME 

Chess was first known in India, according to historians, 
where allusions to the game began to appear in Sanskrit 
literature in the seventh century of our era. A number 
of passages from works of that period have been held by 
Sanskrit scholars to contain references to chess. The 
earliest reference occurs in Subandhu's Vasavadatta. In 
this work the author thus describes the rainy season: 

"The time of the rains played its game with frogs for 
chessmen (nayadyutair), which, yellow and green in color, 
as if mottled with lac, leapt up on the black field (or 
garden bed) squares (koshthika). ,, 

H. J. R. Murray in his excellent History of Chess says: 
"From the mention of two colors only we may perhaps 
infer that Subandhu was thinking of a two-handed form 
of chess. The reference to chess in this passage appears 
to me to be quite satisfactory." 

From India the game spread round the world, but 
changed its characteristics with the changes in men's 
ideals, and especially as to the differences between the 
Aryan- or Indo-European, and the Mongolian. These 
differences prevent even chess, in any one form, being 
(for the next thousand years perhaps) really universal, 
unless we can claim universality for the European-Ameri- 
can chess on the ground that there are now, even in China 
and Japan and wherever the European race can live, some

players of the European game, however few they may be 
to the hundred square miles. But the chess principle — 
the win by checkmate, with similarities as to most of the 
powers and positions of the pieces — is really universal. 

The Indian origin of chess has long been established, 
and Mr. Murray's review of old and new evidence con- 
firms the conclusion that the two-handed chaturanga, 
which is the parent game of all Oriental and European 
variants, cannot be traced further back than the begin- 
ning of the seventh century of our era. Board games are 
of much greater antiquity, of course; and every known 
people, with the exception of the ancient tribes of Aus- 
tralia and New Guinea, seem to have invented for co- 
operative diversion a game played with board and men 
falling into one of three groups: (1) race-games, in which 
the men are moved along a definite track; (2) hunt or 
siege games, in which one side endeavors to block or con- 
fine the adversary; (3) war games, in which the capture 
of prisoners play a considerable part. Typical European 
examples of these three groups are back-gammon (tables 
of nard in medieval times), fox and geese, and chess. 
Such pastimes are so numerous, so various, and so widely 
distributed in space and time that it is vain to seek a 
common ancestry. That games were played on a board 
of 8 x 8 squares in India centuries before the first mention 
of chaturanga is an undeniable fact. In one of the earliest 
Buddhist documents, dating back to the fifth century 
B. C, and purporting to record the actual words of 
Gautama, the recluse is warned to hold aloof "from games 
on boards with eight or ten rows of squares" and "from 
the same games played by imagining such boards in the 
air." This is the oldest known list of games and the old-

fashioned historians, who like to think that Noah re- 
lieved the dulness of his voyage by chess-playing, would 
have cheerfully taken the second prohibition as a proof 
that blindfold chess was practised in Gautama's days. 
There can be little doubt that the historic chess was the 
outcome of a long process of evolution and was not one 
man's invention any more than were the so-called Arabic 
numerals, which also originated in the brooding Indian 
mind. 

In all probability, the earliest advance of chess was 
westward to Persia; the eastward advance was along 
three main lines. One route took the game by way of 
Kashmir to China, Corea, and Japan. A second, possibly 
that by which Buddhism traveled, gave chess to Further 
India. At a later date it spread to the Malays, Tibet, 
and the northern tribes of Asia. Meanwhile Persia had 
passed on chess to Byzantium, and Islam learnt the game 
as a result of the Mohammedan conquest of Persia. 
Henceforward the Moslems were the chief pioneers and 
purveyors of chess, carrying their game as far west as 
Spain, and eastward to India, where they imposed the 
Arabic nomenclature on the Northern and Central 
Provinces. Christian Europe had begun to learn chess 
from the Moors as early as 1000 A. D. From the Medi- 
terranean shores it spread over France and Germany to 
England, to the Scandinavian lands, and to Iceland. 
And wherever it went it killed out the older board games 
(except backgammon) and became the chief indoor diver- 
sion of kings, chieftains, noblemen and their womenfolk. 
Everywhere a knowledge of it was regarded as ennobling; 
expert players were highly honored, richly rewarded. 
Chess migated the manners of the age and provided a 



CHESS 165 

means whereby the lover and the beloved, Ferdinand and 
Miranda, might entertain one another over the board. 

It was not until chess entered Western Europe that it 
took its place in the main stream of modern civilization 
and progressed in form and rule until it assumed its 
present perfection. Chess was first played by Christians 
under the same code that was followed throughout the 
Mohammedan world, and for a considerable period (down 
to 1200 A. D. perhaps) there was no serious difference of 
rule or move "from the Indus to the Atlantic" and from 
Sahara to Iceland. Thus for a time the present univer- 
sality of chess, a common language of joie-de-jouer all 
over the world, was anticipated. But the Moslem style 
stood still after the brief but brilliant creative epoch of 
Islamism had ceased; and any change for the better that 
afterward occurred in Moslem theory and practice was 
due to the reflex influence of the progressive chess of 
Europe.
