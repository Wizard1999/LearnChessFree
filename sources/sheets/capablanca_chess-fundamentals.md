## 2. One-paragraph portrait

A manual by a reigning world champion for a motivated adult beginner who is eager to start playing full games and who is expected to reach master-class understanding largely through his own effort and, if possible, a teacher (the author says outright that a book cannot teach play by itself). [READ] The method is a spiral and example-led: it starts with the simplest mates and king-and-pawn endings so that the power of the pieces is seen without clutter, then loops through combinations, piece values, opening principles, middle-game attack, general theory (the initiative), more endings, pawn structure, and finally fourteen of the author's own games with candid notes (including losses and his own mistakes). Along the way it states a small number of crisp principles, backs each with one or two examples, and leaves variations for the student to work out. The voice is that of an authoritative, calm, slightly vain master: confident and sweeping (a move is condemned as wrong on principle), fond of appeals to experience, willing to admit mistakes in his own games, and openly dismissive of the then-new hypermodern ideas. [READ]

### 4B. Piece values and minor-piece endings

**CI-16 Relative piece values are a set of comparisons, not a table.**
(b) No complete exact table exists; compare the pieces pairwise. ## 6. Teaching method observed

- **Sequencing.** [READ] A deliberate spiral. He says in the chapter II opening that he is going back to endings, then to the middle-game, then to the openings, so that progress will be gradual and homogeneous, and the reader's foundation firm. Endings of the simplest kind come first because the power of the pieces is visible there; the second lap adds opposition and minor-piece values; the third adds heavy-piece endings; the opening section is only a short, principle-based treatment; and the later chapters return to openings through pawns, holes and complete games.
- **Format.** [READ] A principle in italics, one or two exact examples, the conclusion drawn from them. Examples are mostly complete variations including the opponent's best defence; at points the author explicitly shows the wrong try first (Ex. 42, 59) so the learner sees why the natural move fails.
- **Questions to the reader.** [READ] Not Socratic in form; instead short imperatives asking the student to discover why a move fails, to work out a branch himself, or to examine a position closely. By grep I count roughly a dozen explicit hand-offs and about 50 lines that address the student directly.
- **What is left out.** [READ] Variations in the games, the final solution in several endings (Ex. 59, 60), the justification of some principles. No exercises with solutions, no assessment, no graded difficulty: the book relies on the reader's self-directed analysis. The author says openly that a book cannot teach play on its own and that a teacher or experience is needed.
- **Pedagogical stance.** [READ] Types of positions rather than isolated tricks (Ch. III introduces combination types); principles first; explanation of why a move was wrong; honest annotation of the author's own errors (Part II); repeated cross-references; mild boasting. He states some limits openly: the opening lines of Ex. 18 are improvised; the Ruy Lopez positions of Sec. 32 are given without the move sequences so that the student learns to build possible positions in his own mind; the games are chosen because he knows them best.
- **Weaknesses as pedagogy.** [INFER] No hint ladder; the reader is told to work it out without a way to check; absolute language invites over-generalisation; no failing look-alikes (the book rarely shows the case where the principle does *not* hold); positional ideas are asserted from authority.
- **What we can borrow.** [INFER] The spiral order; one-principle-per-example economy; the explicit wrong-try-first pattern in Ex. 42 and 59 (a ready-made contrast pair); the candid self-annotation of Part II as a model for GAME.OWN.

## 8. Candidate exercise & contrast-family ideas

Cue levels follow `content-schema.md`: 1 named, 2 themed set, 3 mixed/unlabeled, 4 in-play. All positions to be generated or selected and verified by us; none are copied from the book.

1. **Rook mate: which king move?** Position: K+R v K, defender king on the sixth rank. Learner picks the attacker's king move (find_move). Cue 1-2. Contrast: working = king to the adjacent file toward the centre; failing look-alike = king onto the same file (the book warns it takes longer; check by DTM); irrelevant = moving the rook along its file.
2. **Stalemate or not?** Position: Q v K or Q v R where a queen move may stalemate. Learner classifies each candidate (classify_position). Cue 2-3. Contrast: working = a move that restricts but leaves a legal reply; failing look-alike = a move that leaves none; irrelevant = a move on the far side with no effect.
3. **K+P v K with a gap.** Predict win/draw for a family varying one feature each: attacker's king ahead with a gap, level, behind; defender has the opposition or not; side to move; rook pawn. Cue 2-3, predict_outcome, tablebase-verified. Working = king ahead with a gap and the opposition secured; failing look-alike = king ahead with a gap but the defender holds the opposition (shows the book's rule is incomplete) [CHECK]; irrelevant = moving the far-side pieces not in play.
4. **Opposition parity.** Kings facing each other with 0, 1, 2 or 3 squares between, either side to move. Learner chooses the king move that keeps or gains the opposition, or predicts the result with one pawn present. Cue 1-3. Working = the right parity with the opponent to move; failing look-alike = the same facing kings with the wrong player to move (the opposition is lost); irrelevant = the pawn's distance when it is not capturable.
5. **Distant opposition as a defence.** King and a pawn each; the defender is a pawn down; choose between a close and a distant king move. Cue 3. Working = distant opposition; failing look-alike = close opposition that the defender's own pawn blocks (the book's Ex. 28 idea, to be rebuilt with our positions) [CHECK].
6. **Race counting.** Two passed pawns, kings far; learner predicts who queens first and whether the first queen captures the second (predict_outcome). Cue 2-3. Working = equal-count race where first queening lands on the controlled square; failing look-alike = same race where a capture of the pawn loses the queen's control of the key square; irrelevant = an extra far-away king move not needed.
7. **Which flank to attack?** Pawn ending with pawns on both wings; learner selects the first pawn move (find_move) between pushing the free pawn, stopping an advance, or marching the king. Cue 3. Working = act at once on the stronger flank; failing look-alike = stopping an advance when the king is far away and the stronger flank is lost; irrelevant = pushing a pawn that is blocked anyway.
8. **Bishop or knight?** Endings with a minor piece each plus pawns. Learner predicts which side stands better (classify). Cue 2-3. Working = pawns on both wings and a passed pawn favour the bishop; failing look-alike = a locked one-wing structure where the knight is at least equal (the book's own exception); irrelevant = swapping a pair of far-flank pawns that does not change the structure. Engine labels as evaluation bands, not one number [CHECK].
9. **Wrong bishop.** Rook-pawn endings with a bishop; learner predicts win/draw. Cue 1-3. Working = bishop that controls the queening square wins; failing look-alike = wrong-coloured bishop with defender king able to reach the corner; irrelevant = the side of the board. Tablebase-verified.
10. **Can two knights mate?** Classify forced mate: yes/no for K+2N v K and K+2N v K+P. Cue 1-2. Working = K+2N v K+P positions in which mate is forced (to be selected from the tablebase); failing look-alike = K+2N v bare king; irrelevant = which wing the pieces stand on. Tablebase-verified.
11. **Q v R separation drill.** Learner finds the check sequence that separates the rook from the king, then plays out against the tablebase bot (D4, cue 4). Cue 3-4. Stalemate-trap variant as a failing look-alike.
12. **B+N mate corners.** Given the bishop's colour, choose the mating corners (classify), then drive the king to the right corner step by step against the tablebase bot. Cue 1 then 4.
13. **Bishop sacrifice on h7 / the pawn in front of the king.** Positions that differ in one feature: knight can reach g5; queen can reach h5; defender knight on f6; defender bishop controls the diagonal. Task: find the winning sacrifice or decline (find_move / classify). Cue 2-3. Working = sacrifice supported by two pieces; failing look-alike = same sacrifice but with a defender piece covering h7/h5; irrelevant = queenside pawn moves. The conditions are ours [OUTSIDE]; verify by engine at the wins or mates level (CD-5).
14. **Pawn cover loosened.** Positions with a castled king where one side has advanced the g-pawn one square; learner classifies dangerous or harmless (classify_position). Cue 2-3. Working = queen+rook-lift+knight on the attacking side available; failing look-alike = same pawn move but a defender piece covers the key squares; irrelevant = a queenside pawn move.
15. **Take the free pawn?** Early-opening positions with an offered pawn. Learner decides capture or decline (find_move). Cue 3. Working = sure material, no trap; failing look-alike = a pawn that loses material to a Légal-type trap; irrelevant = a developing move elsewhere. Tests the book's beginner rule and its limits.
16. **Is this piece cut off?** Positions with a bishop or knight behind its own pawns. Learner classifies effectively out of play vs has a way out (classify). Cue 2-3. Working = piece cannot return except at a cost; failing look-alike = piece can be freed by a sacrifice or exchange; irrelevant = a well-placed piece elsewhere.
17. **Where can a knight sit forever?** Learner marks squares an enemy pawn can never challenge (a hole) and then decides if our pieces can use it. Cue 1-3. Working = hole reachable and supported; failing look-alike = hole attackable by a lever; irrelevant = a hole on a square we cannot reach. Links to POS.OUTPOST.
18. **Rook ending cut-off.** Rook + pawn v rook where the enemy king is cut off by two files (or one); predict win/draw. Cue 2-3. Working = two files and pawn reaches the fourth rank before checks begin; failing look-alike = defender rook behind with the move; irrelevant = pawns on the other wing. Tablebase-verified.
19. **Active or passive defence?** Worse rook endings; learner chooses a defensive plan (waiting vs counter-activity). Cue 3-4. Working = counter-demonstration; failing look-alike = Philidor-type setup where passivity is exactly right; irrelevant = a king move that changes nothing. Engine/tablebase-verified; directly tests the book's absolute claim.
20. **Guess the plan, Capablanca-Blanco style.** Guess-the-move on the French squeeze game (the moves are facts): at selected points learner names the plan class (tie pieces to the weak pawn; switch wing) before moves are revealed. Cue 3-4; GAME.GUESS.

## 9. Agreements and tensions with the other sources in this batch and with modern teaching

I have **not** read the other four books in this task; what follows is background memory [OUTSIDE] and must be reconciled when their sheets exist.

- **Nimzowitsch, *My System*.** [OUTSIDE] Agreements: both prize the open file and seventh rank (Capablanca's rook-ending lessons are about active rooks), the weak square/hole, and pawn weaknesses. Tension: Nimzowitsch is usually counted among the founders of the hypermodern ideas that the preface dismisses as old principles in new tactics [OUTSIDE]; Capablanca's text stresses controlling central squares as a condition for any attack on the king [READ], whereas the hypermodern school accepted control from a distance and delayed pawn occupation of the centre [OUTSIDE]. Capablanca names Nimzowitsch three times in the text (a Four Knights variation in Ex. 54, fn 9 on one of his games, and a post-game analysis correcting a Lasker variation in G5). [READ] Nimzowitsch's terms prophylaxis and over-protection have no direct counterpart in Capablanca; Capablanca's keep-freedom-while-hampering rule is a loose relative of restraint. [INFER]
- **Em. Lasker, *Common Sense in Chess*.** [OUTSIDE, from memory] Both address beginners in plain principles (development, material, the centre). Whether Lasker's weighting of the initiative, exchanges, and the pawn matches Capablanca's needs a check against that sheet. Capablanca frequently quotes or cites Lasker's play and analysis (Ex. 52 comment, G5, G1 note). [READ]
- **Znosko-Borovsky, *The Middle Game in Chess*.** [OUTSIDE, uncertain] I do not reliably know this book's content; the only certain link is that Znosko-Borovsky was Capablanca's opponent in G4 (spelled Snosko-Borovski in the text). [READ] Compare their treatments of attack and defence, and of plans, when the sheet is written.
- **Ed. Lasker, *Chess Strategy*.** [OUTSIDE, uncertain] Likely overlap on pawn structure, weak pawns and holes, and on the theory of the minor pieces; I cannot state specifics. Do not rely on this line.
- **Modern teaching.** [OUTSIDE] (a) Piece values and the bishop pair: modern tables and engine experience agree the bishop pair is valuable but not with the same certainty. (b) Opening principles: still taught as defaults, with exceptions routinely shown. (c) Conditioned rules and exceptions: Watson-style teaching contradicts the absolute wording of Sec. 31-32. (d) Endgame theory: key squares and triangulation and tablebase-verified Lucena/Philidor are more precise than Capablanca's rules of thumb; Dvoretsky-style teaching builds endgame technique on verified positions. (e) Calculation: Capablanca says visualise, but gives no candidate-and-tree method like Kotov's (the book is silent on method; [READ: no method given]). (f) Self-annotation before the engine (CD-12): Part II's candid notes are close to what we want from learners.
