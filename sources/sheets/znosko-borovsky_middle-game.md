## 2. One-paragraph portrait

Znosko-Borovsky wants to turn the middle game, which he says has no theory and no classification, into something teachable: a procedure for **valuing a position** (count, then inspect piece by piece, then identify its *character*), a **plan** that follows from that valuation, and a **mode of play** (active, passive or manoeuvring) that follows from whether you stand better, worse or equal. He builds this from an unusual starting point: three *elements* (force, space, time) derived by thought experiments on the empty board and on the pieces' movements, which occupy about a quarter of the book; the actual middle-game material (Part II) is then organised not by attack/defence or by wing/centre, which he rejects as external, but by *which element carries your advantage*. Part III is an unannotated or lightly annotated exercise bank. The audience is deliberately mixed: he says he cannot separate beginner from strong player, and in practice the book needs a reader who already plays decently, tolerates abstraction, and will study it carefully rather than read it on a train (preface). The teaching method is deductive and Socratic: assert a principle from first principles, test it on a master game, show a case where the naive count misleads, then ask the reader to do the same on the next example. The voice is earnest, dogmatic about objective facts and laws without exceptions, occasionally quaint, and on a few points (piece values in pawn-units, square values, geometric proofs for the knight) unreliable by modern standards. Its lasting value is the **procedure and the contrasts it draws**, not the numbers. His own summary of the stakes, in the preface, is that the middle game "is chess itself" (Preface).

## 3. Structure of the book

- **Preface (pp. vii–x).** Claim that the middle game is unstudied and unclassified; appeal to the book as one connected chain of thought; acknowledgement that examples are few and chosen without his library; advice to read slowly.

## 4. Core ideas

Format: **(a)** name · **(b)** idea in my words · **(c)** conditions under which it holds · **(d)** exceptions or limits (stated by him, or implied/mine) · **(e)** where · **(f)** example type · **(g)** a likely misconception. IDs C01–C34 are for cross-reference in this sheet only.

### A. The frame: elements, counts, and what counts as a gain

**C01. Three elements: force, space, time**
- (b) Every advantage is an advantage in one or more of three things: material and piece power (force), control of board territory especially strong squares (space), and having made more useful moves (time). A sacrifice is a swap of one element for another; a gambit swaps force for time. [READ]
- (c) Useful as a first sorting step in positions that are quiet enough that nobody is mating; best in the opening-to-middle-game transition where moves and ranks can be counted. [I]
- (d) He says the elements usually pull against each other and the job is to see which advantage the actual position makes possible. He stresses **fictitious** gains in each element (C04). Not a complete theory of chess: pawn structure, king safety and activity are handled under *character* (C06).
- (e) Part I Ch. I–III (esp. pp. 50–54); reused throughout Part II. 
- (f) Matched pairs of positions with different leads; the French Defence early-centre example (space against time, credited to Alapin) [READ].
- (g) That *ahead in everything* means winning. His own counterexamples (Diagrams XXXVIII, XLIV) show otherwise.

**C02. Formal (external) count**
- (b) Step one of valuation: count pieces and pawns, note whose pawns and pieces stand on higher ranks and the strong squares, and count the number of moves each side has made to reach the position. [READ]
- (c) Reliable only when the three counts agree and nothing tactical is hanging; he says so.
- (d) The count of *moves made* is the shakiest part (see C03). Also the count ignores captures and structure.
- (e) Part I Ch. III (Diagrams XXXV–XXXVI, Capablanca–Chajes: counts 9 vs 11, then 14 vs 12); Part II Ch. I (Diagrams XLII–XLIV).
- (f) *Count the moves* tables on a mid-opening position.
- (g) That a higher count means a lead. See C04.

**C03. What a move is worth (time in detail)**
- (b) A move loses value if it is repeated, undone, forced by the opponent, or made too early or too late. Freedom of choice is strength: the number of reasonable moves available should grow during mobilisation, and a shrinking list is the sign a position is deteriorating. Moves must belong to a cycle or plan, not be played one at a time. [READ]
- (c) Holds in development and early middlegame positions and for comparing two similar structures.
- (d) He admits the time element cannot be measured as cleanly as space; he declines to treat clock time. Counting *freedom of choice* is a coarse mobility measure.
- (e) Part I Ch. I *Time* (pp. 10–12); Part II Ch. I (Diagram XLV: queen almost without moves).
- (f) Positions where one side has a knight on the rim after two moves and a queen with no safe square.
- (g) That a piece *developed* in one move is better than a piece developed in two; the square matters (C25).

**C04. Fictitious gains and the counts that mislead**
- (b) A gain in one element can be unreal: a rook-pawn run that develops nothing is fake time; a wing advance when the game is decided elsewhere is fake space; a pawn won just before being mated is fake force. Two worked cases: a position where White *gained* three tempi by wasting moves (Teichmann Black vs Vergani, Diagram XLIII) and one where Black led in force, space and time yet lost quickly (McConnell vs Morphy, Diagram XLIV). [READ]
- (c) The check is: *what were those extra moves used for, and where are the pieces?*
- (d) He gives no formula for deciding when a gain is real; you must inspect (C05).
- (e) Part I Ch. III (pp. 53–54); Part II Ch. I (pp. 68–70).
- (f) Minimal pairs: same count, different usefulness of the moves.
- (g) *Counting is objective, therefore correct.*

**C05. Qualitative (internal) valuation: the piece-by-piece audit**
- (b) Step two. For each side ask: which strong squares are occupied or watched? Which pieces are actually in play, which are shut in or doing a pawn's job? Which are connected? Which have few or no safe moves (a queen with none is nearly stalemated)? How many times is each important square attacked, and by what? Which pawns are passed, isolated, doubled, backward, and who can attack them? Which lines are open and who controls them? [READ]
- (c) Quiet positions with all pieces developed or nearly so. It's a checklist, not a calculation.
- (d) It does not find tactics. The same analysis (Diagram XLV, Charousek vs Süchting) is followed by concrete tests of sacrifices, which in that example fail; he warns that rushing a plan without the needed pieces loses the advantage.
- (e) Part II Ch. I, pp. 70–79 (single position analysed over ten pages).
- (f) A position with a clear lead in piece activity but no obvious plan.
- (g) Believing the audit alone produces a move.

**C06. Character of the position**
- (b) Step three. Beyond counts and audits, find the one or two features that make this position unlike the neighbouring ones; those decide the plan. His sharpest example: with White ahead in force, space and time, Black wins at once by a rook sacrifice on the g-file because White's king stands on g1; put the king on b1, change nothing else, and the attack is empty (Paulsen–Morphy, Diagram XXXVIII). [READ]
- (c) Most visible when there is a king-safety asymmetry or a locked pawn structure.
- (d) He does not define *character* operationally; it is whatever the best analysis finds. [I]
- (e) Part I Ch. III (pp. 54–55); Part II Ch. I (pp. 74–75, 81–84).
- (f) One-feature-change pairs (king moved to the other wing; a defender's a-pawn on its home square versus the b-pawn one square up, see C19).
- (g) That character is a vague *feel*; he insists it rests on objective differences.

**C07. The difference method**
- (b) To find the character, list the *differences between the rival positions*: which pawn is in a different place, which piece has a different diagonal, which wing has the king. Every difference suggests a possibility or threat for one side. [READ]
- (c) Works when the two sides are structurally similar; weaker when everything differs.
- (d) None stated. [I] Close to the modern habit of comparing imbalances; unlike modern lists it does not rank them.
- (e) Part II Ch. I (p. 75 on, where he says outright that the differences between the rival positions are easy to list); Ch. IV (Diagrams LXXIV–LXXV: differences as small as a bishop on f2 vs g3).
- (f) Near-symmetrical positions with one asymmetry.
- (g) That a difference automatically favours the side that has it; it may favour the other (a bishop that looks active on an open diagonal in Riga–Berlin turns out to be the less useful placement).

### B. Classification and plan

**C08. Superior / inferior / equal, and active / passive / neutral**
- (b) The right kind of game follows from the valuation: stand better and play actively; stand worse and defend, aiming to restore freedom; stand equal and manoeuvre. Playing actively from an inferior position is the most dangerous choice, and playing passively from a superior one is the most wasteful. [READ]
- (c) Needs an honest valuation first, which is the hard part.
- (d) He grants that in practice the weaker side should still try to win and that counter-attack is normal (Part II Ch. III); he declines to study cases where a bad position wins, saying such wins pass through equality. He says that when one side has an overwhelming advantage in every element, the other side should simply give up the game, which is dated advice (section 5).
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 65–67, 81); Ch. II–IV.
- (f) Pairs of positions with the same pieces and opposite evaluation.
- (g) That *inferior* means *must be passive*. Counter-attack is his stated way out (C22, C23).

**C09. A plan comes from the valuation and is revised**
- (b) A plan is a general direction plus a guess at the opponent's main counter-moves, not a full forecast. It grows from the first move and shows itself gradually. Every so often stop and re-do the valuation from scratch, as a spectator would, to get free from the pull of earlier forced moves; he cites the proverb that the "looker-on sees most of the game" (Part II Ch. I) as the reason. [READ]
- (c) Applies throughout; most useful where the player's current plan is habit-driven.
- (d) He admits no mind sees everything; if the plan is slightly off it may not matter because the game reveals other aims; sometimes take small gains while preventing losses. The Pillsbury–Tarrasch example shows both sides' plans were sound and the game turned on a conversion error (C16).
- (e) Part II Ch. I, pp. 80–81, 99.
- (f) Mid-game position, ask: state each side's plan.
- (g) That a plan must be fixed in advance.

**C10. Allocating pieces and ordering their arrival**
- (b) Decide where the main blow falls, which pieces can be spared for it, and which are the minimum needed for defence. Keep the defenders also useful where possible (knight on f3 and bishop on g2 both guard the castled king and serve the centre). Short-range pieces brought in first get driven off or exchanged before the plan matures; long-range pieces brought in first leave the opponent freedom; mix the two. Threats must be able to dislodge hostile forces from the points they hold. [READ]
- (c) When there is a definite target (king, weak pawn).
- (d) *Reserve only the necessary defenders* is risky if the count of defenders is miscalculated; see C32.
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 86–91); Part I Ch. II (*King*, pp. 38–40).
- (f) Attack-and-defence bookkeeping on one wing.
- (g) That all pieces should rush to the attack.

**C11. Tempo of play must match the size of the advantage; race counting**
- (b) The bigger the advantage, the faster and more energetically it must be pressed; slow preparatory moves let the advantage evaporate, while a hasty attack without enough pieces throws it away. He counts the number of moves each side's plan needs (White's attack needs seven moves and Black's defence five; later Black's pawn win takes five and White's counter-threat four) and compares. [READ]
- (c) Plans with clear, mostly forcing move sequences and few intermediate options.
- (d) He does not count opponent replies or intermediate checks; the race is only as good as the counts [CHECK by engine for any item built on it].
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 76–79, 95–108).
- (f) Race items: two flank plans, which arrives first.
- (g) Believing the shorter plan wins automatically; a plan may need to be altered because a threat changes the count.

**C12. Concealing the plan**
- (b) Hide it by making innocuous-looking moves; or choose forcing moves so that the distant aim is not noticed; the cost of concealment is that slow moves give the opponent time. One game (Showalter–Lasker, Diagram XLVIII) shows a sequence of three quiet moves that invites the opponent to put queen and rook on attacking squares just before the real blow. [READ]
- (c) Against an opponent who ignores why a move is made.
- (d) The anecdote about making an illegal move so the touch-move rule forces a king move is a folk story, not advice.
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 88–89).
- (f) Position with a quiet preparatory manoeuvre.
- (g) Beginners may take this as licence for pointless moves.

**C13. A badly placed piece: use it, move it with a threat, or trade it**
- (b) Before retreating a poor piece, check whether it can serve a different purpose where it stands (a rook on d3 swinging to g3). If it must relocate, do it with a threat, otherwise the opponent uses the time. Plans often include exchanging one's worst piece for the opponent's best one. [READ]
- (c) When the position is not under direct attack.
- (d) None stated.
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 84–87, Lasker–Capablanca).
- (f) A position with one passive piece; learner chooses between retreat, reroute with threat, or trade.
- (g) That the only cure for a bad piece is retreat.

### C. Threats

**C14. Direct and distant threats; real and fictitious threats**
- (b) Direct threats act now (a pawn attacks a piece; a check). Distant threats appear only after several moves and are more dangerous because they are hidden and complex. A chain of direct threats makes the opponent unable to deal with the distant one; the decisive element is usually the distant one (Tarrasch–Janowski, Diagram XXXIX). A fictitious threat is one that would not actually gain anything if carried out, but it still ties the opponent and becomes real the moment he forgets it. Threat-making is not pointless when the opponent can parry: it constrains his pieces, improves yours and delays his plan. He cites an old saying for this: "the threat is stronger than its fulfilment" (Part I Ch. III, Threats). [READ]
- (c) Applies from the opening on; most relevant to the side with the time advantage (C19).
- (d) Beware overvaluing your own fictitious threats; he regards games built on such bluffs as false chess. A piece left en prise can be safe for a while, but the position changes and the same piece can become capturable three moves later. [READ]
- (e) Part I Ch. III *Threats* (pp. 55–60); Part II Ch. I (pp. 97–98).
- (f) *Can I take it?* items where the capture is bad because of a pin or discovered line; *does this move threaten anything?* items.
- (g) That any attacked piece is *threatened*. Learners treat all captures as real threats or all as bluffs.

**C15. A preparatory move can make the opponent's real threat fictitious**
- (b) In a game where he himself was caught, Rubinstein (White) first played a quiet king move that took his king off the diagonal on which a queen check would have won a piece after a central exchange; Znosko-Borovsky (Black) skipped the equivalent precaution, played the central break, and lost a piece (Diagram XL). Before executing a plan of your own, remove the enemy's counterplay first; in Rubinstein–Duras (Diagram LXXV, Ch. IV) he praises a pawn move by White that stops the opponent's freeing break before the planned queen manoeuvre. [READ]
- (c) Most useful in equal positions where both sides have small threats.
- (d) Costs a tempo; only pays when the opponent's threat is genuine and the position lets him execute it.
- (e) Part I Ch. III (pp. 58–59); Part II Ch. IV (pp. 171, 183).
- (f) Positions with a threatening idea for each side; learner chooses prevent vs proceed.
- (g) That prophylaxis is passivity.

### D. Conversion, and the three kinds of superiority

**C16. Converting an advantage between elements; the dangerous moment**
- (b) An advantage rarely grows forever. Time cannot grow much, so cash it in early; space can keep growing; force is kept by not being forced to give it back. The act of changing one kind of advantage into another is where most games are decided. In the long Pillsbury–Tarrasch case (Diagrams XLIX–LIV), Black turned a time-and-space lead into a won pawn by capturing with the bishop that was holding his king-side together; the author says he should instead have taken with the rook, giving up the exchange, because the bishop was the defender he needed. White's plan of opening the g-file to bring a rook into the attack then decided the game. [READ; the variations are the author's and unverified; CHECK]
- (c) When the advantage has stopped growing and a choice has to be made.
- (d) He says choosing the moment is very hard and gives no formula.
- (e) Part II Ch. I (pp. 106–109); Ch. II (pp. 123, 126, 131).
- (f) *Take the pawn?* items where taking removes the key defender.
- (g) *Win material as soon as possible.*

**C17. Crushing advantage: look for the quickest forced win; or simplify**
- (b) With a decisive advantage in all elements, do not wait; look for the most forcing line. If there is no immediate mate, aim to exchange pieces so that the material or positional lead wins in the ending, but only after judging that the ending is actually won: an extra pawn may be insufficient and he refers that judgement to endgame knowledge outside the book. He shows a case where a quiet, merely good move (instead of a forcing one) lets Black generate counter-threats (Rubinstein–Flamberg, Diagram LVI). [READ]
- (c) Large, concrete lead.
- (d) He stops where endgame theory begins.
- (e) Part II Ch. II intro (pp. 110–115).
- (f) Positions with a won game and a tempting slow move.
- (g) Equating *winning* with *no need to calculate*.

**C18. Advantage in force**
- (b) Material is the easiest advantage to establish and the most decisive; the extra pawn also changes how both sides may play: the side ahead can defend more freely and take small risks; the side behind loses attacking options. Counter to the folk view that the side ahead must play more carefully. Give material back only for something tangible. Decide whether the extra material counts for more in the middle game or the ending and steer there. The realisation of material should be a distant aim reached through gains in the other elements. [READ]
- (c) The extra piece or pawn should be active or at least not a liability (a doubled extra pawn needs care, as in Blackburne–Lasker).
- (d) He also says, as a proverb, that winning a pawn *means* winning the game; that is not an engine-era claim (section 5).
- (e) Part II Ch. II (pp. 116–123).
- (f) Material-up positions where the learner picks between simplifying, attacking or consolidating.
- (g) That the player ahead should be passive.

**C19. Advantage in time**
- (b) The advantage is fragile: it vanishes if the opponent can attack the advanced pieces so they must retreat, or if he simply completes development while you have no more good moves. So (1) keep harassing him, (2) do not retreat advanced pieces, support them, (3) prevent his key developing move (castling, bringing out his second knight), (4) convert before it fades. Each move should make a threat, and passive moves aimed only at defence give back the lead. In Tarrasch–Mieses (Diagram LX) White is ahead through two advanced knights; the whole line depends on small facts: the defender's a-pawn still on its home square allows a sacrifice combination, while the b-pawn one step up in the same place refutes it. In Niemzowitsch–Capablanca (Diagrams LXI–LXII) Black uses a time lead by making nearly every move a threat and shuttling pieces through quiet squares. [READ]
- (c) The leader has the more active pieces; the opponent has not yet castled or developed.
- (d) A *quiet* move that merely defends is often correct for the other side; this advice is for the leader.
- (e) Part II Ch. II (pp. 123–130).
- (f) Items where one move is a threat and a similar-looking move is not; the learner picks.
- (g) That a time lead is permanent.

**C20. Advantage in space**
- (b) Unlike time, a space lead does not decay and does not need immediate threats. The task is to hold the advanced posts and take away the opponent's squares quietly, even losing several tempi, because he cannot use his extra moves. The danger point is when he, strangled, gives up a piece or pawn to break free; the leader must watch for that liberating sacrifice. In Lasker–Capablanca (Diagram LXIII) White retreats a knight without threatening anything in order to deny the opponent a freeing pawn advance; in Capablanca–Marshall (Diagram LXIV) White ties down the opponent with a restricting bishop and slowly doubles rooks. [READ]
- (c) The opponent's pieces really have no safe squares and no pawn break.
- (d) He says a space advantage can keep growing without limit; modern readers should qualify this (section 5, item 9).
- (e) Part II Ch. II (pp. 131–141).
- (f) Restraint items where the best move prevents a freeing move.
- (g) That a space advantage requires piece activity everywhere; and that restriction is the same as passivity.

### E. Defence and equal positions

**C21. Inferior positions: diagnose before you defend**
- (b) Identify (1) your chief weakness, (2) the opponent's chief threat, (3) whether his advantage is enough to win or must be increased, (4) whether it will decide in the middle game or the ending. The aim is to restore your own freedom of choice; forced moves signal a critical position. Defend with a long plan rather than parry threats one at a time. If material must be lost, decide in advance which and for what ending: in Schlechter–Lasker (Diagram LXVI) Black gives up two pawns to reach a rook ending he can hold, keeping the pawn that matters. [READ]
- (c) The opponent's lead is real but not crushing.
- (d) He advises not studying enormous disadvantages because resigning is better, which a modern teacher rejects as a rule of practice.
- (e) Part II Ch. III (pp. 142–162).
- (f) Defender's-choice items: which weakness to keep, which to give.
- (g) That defending means making only direct parries.

**C22. Defence against each element**
- (b) Against a space bind: create play on the opposite wing so you are not entirely paralysed. Against a time lead: keep up small threats of your own and protect, so the leader cannot run free. Against a material deficit: choose between passive defence (a draw-minded ending) or counter-attack, e.g. the bishop and extra queen-side pawns against a rook in Capablanca–Mieses (Diagram LXV); against pawn storms on the castled king: keep the pawn barrier on the third rank, keep a rook on the sixth, and a second line of defence on the seventh (Euwe vs Znosko-Borovsky, Diagram LXXI). [READ]
- (c) Appropriate piece placement is available; the first line of defence is intact.
- (d) Seventh-rank barrier works only if the pieces have moved off the seventh rank (he notes a similar scheme failing in an earlier game because it didn't meet the main threat).
- (e) Part II Ch. III; Part III Ch. IV.
- (f) Defender items with one chief threat identified.
- (g) That any defensive plan will do; the point is to address the chief threat.

**C23. Counter-attack: conditions and commitment**
- (b) Counter-attack works when your own defence is complete and the opponent's position has weak points or pieces that allow uninterrupted threats; otherwise the opponent simply carries out his attack one or two moves later. The worst course is a half-hearted third option: neither defending nor attacking decisively (Lasker–Janowski, Diagram XCIV). Changing from passive to active should come at the moment when the opponent tries to demolish the whole defensive system (Capablanca–Znosko-Borovsky, Diagram LXVIII–LXIX). [READ]
- (c) You have tempo-tracking: your threats arrive before his.
- (d) A sacrifice of the exchange or a pawn is often the price (C16).
- (e) Part II Ch. III (pp. 153–156); Part III Ch. IV–V.
- (f) Counter-attack vs defend items, with race counts.
- (g) That the best defence is always attack.

**C24. Equal positions: no position is truly equal; do not force**
- (b) Equal positions have no obvious plan, and the main mistakes are strategic: wrong valuation, then a wrong plan pursued stubbornly, or constantly changing plans. Look for the small differences that decide the plan (C07). If you cannot realise the initiative, prevent his and parry; the result is a draw. If you want to play on, prepare: stop his chances first, place your own pieces, and only then begin. Passive defence is legitimate when his edge is small, but must be persistent. In Riga–Berlin (Diagram LXXIV) Black ignores that White's bishop placement favours White and loses by pressing; in Burn–Znosko-Borovsky (Diagram LXXIII) White presses a wrong idea too stubbornly. [READ]
- (c) Few pieces and no clear target.
- (d) He notes the drawish character of the early-20th-century position game.
- (e) Part II Ch. IV (pp. 163–184).
- (f) *Which side has the initiative?* items; *is it time to attack?* items.
- (g) That the player with the move or the more active-looking piece has the initiative.

### F. Board, pieces, pawns

**C25. Board geometry: centre, lines, rank crossing, possession**
- (b) Central squares touch more of the board, so a piece there exerts more; the edge limits a piece. Ranks and files are more powerful than diagonals (a diagonal piece controls only one colour); the two long diagonals are the strongest diagonals; the lines through the centre are the strongest lines; he claims the second line from the edge is weakest. Crossing the middle line (reaching the fifth rank) raises both activity and danger; sixth and seventh ranks menace the enemy but are costly to support. Holding a square means occupying it or controlling it; there are not enough pieces to occupy all the good squares. Bishops belong on central squares to strike both ways, and to hit the enemy centre a bishop may stand on the edge. [READ]
- (c) Empty-board reasoning; he applies it to the opening (pieces to the centre, king out, centre pawns forward) and to the choice of squares for pieces.
- (d) Squares are valued by counting reachable squares; this is a heuristic. He admits it does not mean every piece should occupy the twelve best squares; four fronts would result.
- (e) Part I Ch. I *Space* (pp. 3–10); Ch. IV.
- (f) *Which square is better for this piece and why?*
- (g) That the centre means only the four central squares; he also speaks of a wider centre.

**C26. The knight**
- (b) The knight controls a ring of squares and no line. It is weaker than the bishop in open positions and stronger in crowded ones because it jumps over pawns. It loses power near the edge and gains from being advanced (it keeps all its squares behind it). A piece that attacks a knight cannot be attacked by it in return, except another knight; therefore a knight attack on a queen, rook or king cannot be answered by capturing the knight with the attacked piece. Because it is weak in value and hard to dislodge, it is the best piece to put on an advanced strong square, ahead of bishop or pawn. [READ]
- (c) The enemy cannot easily challenge the knight with a pawn or an exchange; the board has pawn tension or closed files.
- (d) The strong-square role depends on the square being safe from pawns (he makes this explicit under *hole*, C28). He overstates *weakest piece after the pawn*.
- (e) Part I Ch. I (pp. 19–27); Ch. II (pp. 46–48).
- (f) Which piece can capture the forking knight? Which outpost is truly safe?
- (g) That the knight is erratic; he argues the opposite.

**C27. Bishops, rooks and the opening/closing of lines**
- (b) Rooks and bishops live on lines; if closed, they are reduced toward pawn value. Files are hardest to open (pawns start on them) and need an exchange, a capture or a sacrifice; diagonals are easier. Closing a line is useful when the enemy is stronger, when the line leads to your weak points, or when he has pieces that command lines and you do not. The question to ask before opening is **who benefits**: often one side opens a line and the other profits. As a rule opening lines helps the stronger side or the stronger part of the position. A rook is normally best behind its own pawns, ahead of them only exceptionally (it can be chased by weaker pieces and cannot easily retreat); a bishop in front of the pawns is normal. [READ]
- (c) Pawn tension present, files not yet open.
- (d) *Rook behind pawns* is a rule of thumb for the middlegame he describes; the rook before pawns on a half-open file or the seventh rank is an obvious exception that he allows.
- (e) Part I Ch. II (pp. 41–46).
- (f) *Should I open this file?* pairs where one opens for the opponent's rook.
- (g) That an open file is automatically good for whoever has a rook available.

**C28. Pawns as a barrier; holes**
- (b) Pawns are the static element and defend best side by side on one rank; each advance is irreversible and weakens. The castled-king trio on the second rank guards every square of the third rank; enemy pieces cannot approach nearer than the fourth rank without being hit, and each pawn needs at least two attacking pieces before a threat is serious. A **hole** is a square in front of your pawns that no pawn can any longer cover; advancing the knight-pawn (g-pawn) creates two holes (f3, h3 in modern terms), advancing the bishop-pawn alone opens a diagonal for a bishop, advancing the rook-pawn alone leaves a pawn defended once, and advancing both leaves a hole on the knight-pawn square. The attacker may therefore want you to advance a specific pawn for different reasons (g-pawn: holes; rook-pawn: a target; bishop-pawn: opens a diagonal if he has the right bishop). Doubled pawns do not protect one another and, by his reckoning, count as little more than one pawn; isolated pawns lose all pawn protection. [READ]
- (c) A king behind the pawns; opposing pieces able to use the holes (a bishop of the right colour, a knight, a queen).
- (d) He allows a bishop on g2 or a knight on g2 to guard the holes; and he allows that doubling can sometimes be undone by a later move. His claim that doubled pawns are often worth about one pawn is overstated (section 5).
- (e) Part I Ch. II *Pawns* (pp. 28–38).
- (f) Castled-structure items: given a pawn advance, count holes, find the exploiting piece.
- (g) That a pawn advance is *safe* when no piece attacks the pawn right now.

**C29. Passed pawns, and what to do with them**
- (b) A pawn with no enemy pawn to stop it is strong, especially in the centre where it splits the opponent's deployment; a rook behind it grows stronger as the pawn advances. The owner must see how far he can push without losing it. The opponent should blockade with a piece that also hits it from the side (a knight or bishop works better than a pure blocker), and try to pin the pawn to the square where it is easiest to attack and hardest to defend. When the pawn is too well protected to win, still pin it and attack it, to draw his pieces to its defence. [READ]
- (c) Pieces available on both sides; not a bare ending.
- (d) A passed pawn that is isolated may be a weakness, not a strength; in the centre its strength outweighs that.
- (e) Part I Ch. II (pp. 37–38); Part II Ch. I (Diagram XLV).
- (f) Passed-pawn plan items.
- (g) That a passed pawn must advance.

**C30. Pawns as attackers**
- (b) The pawn is the weakest piece yet the most threatening: every other piece must retreat from it. But its short reach means it should advance in an attack only when the enemy camp is already weakened or the weakening and the collapse coincide. Pawns usually storm in order to open lines, often by being sacrificed; the pieces they attack in front of the target should be few. Once the screen is cleared, the pawn either occupies a hole or attacks a weak pawn; even a sound pawn can be attacked if exchanging opens a file or advancing leaves it weak. Against a castled king, the pawn attack succeeds only if the opposing pawn barrier on the third rank can be broken. [READ]
- (c) Support from pieces; a clear target.
- (d) Opening files yourself is bad for the defender (a comment in Tarrasch–Schlechter, Diagram LXXXVIII).
- (e) Part I Ch. II (pp. 34–35); Part II Ch. III (p. 160); Part III Ch. III items 6–8.
- (f) Pawn-storm pairs: storm with piece support vs pawns alone.
- (g) That a pawn storm is always committed.

**C31. Queen and king**
- (b) The queen belongs in the centre in the middlegame and should not be held back like a rook, but should not come out early because nothing worth hitting exists and any weaker piece can chase her. She needs open space; on a crowded board she may wait on the flank and make threats from a distance. The king is not an active middlegame piece: get him to the flank, do not let him obstruct the other pieces and do not keep pieces tied to his defence unless he is in danger. His presence on a wing makes an attack on that wing worth sacrifices, so the attacker will go further than he would against a pawn-only target. Short castling is the ordinary choice and gets the king safe fastest; long castling brings the rook to the centre but needs more moves and suits special cases such as attacking the opponent's king-side; an attack on a long-castled king aims at the undefended rook pawn and the shattered third rank (Breyer–Mieses, Diagram XCI). [READ]
- (c) Middlegame with queens on the board.
- (d) Dated in places (section 5, items 7 and 8). The king-activity statement is one he qualifies for endings.
- (e) Part I Ch. II *King* (pp. 38–41), *Queen* (pp. 48–49); Part III Ch. III item 11.
- (f) Choose-your-castling items; queen-development items.
- (g) That king safety means moving away from all play; he says defenders should also be active.

**C32. Weak squares versus weak points; counting attackers and defenders**
- (b) A square's strength on the empty board (C25) is the same for both players; a **weak point** created by the pieces and pawns is the opposite: weak for one is a source of strength for the other. Also distinguish whether a piece is weak because of the square it stands on or because of the particular position. In a king attack count: attackers needed vs defenders available; if both sides have exactly enough, moving any defender away tips the balance and the attacker needs an extra piece, which is what opening the g-file to bring a rook is for (Pillsbury–Tarrasch). [READ]
- (c) A concrete king-side with identifiable attackers.
- (d) Counting is only as good as the identification of which defenders can really be removed.
- (e) Part I Ch. II (p. 38); Part II Ch. I (pp. 92–96).
- (f) Attacker/defender count items.
- (g) That *weak* is a fixed property of a square.

**C33. Attack types (Part III Ch. III) with conditions he states**
- (b) Eleven brief cases: *centre attack* aimed at the king's weak point (f7 in the example) when the king is on the flank and the enemy pieces are crowded on the second and third ranks; *centre plus wing*; *king in the centre* (target the pawn that shelters him, stop castling, open files, centralise a rook on the open file); *both wings*; *king-side castled* (weak e- and f-pawns in front of the king, a plan of advancing the opponent's own e- and f-pawns to open the f-file; a long struggle for open files); *pawn sacrifice to open files* (the aim is to force the enemy to advance a pawn and create a hole); *breaking up the pawn position* (disordered pawns plus knights in front of the pawns keep threats flowing); *pawn storm* (the king is covered by a bishop; the storm tries to dislodge it by pushing the rook-pawn at the knight-pawn first and the bishop-pawn after); *bishop attack* (a double-bishop sacrifice on h7 and g7, Lasker–Bauer, Diagram LXXXIX, a relative of the Greek-gift family [OUTSIDE identification, CHECK]); *knight attack* (weak knight-pawn and rook-pawn squares and a key square near the king; a second knight brings a threat to win the exchange); *queen-side castled king* (undefended rook pawn, escape squares, the third rank). [READ]
- (c) Each short example is chosen to show a typical feature, and he leaves it to the reader to rebuild the evaluation.
- (d) Annotations are brief; most are one-line. Few conditions are stated beyond what is above.
- (e) Part III Ch. III (pp. 192–206).
- (f) Position families for attack nodes.
- (g) That each type is a rule; each is a pattern with a precondition.

**C34. Defence and counter-battle types (Part III Ch. IV–V)**
- (b) Four defence cases and five counter-battle cases. Defensive items stress choosing the right moment to turn from defence to attack and the cost of indecision; the counter-battle chapter is about two simultaneous attacks, where speed (counting) and sacrifices of the exchange or a pawn decide, and where one side's mistake is believing his own attack is faster. [READ]
- (c) Both sides have real weaknesses.
- (d) Light commentary; the diagrams are the point.
- (e) Part III Ch. IV–V (pp. 207–222).
- (f) Race items.
- (g) That the attacker always has the initiative.

## 5. Claims that are dated, absolute, or contestable

The rhetoric of the whole book is absolute: the opening of Part I says chess develops according to rigid laws that admit of no exception, like mathematics or mechanics, and that mistakes are punished by those laws; he repeatedly appeals to objective facts. This conflicts with CD-4 (conditions, not rules) and should not be copied. Specific points for an engine-era reader:

1. **Numeric piece *force* values** (Part I Ch. I): he assigns a pawn about 3.5 units, a king 8, a knight about 8 (about 2.3 pawns), a bishop 10 (just under 3 pawns), a rook 14 (4 pawns), a queen 27 (about 8 pawns, and he says it is worth about two rooks). These come from counting reachable squares and are not the standard 1/3/3/5/9 [OUTSIDE]. Do not use. Reasoning: reachable squares ignores piece interaction, structure, pawn promotion, and the value of coordination.
2. **Square-value belts** (numbers like 27, 25, 23, 21 for ranks/files/diagonals; extra belts when knight moves are added). Neat but not predictive. Use only the qualitative statement that central squares are more useful. [CHECK if any such claim is turned into an item: engine piece-square tables show a different pattern, and the knight values at the rim are much lower than the bishop's.]
3. **Diagonals are always weaker than ranks and files.** Reasoned from half-board access. Contestable: bishops are strong, especially in pairs and in open positions; the claim confuses *controls fewer squares* with *less useful*. [OUTSIDE]
4. **The knight is the weakest piece after the pawn; the bishop is stronger.** Both pieces are normally treated as equal in value, with context deciding. His point that the knight is better in crowded positions and worse in open ones is the useful form.
5. **Rook before pawns is exceptional; the second line from the edge is weakest.** Both overgeneralised. Rook lifts and rooks on half-open files in front of own pawns are common [OUTSIDE]; the *weakest line* claim is an artefact of the counting scheme.
6. **Doubled pawns are often equivalent to losing a pawn; pawns defend best on one rank; any pawn advance weakens.** The truth is conditional on structure, open files, bishop pair, and king safety [OUTSIDE]. Teach as a contrast family, not as a rule.
7. **The king is outside the game until the ending; short castling lacks individuality (his term, paraphrased); long castling costs extra moves.** Dated. Modern play castles either way freely and endgame-like middlegames often use the king actively (his own Schlechter–Lasker example moves the king toward the queen-side in a middlegame). [OUTSIDE]
8. **Pawns other than the centre ones should stay home in the opening; the f-pawn should not move; the Ruy Lopez is best because the centre fight lasts longest.** Typical of 1900-1920 doctrine; hypermodern and later approaches fight for the centre with pieces and flank pawns. [OUTSIDE]
9. **An advantage in time is transient; space lasts and *can develop indefinitely*.** A useful contrast, but both are overstated. Space can be undermined by pawn breaks and counter-sacrifices (he admits the liberating sacrifice), and a time lead can persist as initiative. [OUTSIDE]
10. **Winning a pawn means winning the game; the side ahead need not be careful.** Pre-engine heuristic. A pawn plus in the middlegame is typically worth much less than a win; engines often evaluate such positions near equal when compensation exists. [CHECK with engine on any *extra pawn* item]
11. **Do not study cases of overwhelming disadvantage because resigning is better.** Practical advice contradicts modern coaching of resilience and swindles. Not adoptable.
12. **Tempo counting as a measure of time.** The count treats every move as equal; captures, checks and tempo-gaining threats complicate it. He himself discards counts in several examples. Use as an illustration of development only. [CHECK any item that uses it]
13. **Plan-racing arithmetic** (my C11): ignores intermediate checks and forcing replies; each claimed *X needs seven moves, Y needs five* is a hindsight reconstruction. [CHECK]
14. **Authorial analysis of the example games.** The *forced* wins, the criticisms of moves (*should have taken with the rook*) and the claim that the sacrifice would be sound if the bishop were kept (Pillsbury–Tarrasch) are 1920s-level analysis, un-engined. Treat as hypotheses. [CHECK all]
15. **Selection bias.** Examples are chosen to show the book's categories and almost all end with a win by the side he is studying; few counterexamples where the *correct* plan fails. Also the *characters* are identified after the result is known (hindsight). [I]
16. **No safety routine.** There is no *check what your opponent's last move threatens* habit beyond the threat discussion; no candidate-move or blunder-check procedure. For a Tier 1–3 curriculum this is a gap to be filled from other sources, not by this book. [I]
17. **Attribution.** He credits Alapin for the time/space interplay in the French (Part I Ch. III) and repeats a maxim that one should not hurry to convert positional into material advantage; I did not verify the first, and the second is an unattributed maxim. [OUTSIDE/CHECK]

## 6. Teaching method observed

- **First principles, then examples.** About a third of the book is the *empty board and the pieces* before any real position appears. He insists the reader not skip it (preface). This is deductive and, in the knight argument, speculative. [READ]
- **A fixed three-step valuation** (formal count → piece-by-piece inspection → character), repeated, with a long worked example. A reader can imitate the procedure even if the numbers are poor.
- **Counterexamples where the easy method fails.** Positions are given where the count says *ahead* but the player is lost (Diagrams XXXVIII, XLIII, XLIV). This is exactly the *familiar answer is not best* idea in CD-2 and the contrast approach in CD-4. [READ]
- **One-feature-change thought experiments.** Remove Black's extra pawn and see what changes (Blackburne–Lasker, Diagram LVIII); put the king on b1 instead of g1 (Diagram XXXVIII); put Black's queen-side pawn on the b-file instead of the a-file in Tarrasch–Mieses (Diagram LX); take the same bishop on f2 or g3 in Riga–Berlin. This is the closest thing to a contrasting-cases method in any of the sources I know, and the most reusable device. [READ]
- **Classification as structure.** Superior/inferior/equal and, inside each, force/time/space. The classification is the book's pedagogic scaffold.
- **Questions put to the reader.** For example, after counting tempi in the Lasker–Capablanca position he tells the student to notice an oddity and find its cause for himself. Part III intentionally withholds commentary and says the examples are exercises on the previous parts: the reader is expected to value the position, state the plan, the direction, the tempo and the change of threats unaided. [READ]
- **Repeated summary lists** after examples (e.g., the methods by which superior positions are kept and increased: open lines, diagonals, weak points, tying down and driving pieces to bad squares, mixing threat types).
- **Long worked sequences with no board** (up to 17 pages for one position, per the preface). High load for the novice; the missing diagrams in this copy make this worse for us.
- **No exercises with answers, no spaced retrieval, no difficulty grading.** [I]
- **Voice.** Earnest, expert, rhetorical; uses mathematical analogies, a military vocabulary (storm troops, fronts) and occasional humour (the false-move story).

## 8. Candidate exercise & contrast-family ideas

Cue levels: **1** = full cue (concept named, pointer given); **2** = relation prompt (which squares/pieces to look at); **3** = category cue only (e.g. *plan, not tactic*); **4** = unlabeled. This is my reading of the map's depths and CD-10's hint ladder, flagged in section 10. No FENs: positions must be reconstructed from outside records and checked.

**Exercise ideas**

1. *Three-step valuation (formal count / audit / difference).* Position type: quiet middlegame, all minor pieces out, no immediate tactics. Learner action: fill a three-line template (material and space and development; which pieces are out of play; the single biggest difference), then choose a plan class (attack, defend, manoeuvre). Cue 2→4. Verify plan class with engine; accept any move set with the same plan class (CD-8).
2. *Same count, different truth.* Pair of positions where move counts say *ahead* but one side's moves were wasted (rook-pawn pushes, knight shuffles) versus a pair where the lead is real. Learner marks which tempi were useful, then states who is ahead. Cue 3.
3. *Remove the pawn.* Take a position where an extra pawn dictates both sides' options (Blackburne–Lasker type); ask what changes if the pawn is removed. Learner explains in a sentence which plans become available to whom. Cue 3–4.
4. *Move the king.* Same position twice with the king on the long-castled square versus the short-castled one; a rook sacrifice either works or hangs. Learner predicts which. Cue 3–4. Verify by engine.
5. *Real or fictitious?* Opponent's last move attacks a pawn or piece that looks capturable. Learner decides whether capturing loses because of a pin, check or discovered line, and says what the threat was. Include no-threat items (move is harmless). Cue 2→4.
6. *Disarm it first.* Two-move choice: execute your plan now, or first play a quiet move that removes the opponent's one counter-threat; the engine shows the plan fails without the preparation. Cue 3–4.
7. *Cash in or keep?* Time-advantage position where winning a pawn removes the best defender versus a position where winning it is safe. Learner chooses capture, quiet threat or exchange. Cue 3–4.
8. *Quiet restriction.* Space-advantage position where the best move prevents the freeing pawn break and makes no threat; failing look-alike offers a showy threat that frees the opponent. Learner names what the opponent wants to play. Cue 3.
9. *Count the holes.* Castled king with one or two advanced pawns; learner lists squares the pawns can no longer guard and names which enemy piece could use each. Include a version with a guarding bishop or knight that fixes the hole. Cue 1→3.
10. *Attackers versus defenders.* Castled-king position; learner counts attackers who can reach and defenders who can answer, then picks the plan *bring one more piece* (open a file) or *exchange a defender*. Cue 2→4.
11. *Which piece can capture the forking knight?* Knight fork on a queen, rook or king; learner finds the only legal ways out (move, block not possible, capture only by a knight or by a different piece). This is the fact that a knight's attack cannot be returned except by a knight. Cue 1→2.
12. *Open the file for whom?* Position where a pawn exchange opens a file; learner decides whether it helps their rook, the opponent's, or neither. Pairs differ in who can reach the file first. Cue 3.
13. *Reroute, retreat or trade.* One badly placed piece; learner chooses between retreating, rerouting with a threat, or trading it for the opponent's best piece, with reasons. Cue 3.
14. *Chief threat, chief weakness.* Defender's position; learner writes (a) the opponent's main threat in one sentence and (b) own greatest weakness, then picks a move that addresses both. Cue 3–4. Plan-class scoring.
15. *Race.* Two flank plans (pawn majority queen-side versus king-side attack). Learner counts moves needed by each and picks counter-attack or defence; engine confirms whether the count held. Cue 2→4.
16. *Seventh-rank barrier.* Defender with a sixth-rank barrier about to be broken; learner prepares the next line of defence. Failing look-alike: rooks remain on the seventh and the barrier is pointless. Cue 3.
17. *Equal but not identical.* Two near-symmetrical positions differing by one bishop square or one pawn; learner says who may take the initiative and who must wait; includes a version where pressing loses. Cue 3–4.
18. *Passed pawn: push, blockade or ignore.* Learner picks blocker piece (knight vs bishop vs rook) and square, justified by the sideways attacks the blocker retains. Cue 2→4.

**Contrast families (working case / failing look-alike / irrelevant case)**

A. *Development lead.* Working: opponent's king is in the centre and two minors are undeveloped; each of your moves can carry a threat. Failing look-alike: you have made more moves but they were rim-pawn pushes and the opponent's king is safe. Irrelevant: a closed pawn-locked position where neither side can open lines for many moves.
B. *Cashing a time advantage.* Working: a combination converts the lead into material while the key defender stays. Failing: the capture removes your best defender and gives him the attack. Irrelevant: pure endgame.
C. *Space lead.* Working: opponent has no safe squares and no pawn break; quiet restriction wins. Failing: the same space but a freeing pawn lever exists. Irrelevant: opened position where space does not matter.
D. *Hole in the castled structure.* Working: the g-pawn has moved, the attacker has a bishop of the right colour. Failing: same pawn move but the defender's bishop or knight covers both squares. Irrelevant: the defender has no king there (long-castled other side).
E. *Knight outpost.* Working: square protected by a pawn and not attackable by any enemy pawn. Failing: the enemy can play a pawn lever to expel it. Irrelevant: no useful targets from the square.
F. *Pawn storm.* Working: defenders are few, the storm is supported by pieces, the barrier on the third rank can be broken. Failing: the same pawn moves with the barrier intact and the defender's pieces near. Irrelevant: opposite wing play.
G. *Opening a file.* Working: your rook can reach the file first and its entry square is safe. Failing: the opponent's rook arrives first. Irrelevant: the file leads only to a blocked pawn.
H. *Counter-attack.* Working: defence complete and your threats arrive first. Failing: attack is one move slower, or leaves your king bare. Irrelevant: a purely defensive endgame.
I. *Doubled pawns.* Working: they cannot be repaired and the opponent controls the key square. Failing: they open a file, give the bishop pair, or control the centre. Irrelevant: they stand far from any play.
J. *Equal position, initiative.* Working: the small difference (bishop on the open diagonal, pawn lever available) licenses play. Failing: the same difference favours the other side, so pressing worsens the position. Irrelevant: the position is simplified to a dead-drawn ending.

## 9. Agreements and tensions with the other sources in this batch, and with modern teaching

I only list points where I actually know the other works; none is verified against their sheets, so the reader of those sheets should reconcile. All [OUTSIDE] unless stated.

- **Capablanca, *Chess Fundamentals* (1921).** Znosko-Borovsky says he drew on Capablanca's writings and on a Capablanca lecture about a theory of the middle game (preface) [READ], and the Bell advertisement lists Fundamentals beside this book [READ]. Agreement [OUTSIDE, recalled]: plan follows from the position's weaknesses; centre matters; simple, economical explanation of material values (Capablanca uses the ordinary scale, so Z-B's numeric scheme conflicts with it). Tension: Capablanca teaches by short principles and clean examples; Z-B by long derivation. Capablanca is a player in roughly nine of Z-B's example games (by my rough count from the list of positions), so the book is partly a book about Capablanca's play [READ].
- **Nimzowitsch, *My System*.** Same publisher family [READ, ad]. Overlaps [OUTSIDE, recalled]: open files and the seventh rank; blockading a passed pawn with a piece that also attacks it; restraining the opponent by taking squares rather than making threats (C20); a hint of prophylaxis (C15). Tension: Nimzowitsch gives pawn-chain, overprotection and restraint as named, central concepts and disclaims universal laws; Z-B does not name them and speaks of laws without exceptions. Nimzowitsch is also a player in Z-B's examples (as Niemzovitch) [READ].
- **Em. Lasker, *Common Sense in Chess*.** I believe the lectures treat time and space as values and discuss the nature of the struggle in plain language, but I cannot confirm that the vocabulary (force, space, time) matches Z-B's. [OUTSIDE, uncertain.] Lasker the player appears in roughly ten of Z-B's example games and Tarrasch in roughly eleven (rough counts from the list of positions) [READ], so the book often illustrates Lasker's practice rather than his theory.
- **Ed. Lasker, *Chess Strategy*.** [OUTSIDE, partly recalled] It is a principle-based guide to practical play with attention to pawn structure and weak squares, closer in spirit to Z-B's pawn and hole discussion than to his element theory. Listed in the same series [READ].
- **Silman and modern imbalance teaching.** Z-B's difference method (C07) is an early form of *compare the imbalances, then plan*; unlike Silman's list it does not name categories (minor pieces, pawn structure, files, space) [OUTSIDE].
- **Kotov, Aagaard, Dvoretsky.** The book has no candidate-move list, no tree, no *opponent's best reply* routine beyond threat awareness [OUTSIDE]; it teaches evaluation, not calculation. Dvoretsky's prophylaxis resembles C15 but is more systematic.
- **Watson / rule-independence.** Strong tension with the *rigid laws* stance; Watson-style exceptions are exactly what contrast families are for [OUTSIDE].
- **Modern engines and dynamic play.** Tempo and initiative are accepted ideas; the numeric forms, the *no exception* claim, king-never-active, pawn-up-means-won and the diagonal claim are not. Engines also give much more weight to piece activity, king safety and initiative than to counting moves [OUTSIDE].
- **Rowson / Heisman (process).** Z-B has no blunder-check or *what does his move threaten?* routine; the closest are the threat sections. [OUTSIDE]

## 10. Open questions

1. **Cue definition.** I assumed cue 1 = fully cued and cue 4 = unlabeled. CD-13 mentions *performance at cue 3–4* for test-out, and CD-10 gives a hint ladder; which direction is the numbering? Please confirm before any exercise carries a cue field.
2. **Which edition?** Our file is the 1930 reprint. Is the 1922 first printing available for a text comparison? Does anything in our use depend on it?
3. **Use of the elements vocabulary.** Do we adopt force/space/time as a Tier 3–4 lens, or only the three-step valuation and difference method without the vocabulary? My lean: use the valuation and differences, drop the elements as a theory.
4. **Position reconstruction.** Diagrams are missing. Which game scores can we lawfully use, and who does the position-by-position identification, followed by engine verification? A first target list is in section 8.
5. **Engine tests of his rules.** Candidates for a small verification study (with a Lichess-sourced sample, if permitted): doubled pawns vs material, rook-pawn pushes as lost tempi, the pawn-up value in the middlegame, fianchetto holes. Are these worth the effort or just stated as conditions?
6. **Where does the planning material live?** The map has no explicit planning strand node set beyond `POS.*`. Are `POS.PLAN.DIFF`, `POS.PLAN.MODE`, `POS.PLAN.RACE`, `POS.CONVERT`, `POS.TEMPO`, `DEF.DIAGNOSE` acceptable new ids, or should they fold into `POS.IMBALANCES` and `LOOP.V4`?
7. **Tier of this book as a reading.** At what tier should a learner meet any of Z-B's long worked analysis? My estimate is T4+, with only excerpted ideas (real/fictitious threats, hole, knight fact) earlier.
8. **Passivity risk.** Does his advice for inferior positions (avoid activity, don't open files) conflict with our CD-5/CD-11 stance on initiative and counterplay? How to teach *defend actively* without adopting his doctrine.
9. **Ownership of examples.** Four of his examples are his own games; the same rights logic applies to moves, but should we avoid featuring the author's own games as authorities?
10. **What I could not decide:** whether the quiet-retreat and *no threats needed* claims for space advantage (C20) hold in engine analysis for the specific Lasker–Capablanca and Capablanca–Marshall positions; whether the Pillsbury–Tarrasch capture error is real; and the identification of the Lasker–Bauer game as a Greek-gift family case. All need engine verification.
