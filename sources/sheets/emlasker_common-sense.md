## 2. One-paragraph portrait

[READ] A lecture course by the reigning world champion (he refers to his match with Steinitz) for **club players who already know the rules, the history and the notation**. Its stated aim is to treat *every* phase of the game, opening, attack, defence, endgame, by a small set of general principles derived from the nature of chess as a **fight between two minds**; the Preface hopes the principles could be unified into one master principle (the germ of a theory of any fight), but Lasker explicitly declines to state it. The method is: a principle (often put in military or boxing terms), then model games and positions adapted to the purpose, then commentary that points at the moment a principle was kept or broken; refutations of the wrong way are shown on purpose; exhaustive variation trees are rejected in favour of leading lines plus principle. The voice is a confident, wry, occasionally self-correcting lecturer (he says he speaks authoritatively and cannot yet prove the point), who asks the reader to be sceptical and to work. About half of the book is 1895-era opening analysis (Ruy Lopez, Evans and King's Bishop's gambits, French) that is **largely obsolete as theory but valuable as an example of reasoning from principles to a verdict**; the durable core is Lectures 1, 7-8, 9 and 10-12.

## 4. Core ideas

Format per idea: (a) name; (b) idea in my words; (c) CONDITIONS; (d) EXCEPTIONS/limits; (e) where; (f) example type; (g) likely misconception. Tags on statements about the book are [READ] unless otherwise shown.

### 4.1 Chess as a fight with economy of force and time
- **(a)** Chess as struggle; economy of force and of time.
- **(b)** Lasker rejects "mere game", "science", "art", and calls chess a fight in which intellect decides. Both sides begin with equal force and a known enemy, so the first step mirrors a general's: mobilise. In Lecture 7-8 he generalises: whenever you attack, three things decide *whether* and *how*: the proportion of your attacking force to what directly opposes it; the nature of the terrain (what shields or exposes the enemy); and how your attacking force relates to the rest of your army. The third factor sets the *tempo* of the attack: if enemy reserves must be prevented from arriving you economise in **time** (strike fast); otherwise you may economise in **force** and advance step by step.
- **(c)** The time-vs-force choice presupposes that both resources exist as variables: you can trade material for tempo (gambits, Morphy-style pawn sacrifices) or spend tempi to keep material.
- **(d)** Lasker states that the unified principle behind all this is deliberately not formulated; the trade-off is described qualitatively, with no exchange rate. [READ] The tidy military analogy hides chess-specific facts (equal armies, perfect information, forced replies).
- **(e)** Preface; Lecture 1 opening paragraphs; Lecture 7-8 opening.
- **(f)** Narrative/analogy plus a quick-kill game and a slow-pressure game.
- **(g)** "A sacrificed pawn is a lost pawn" (treating material as the only currency), or its opposite, "tempo is always worth more than a pawn". Lasker's point is that it depends on the factors above. A second misconception: reading the military analogy as saying chess is *only* force.

### 4.2 Mobilise first (development as the first task)
- **(a)** Development / seizing open lines and points.
- **(b)** Before anything else, bring your pieces into play and onto lines that matter. Neglect gives the opponent time to strike a vital point and end the game quickly. Lasker puts the process at no more than about six moves (his promise, to be borne out later).
- **(c)** Applies to the opening phase when nothing concrete forces a different move; his model games show punishments for neglecting it: moves that do not add force to any piece, bringing the queen out for a quick grab, preferring a pawn move that does not develop.
- **(d)** The six-move figure is a rule of thumb, stated as a general tendency. [READ] He immediately collects exceptions in later lectures (see 4.5). The statement as printed rests on a missing page.
- **(e)** Lecture 1; reapplied in 3, 4, 5, 6.
- **(f)** Short games where a developed side mates a neglected side in a handful of moves; positions "adapted" to show a choice between a developing move and a non-developing one.
- **(g)** "Develop fast" turned into "make as many different piece moves as possible" or "never move a pawn"; ignoring whether the developed piece stands on a *useful line*.

### 4.3 Order and purposefulness of development moves
- **(a)** What to develop, in which order, and what not to do.
- **(b)** From the surviving applications: knights before bishops; centre pawns and knights early; every move should add to the working power of some piece; avoid rook-pawn moves out of fear of an attack that is not yet real; avoid taking pieces out only to chase a pawn; do not castle earlier than needed if the queenside pieces are still undeveloped; in at least one structure, trade pawns first and place the bishop after.
- **(c)** The "knights before bishops" and "pawn exchange first" advice is tied to the particular structures shown, where knight squares are clearer to judge than bishop squares. [READ] The text does not state the full condition set (rule text missing).
- **(d)** Lasker himself marks exceptions (4.5), and in Lecture 3 uses a developing-but-unusual pawn move to prepare the bishop. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 1 models; Lecture 6's "amendment" about the queen-bishop pawn.
- **(f)** Move-choice items in opening positions; "what was the first inaccuracy?" in a short lost game.
- **(g)** Treating "knights before bishops" as a law of nature rather than a default. Also: believing that any move that avoids a threat is a "defence" and so justified, when it merely loses time.

### 4.4 Gambits and pawn sacrifices for development
- **(a)** Pawn-for-time trades; judging whether a gambit is justified.
- **(b)** A gambit invests a pawn in facilities for development and open lines. It is justified only if the investment buys something durable. Lasker asks, of the King's Bishop's Gambit, by what right White gives up a pawn in an otherwise equal position, opens his own king's side and leaves a queen check unanswered. He concludes the gambit is unsound and that the only reason for a gambit would be to tempt the opponent into a too-hasty attack. "Sound and consistent play, he says, should beat trickery.
- **(c)** The defender's reply is shown to work *because* the defender develops with tempo and keeps his own king safe. In the Evans, White's centre plus open lines is the compensation, and Lasker judges the old "normal position" favourable to White even a pawn down.
- **(d)** In two places Lasker declines to rule out sound gambits: the Evans he treats as a respectable opening; he also admits brilliant but unsound games (the Immortal) were singled out with special names by players of the time. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 1 (the f4 gambit game with the remark that the pawn is invested in development); Lecture 4; Lecture 5.
- **(f)** Gambit-acceptance decisions: grab vs develop; "does the compensation survive the first accurate reply?"
- **(g)** "Gambits are unsound; always take the pawn" or "attacking play always justifies a sacrifice". Lasker's actual position is verdict-by-compensation, not slogan.

### 4.5 The development rule has exceptions (the game acquires a character)
- **(a)** Exceptions to the develop-quickly default.
- **(b)** In the Ruy Lopez line of Lecture 3, a quiet pawn move (strengthening the centre / preserving a bishop) departs from quick development because the game has already acquired a character of its own that raises the value of some pieces over others; the bishop becomes the backbone of a coming attack, so it is protected rather than rushed. In Lecture 6 he adds an amendment for closed ("close") games: do not obstruct the queen-bishop pawn with the queen's knight unless you intend to open the game at once with the king-pawn advance, and push that pawn to its fourth square early. He also records a principle he attributes to Steinitz: when you advance a pawn to the fifth rank in the centre, back it up with the bishop-pawn quickly.
- **(c)** Conditions are stated informally: a *settled structure* with known targets; a piece that has a role in a coming attack; a pawn that needs support when advanced.
- **(d)** Because Lasker often calls moves contrary to the rules and then lets them stand, the reader can misjudge; the exceptions are not systematized. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 3 (around the c3 idea); Lecture 6 end.
- **(f)** Contrast pairs: same position type, one with a settled structure where the quiet move is right, one early position where it just wastes time.
- **(g)** Learning the rule as absolute (and then being unable to find the "ugly" good move), or abandoning the rule after seeing one exception.

### 4.6 Exchange policy and bishop vs knight in the opening
- **(a)** When to trade a bishop for a knight early; when pawn structure damage is (not) a reason.
- **(b)** General rule: do not trade the long-range bishop for a knight early in the game, as the knight's power is limited to a circle. Voluntarily trading a bishop for a **pinned** knight, especially to win a pawn greedily, is called a mistake. In Lecture 7-8, a bishop-for-knight trade that doubled the opponent's pawns is criticised because it gave the opponent open files and development, and Lasker calls talk of doubled or isolated pawns as endgame weaknesses, early in the game, a chimera.
- **(c)** The criticised trades hand the opponent open lines/development and cost the trader tempi or space; where the structural damage buys the trader nothing visible, the damage is rated as non-compensating.
- **(d)** He states the pinned-knight trade is always a mistake; his own deferred note (Preface) says exchange questions need very fine differentiation and are postponed to a future work, so the rule is knowingly incomplete. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 3 (bishop retreat after the rook-pawn move), Lecture 6 (greedy pawn grab game), Lecture 7-8 (Anderssen vs Steinitz commentary).
- **(f)** Exchange decision items with an open vs a closed centre.
- **(g)** Memorising "never trade bishop for knight" (or "bishop pair always wins"); the sheet's job is to keep conditions (what the trade buys or concedes).

### 4.7 Pawns that work vs pawns that merely need protection
- **(a)** Pawn activity as a measure of the better position.
- **(b)** In the Lecture 3 analysis Lasker explains why White's position is superior: Black's queenside pawns do nothing and only need protection, while White's pawns are either active or are potentials of future action. Related: a pawn move without a clearly defined purpose deserves blame (Hastings game, Lecture 10-12): it took a good square from a knight and left a strong point for enemy knights; and opening files for the enemy rook helps the enemy.
- **(c)** The comparison is made after development is complete and there is a target.
- **(d)** In modern engine-era play a damaged queenside can be compensated by piece activity (see Section 5). [OUTSIDE]
- **(e)** Lecture 3 (twice); Lecture 10-12 (Hastings game commentary).
- **(f)** Annotate-the-plans exercises: which side's pawns "work"? Which pawn move had no purpose?
- **(g)** Equating "more advanced pawns" with "better".

### 4.8 Attack needs tangible superiority (and what attack is)
- **(a)** Superiority precondition; attack as removing obstructions.
- **(b)** The model games are summed up in two rules. Rule (i): do not attack unless you have a tangible superiority, either in the working strength of your pieces or in their reach; corollary: if you do, the reaction puts your army in a critical state and the unavoidable counterattack finds you disordered. Rule (ii): the first aim of an attack is to create strong points as near the opponent's camp as possible and occupy them with pieces that have large fields of action; corollary: try to force the opponent's pawns to advance on the wing where you attack. He also defines **attack** broadly as any process that removes obstructions (like any fight), the ultimate aim being to capture the king.
- **(c)** Attack when you are better developed, have more force aimed at the weak area, and can see a concrete target. A very good opponent rarely offers the conditions for a quick attack.
- **(d)** He gives no numerical measure of "tangible superiority" and the examples are all hindsight-clear. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8, after the model games; Lecture 9 (the defender's view of premature attacks).
- **(f)** Go/no-go decisions: attack, keep developing, or defend; punish-the-premature-attack items.
- **(g)** "Whoever has the initiative should attack" or "attack because it is my move". The rule is a condition check, not a style.

### 4.9 Weakness, weak point, strong point; obstruction
- **(a)** Weaknesses and strong points (Lasker's coined terms); obstructions.
- **(b)** A **weakness** is a piece or group of pieces whose defensive strength is deficient relative to its importance (a queen with little range; a pawn that can neither advance nor be defended by pawns). A **weak point** is a square, possibly empty, that only heavy pieces can attack, so pawns, knights, bishops (and sometimes protected rooks) are safe there. What is a weak point for the opponent is a **strong point** for you; occupying one with a piece of large reach often half decides the battle. **Obstructions** are pieces or pawns of low importance that block lines of action. Enemy obstructions are easier to remove (many attackers can threaten them) than your own blocked pawn; an isolated blocked pawn of your own is typically removed by placing a piece under its protection and forcing an exchange.
- **(c)** The strong-point idea requires that the enemy have no pawn or minor piece able to challenge the square.
- **(d)** Obstruction also helps the *attacker* (Morphy's pawn sacrifice delays the enemy king from castling; a queen can blockade an enemy pawn, a rare case, he says, where a heavy piece obstructs). [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8; reapplied in Lecture 9 (reverse the rules) and the endgame lectures.
- **(f)** Square-colouring tasks ("which squares can no enemy pawn or minor piece contest?"); "name the obstruction" tasks.
- **(g)** Calling any isolated or doubled pawn a weakness; or any hole a strong point whatever pieces are left.

### 4.10 Pace of the attack
- **(a)** Quick vs moderate attack.
- **(b)** Large superiority in an area where the opponent has real weaknesses (king or queen badly placed) calls for quick, forcing play in which each move does much and reserves are brought up with tempo while the enemy's reserves are kept out by obstructions. Variations are then few, because the defence has forced replies, and are analysable. When superiority is not clearly defined, attack at a moderate pace, advancing on strong points and methodically creating new ones near the defence; the plan matters most and time is secondary. The less pronounced the advantage, the slower the pace.
- **(c)** Pace is set by the margin of superiority and by whether enemy reserves can arrive.
- **(d)** The criterion "clearly defined" is not operationalised; the model games show only clear cases.
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8.
- **(f)** Pair games: a king in the centre (fast) vs a castled king with sound pawn cover (slow).
- **(g)** Believing every attack must be fast, or that a slow plan is "passive".

### 4.11 Three kinds of move
- **(a)** Develop / attack / defend.
- **(b)** Every move either brings new force into play, makes pieces do something, or undoes the opponent's work (protects a weak point, blocks a line). What the position requires decides which. Pointing at weaknesses on the way is a way to bring reserves up with gain of time.
- **(c)** Selection must follow the exigencies of the position, not habit.
- **(d)** Overlaps are common (a developing move with a threat); the classification is a thinking aid, not a calculation.
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8.
- **(f)** "Name the purpose of the last move" annotation; choose among three candidates of the three kinds.
- **(g)** Treating the categories as exclusive or as a ranking.

### 4.12 Choosing the wing and creating targets by forcing pawn moves
- **(a)** Direct the attack at the side where targets are; make the opponent's pawns advance.
- **(b)** In Anderssen-Steinitz (as Lasker presents it), a kingside pawn chain, prepared slowly, produces an overwhelming attack; the preparation leaves the opponent no strong point either behind or in front of the advancing pawns. In Lasker-Blackburne, kingside attack is judged hopeless because that wing offers no weaknesses; the fight is in the centre and on the queenside, where a queenside advance fixes a rook pawn as a target, and the material won is then converted. In Noa-Tarrasch, small moves force the opponent either to push a king-side pawn (opening a square) or the bishop pawn (weakening the king pawn); the queen's wing pawn advance dislodges an obstruction.
- **(c)** The target wing is selected by where the opponent has defects, not by where you have more pawns.
- **(d)** Some of the "forcing" arguments are post-hoc praise (exclamatory praise). [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8 (the second half).
- **(f)** Wing-selection items; "which pawn move forces a concession?"
- **(g)** Attacking wherever your own pieces point.

### 4.13 Analysing by plans, not trees (and counting tempi)
- **(a)** Plan enumeration with tempo count; board partition.
- **(b)** Lasker declines to enumerate all variations: it is useless unless very thorough, has been shown by centuries of experience to allow grave errors, and hides the viewpoint from which a position's character is understood. Instead he lists the complete set of plans open to a side (for Black in the Evans normal position: advance the f-pawn; start a queenside pawn attack; break the centre with a c-pawn move; he says there is no other plan) and compares them by counting how many moves each needs while the opponent does something else. In Lecture 7-8, for nearly balanced positions he proposes dividing the board into regions, analysing the partial questions experimentally, then summing.
- **(c)** Works when the number of strategic plans is small and the pawn structure fixes them.
- **(d)** The claim that there is no other plan is a strong completeness claim that is rarely provable; modern engines find extra resources (see Section 5).
- **(e)** Lecture 4 (the Evans normal position); Lecture 7-8; Lecture 3 remark that deep analysis is not meant as memory ballast.
- **(f)** "Which plans does each side have? How many tempi does each need?" exercises.
- **(g)** Believing the tempo count is exact (pawn races vs king-safety) and ignoring forcing moves.

### 4.14 Mating requirements (the ultimate object)
- **(a)** What an attack ultimately must achieve.
- **(b)** To capture the king by force you must command up to nine squares (the king's and its eight neighbours), fewer if the king is at the edge or hemmed in by its own men; the checking piece must be immune to capture and its line immune to interception. The same task is made harder by the need to protect your own king.
- **(c)** Basic geometry; true on any board.
- **(d)** Command here is looser than attack-and-capture; stalemate considerations are not discussed. [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 7-8.
- **(f)** Mate-pattern analysis ("which of the nine squares are covered and by what?").
- **(g)** Counting attackers instead of covered squares.

### 4.15 Defence: wait, do not self-weaken, support the weak point
- **(a)** Principles of defence.
- **(b)** (i) When your position is sound and the opponent prepares an attack, you may ignore the preparations, bring up reserves, evade with the smallest possible defensive move (the boxer's slip), and counterattack quickly. (ii) When you have really compromised yourself, find the weak points (typical: the knight's-pawn and rook-pawn after castling; a knight on the third rank), first vacate them if they hold important pieces (queen, rook, often knight or bishop), then support them from squares that are hard to reach, and use the rest of the army to engage the opponent's reserves. (iii) Do not change your own position voluntarily without forcible reasons; most players fail here by pushing a rook pawn to stop a pin, advancing a knight-pawn to chase a well-placed knight, or retreating a piece because it might be driven away. Wait until the opponent has spent time, material or position on the threat. (iv) Reverse the rules of attack: prevent the opponent from creating strong points near your line. (v) Superfluous defensive moves (preventing something that is no threat) spoil many games.
- **(c)** (i) requires an equal or better position and no real target; (ii) applies when a concrete target exists.
- **(d)** Lasker closes with an absolute: follow the rules and you will find a defence however dangerous the attack looks (Section 5). [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 9; commentary at several earlier points (e.g., Lecture 1 final remarks that you will recoup by the same process, corrupted).
- **(f)** Items with an apparent threat that is really not a threat; items with a real target requiring vacate-then-support.
- **(g)** "Passive defence loses": Lasker's defence is *economical*, not passive. The opposite error: believing every pawn move to meet a possible threat is wise (it is the thing he warns against).
- **Quotation Q2 (Lecture 9):** "Abstain from changing it voluntarily, except for most forcible reasons."

### 4.16 Finding the only defence; do not trust an unverified perpetual
- **(a)** Defensive resourcefulness and verification.
- **(b)** Lecture 9 gives two positions from Lasker's match with Steinitz. In one he played a quick move believing a capture would allow perpetual check; a later analyst (credited to Tchigorin) showed the king escapes, so the plan he feared would actually have been winning for him. In the other, several natural defensive moves are checked and rejected; only one king move holds, and Lasker says attack against it must be handled very cautiously. The moral is to look deeply and test each natural move.
- **(c)** Applies in positions with an apparent threat where several defensive moves look similar.
- **(d)** Cannot be verified here (positions are diagrams, moves partly corrupted). [CHECK]
- **(e)** Lecture 9, last two positions.
- **(f)** "Does the perpetual exist?" items; "list the candidate defences and refute all but one".
- **(g)** Assuming a draw by perpetual without checking the king's escape squares.

### 4.17 In the endgame the rules persist, and the king becomes a fighter
- **(a)** Endgame as continuation of the same principles; king activity.
- **(b)** When the middlegame has thinned both armies and direct mating attacks have lost their chance, the king, until then an object of attack, becomes a strong attacking and defending piece. The general rules of attack and defence are unchanged: weaknesses are mainly blocked pawns that cannot be defended by other pawns; the attacker needs a superiority of some kind; weak points of the opponent are strong points for you.
- **(c)** Queens (and most pieces) off, no immediate mating threats against your king.
- **(d)** Ideas valid in the early game become sensibly modified in the ending (the value of each piece changes). [READ]
- **(e)** Lecture 10-12 opening; the king passage; Morphy's endgame attacks.
- **(f)** "Should the king come out?" decisions; king-march technique items.
- **(g)** Keeping the king at home in an ending because a king in the centre "is dangerous".

### 4.18 Passed pawns and points of vantage
- **(a)** Passed-pawn mechanics.
- **(b)** With reduced material a passed pawn needs never more than five moves to queen, often fewer. Squares and lines from which enemy men can stop the pawn Lasker calls points of vantage with respect to that pawn; the game becomes a fight for them. If the pawn's whole path consists of strong points the opponent must commit a man (perhaps his king) to watching it; satisfied that part of the enemy army is tied down, you may attack with everything else elsewhere. Even backed by little force a passed pawn can be dangerous (a middlegame-style combination built on a far-advanced pawn).
- **(c)** The tie-down effect requires that the passed pawn really cannot be stopped cheaply.
- **(d)** The five-move claim is for pawns that begin from their initial rank with the double step. [READ implicit] [OUTSIDE] correct.
- **(e)** Lecture 10-12.
- **(f)** Race/tie-down items; "which enemy piece is now tied?".
- **(g)** Pushing a passed pawn mechanically without asking what it ties down or whether the king can support it.

### 4.19 The principle of exhaustion (moves as a resource; zugzwang and corresponding squares)
- **(a)** Principle of exhaustion (his term): zugzwang, tempo reserves, corresponding squares.
- **(b)** When both sides are so well placed that neither can improve unless the opponent gives way, having to move loses power: once equally placed, in his words (Q3, Lectures 10-12), "the move ceases to be a privilege". In such positions each side carefully holds back moves that would help or at least not harm, until the right moment. Lasker works out king-and-pawn examples by matching squares: for each placement of one king, there is a square or a set of squares the other king must occupy; if the side to move must leave the matching squares, it loses. Hence conclusions of the form: draws if this side is to move, loses if the other side is. In one example he changes who is to move by a king manoeuvre that costs an odd number of moves, which is what we would call losing a tempo or triangulation. A pawn is held back as long as possible because pawn moves are tempo reserves.
- **(c)** Little room for manoeuvre, blocked or locked pawns, and no pieces whose moves could pass the turn.
- **(d)** If either side has a free tempo move (a pawn or piece that can move harmlessly) the principle does not bite. The diagrams are missing in the OCR so I cannot reconstruct which positions were used. [CHECK]
- **(e)** Lectures 10-12, first illustrations and the McLaren question.
- **(f)** Tablebase-verifiable king-and-pawn positions: who is to move?
- **(g)** Believing "the opposition" is a fixed square pattern rather than a consequence of who must move; or believing the side with the move is always better.

### 4.20 Piece values in the ending
- **(a)** Endgame values are positional, not fixed.
- **(b)** Value is set by a piece's fighting power against the enemy king as an attacker, against passed pawns, and by its reach when obstructions are few. King: powerful, takes three squares from the enemy king in opposition, reaches any square quickly from the centre, cannot be obstructed, but cannot itself be used as an obstruction or exposed to direct attack. Rook: strong against the king (checks, cutting off), helpful for removing obstructions, best placed behind a passed pawn (his reach grows as the pawn advances); can stop two unsupported passed pawns on the fifth and sixth, but two united pawns on the sixth queen against it; too valuable to be given up lightly for pawns. Bishop: restricted to one colour, weaker against a king than a rook, supports passed pawns less well because the path has squares where obstructions are safe from it, but useful for stopping pawns from afar and mutual pawn-bishop protection; its value swings widely with the position. Knight: usually the weakest, short reach, may need several moves to cross; cannot be obstructed and shines in blocked positions on strong points near the enemy; he says opposite corners need five moves (wrong: six). Bishop vs knight: the bishop at least as good on average in opening and middlegame and more so as obstructions vanish; knight prefers complicated endings with pawn blocks; two bishops are appreciably stronger than two knights or bishop+knight.
- **(c)** Depends on pawn structure, openness, and king position.
- **(d)** See Section 5 for claims needing a tablebase or a count.
- **(e)** Lecture 10-12, the long piece-by-piece passage and the correspondence-game examples.
- **(f)** Which piece do you want? Bishop vs knight in open vs blocked structures; rook behind or in front of the pawn.
- **(g)** Treating the 3-3-5-9 values as constant.

### 4.21 Sources of opening judgment: where a first move leaves you weak
- **(a)** How a defence's first move shapes its character.
- **(b)** In the French, the first pawn move blocks the defender's queen's bishop's long diagonal and, equally, blocks the attacker's bishop diagonal toward the f-square which in ordinary king-pawn openings is the initially weakest point of Black's camp. Those two features give the French its permanent character. [READ]
- **(c)** Strictly about first-move structure.
- **(d)** Some claims are dated (see Section 5), e.g. that early advance of unbacked pawns is always inadvisable.
- **(e)** Lecture 6 opening.
- **(f)** Which diagonals are closed by this pawn? Where is the initial weak point?
- **(g)** Calling a blocked bishop "bad" regardless of what it later does.

### 4.22 Why show the inferior alternative (contrast as method)
- **(a)** Truth appears through contrast with apparent truth.
- **(b)** Lasker explicitly says that truth gets its strength from contrast with what only seems true, and in several places he lets the opponent pay for daring moves so the reader sees the full set of possibilities. He also says deep variations are shown not to burden memory but to demonstrate that the superior position forces a win by whatever route.
- **(c)** Contrast is aimed at principle learners who already know the position type.
- **(d)** Not systematic; no irrelevant case is ever shown.
- **(e)** Lecture 2 (daring move), Lecture 3 (opening paragraph and the memory remark), Lecture 4.
- **(f)** Contrast families (Section 8).
- **(g)** Reading the refuted line as "the opponent made an obvious mistake" rather than as a plausible look-alike.

## 5. Claims that are dated, absolute, or contestable

Reasoning: an engine-era reader should treat all opening verdicts as 1895 judgments and all absolute words as hypotheses.

1. **Opening verdicts.** All are 1895 theory.
   - Ruy Lopez 3...a6 "damages the queenside, no compensation" (Lecture 3). [OUTSIDE] The Morphy Defence is the modern main line; the open variation Lasker analyses is considered balanced. Principle-based conclusion fails because piece activity and later ...b5 / ...d5 counterplay compensate. [CHECK] by engine if used as an example of "pawn-work vs piece-activity".
   - Ruy Lopez 3...Nf6 gives Black a satisfactory game (Lecture 2). [OUTSIDE] Consistent with modern respect for the Berlin; the line shown is the old 5.d4 treatment, not the modern main line. [CHECK]
   - King's Bishop's Gambit is declared lost for the first player with best play (Lecture 5). [OUTSIDE] Overstated; modern theory regards the 3.Bc4 Qh4+ lines as playable-to-inferior but not lost. [CHECK]
   - Immortal Game: Lasker says White could have crushed Black by a central pawn advance at move 17 (his words, descriptively). [OUTSIDE] Engine analyses of the game are known to differ from the romantic verdict; [CHECK] if used.
   - Evans Gambit: the "normal position" is said to give White much better chances (Lecture 4). [OUTSIDE] Modern view: playable, not clearly better. [CHECK]
   - French: advancing the e-pawn to the fifth (his view: early pawn advances not backed by pieces are always inadvisable), Lecture 6. [OUTSIDE] The Advance Variation is a main line today. 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5, which he calls contrary to the rules of development, is also a main line. These illustrate that *development rules are defaults*, not verdicts.
   - His view that the French Exchange Variation gives an even game, in which the first-move advantage counts for little: [OUTSIDE] largely still accepted.
2. **Absolute words.**
   - Trading a bishop for a pinned knight is "always" a great mistake (Lecture 6). [OUTSIDE] Routine in many structures. [CHECK] with examples before teaching any version.
   - Doubled or isolated pawns as endgame weaknesses in the early game are a "chimera" (Lecture 7-8). [OUTSIDE] Too strong; structure matters in many plans; though his narrower claim, that the open files and tempi gained may outweigh the damage, is a fair dynamic caution.
   - His closing promise that following the rules always yields a defence, however dangerous the attack looks (end of Lecture 9). Over-promising; some positions are lost.
   - His statement that Black has no other plan in the Evans normal position (Lecture 4). A completeness claim; engines usually add resources. [CHECK]
   - Ignoring the opponent's attacking preparations when your position is not inferior (Lecture 9). [OUTSIDE] A good default, but it contradicts later prophylactic thought (Nimzowitsch, Petrosian) in positions where a plan, once begun, cannot be stopped; the condition (equal, no target) must be checked.
   - Chess has no forced solution from the start, he argues, because the first move's advantage is trifling (Lecture 7-8). [OUTSIDE] Consistent with the modern belief that the initial position is a draw, but this is not proven.
3. **Factual/arithmetical slips.**
   - Knight needs five moves to cross between diagonally opposite corners. I computed the shortest path (breadth-first search on an 8x8 board): **six** for opposite corners; five is the figure for corners on the same rank or file. [CHECK] (computation done by me; trivially reproducible).
   - He gives the rook a command of thirteen squares at a time (Lecture 10-12). On an empty board a rook attacks fourteen. A bishop maximum is thirteen. May be a slip or an OCR/typographical issue. [CHECK] against another scan.
   - A lone king can stop three united passed pawns not beyond the sixth rank, or two with one on the seventh (Lecture 10-12). [OUTSIDE] plausible but conditional on king position; [CHECK] by tablebase before teaching any version.
   - Endgame king/pawn conclusions (the verdicts of the form: draw if White moves, loss if Black moves) depend on missing diagrams. [CHECK]
   - His ranking of the knight as the weakest piece: [OUTSIDE] debatable, though he adds a favourable-circumstances proviso.
4. **Style-level dating.** The term close games, tricky play, language about sickly policy, ethical language about gambits, military analogy as explanation; the assumption that the audience's reading of "common sense" is shared.
5. **Terms that conflict with later usage.** *Weakness*, *weak point*, *strong point*, *obstruction*, *exhaustion*, *pace* are Lasker's coinages; later authors use outpost/hole, blockade, zugzwang/opposition, initiative. Mapping must be explicit so learners are not taught two vocabularies.
6. **Authority claims.** Lasker admits at the end of the Lecture 1 surviving fragment that he speaks authoritatively without proof; the Preface asks the reader to demand proofs (Q1: "reasonably sceptical and exacting in the matter of proofs"). The curriculum should accept that invitation: each principle taught must come with a verified failing look-alike.

## 6. Teaching method observed

1. **Principle, then proving ground.** [READ] A principle is announced (often in military or fighting terms), then tested in model games and positions selected or adapted to the purpose.
2. **Show the punishment.** [READ] Early lectures open with short games where one neglected principle is punished quickly; the commentary names the offending move.
3. **Show the refutation of the plausible alternative.** [READ] Lines where the side that played a daring move is made to pay for it are included deliberately; contrast is stated as a method (4.22).
4. **Questions to the reader.** [READ] He asks the reader mid-game what the right move is, and in one place challenges the reader to find a mate in two; and in Lecture 9 poses a defensive problem and discusses every natural reply before giving the only move.
5. **Rules with corollaries and amendments.** [READ] Rules are not final: an amendment is added after each opening lecture, and exceptions are acknowledged in place.
6. **Analysis by principle, not tree.** [READ] Variation enumeration is explicitly rejected (4.13); he states that deep variations are not meant to burden the memory.
7. **Reasons for moves in short phrases.** [READ] Typical annotations say a move develops and attacks at once, or blocks a dangerous file while developing, or bears down on weak points; such short reasons work well as reason tags for our own text.
8. **Self-correction as pedagogy.** [READ] He shows his own miscalculation (Lecture 9) and credits an audience member's suggestion in Lecture 4 and a question from McLaren in Lecture 10-12, modelling inquiry.
9. **Level.** [READ] Assumes fluent descriptive notation and ability to follow 15-30-move games without diagrams in the line text; many sentences assume the reader tracks positions mentally. Not suitable as a first book; suitable as a source for ideas for T2-T5 nodes. For adult learners today, material needs diagrams.
10. **Weak spots in method.** [READ] The "four rules" are central yet the claim of unification is never delivered; evaluation language is verdict-heavy (exclamatory praise) and the proof standard is that the opponent could not escape in the lines shown.

## 8. Candidate exercise & contrast-family ideas

Cue levels in this section: 1 = most support (concept named, key squares marked) to 4 = no support (I assume this reading of the map's cue ladder; adjust if the project's definition differs). Every idea below needs positions generated and engine-verified; none is supplied here. Format: position type; learner action; cue level; contrast family (working case / failing look-alike / irrelevant case).

1. **Developing vs time-wasting move (early opening).** Position type: after three or four moves, no concrete threat. Learner chooses the move that adds the most to the working of a piece. Cue 1-3. *Working:* a knight to its natural square with tempo. *Failing look-alike:* a "useful-looking" rook-pawn move adding no force while the opponent has an open line to a weak point. *Irrelevant:* a position with a real pin where a rook-pawn move is the concrete answer. Node: `OPN.NONDEV`.
2. **Exception to quick development.** Position type: a settled structure where one piece is destined for a coming attack. Learner chooses between completing development and a protective/consolidating pawn move. Cue 2-4. *Working:* the quiet move that preserves the key piece. *Failing:* same-looking quiet move in an unsettled position where it loses the initiative. *Irrelevant:* a structure with no piece destined for a plan. Node: `POS.EXCEPTIONS`.
3. **Take or develop after a pawn offer (gambit acceptance).** Position type: opponent has sacrificed a pawn for development. Learner decides: grab, return the pawn, or develop. Cue 2-4. *Working:* the gambit where compensation is real and the pawn should be returned. *Failing look-alike:* a gambit-looking position where the pawn can be kept because the attacker's lines are closed. *Irrelevant:* a free pawn offered with no development consequence. Needs verified evaluations. Nodes: `OPN.DEVIATION`, `SAC.TYPES`.
4. **Go / no-go: should I attack?** Position type: middlegame, both kings castled or one king central. Learner picks: attack, finish development, or defend; must justify with a count of pieces bearing on the target area and which enemy men defend. Cue 2-4. *Working:* a development lead with a king in the centre. *Failing look-alike:* an attack on a fully developed opponent with no weak target (premature attack that runs out of steam). *Irrelevant:* a quiet endgame-like position. Node: `ATK.SUPERIORITY`.
5. **Weak or strong point?** Position type: mid-board with pawns and minor pieces. Learner marks squares that enemy pawns and minor pieces can never challenge and chooses where to put a knight/bishop with a large field. Cue 1-3. *Working:* an outpost near the enemy line with a large field. *Failing:* a square that looks weak but can be challenged by an enemy pawn in one move. *Irrelevant:* a deep hole with no access to targets. Node: `POS.WEAKSQ`.
6. **Which pawn move forces a concession on the attacked wing?** Position type: queenside or kingside pressure, a quiet pawn lever available. Learner finds the pawn move that forces the opponent to advance a pawn or weaken a square. Cue 2-4. *Working:* a lever that fixes a target. *Failing:* a lever that frees the opponent's pieces. *Irrelevant:* a wing with no targets (Lasker's own example of a wing offering no weaknesses). Nodes: `POS.BREAKS`, `ATK.PAWNSTORM`.
7. **Do I need this defensive move?** Position type: opponent makes a move that looks like a threat (pawn to an attacking square). Learner decides: ignore and develop or respond. Cue 1-4 (include many no-threat items). *Working:* waiting move wins because the threat is not real. *Failing look-alike:* the same-looking move that is a genuine threat needing prevention. *Irrelevant:* a position with nothing to defend. Node: `DEF.WAIT`, `SAF.THREAT`.
8. **Vacate then support.** Position type: opponent threatens a weak point holding an important piece (e.g. king-side pawns after castling with a queen on the target square). Learner picks the order of moves: vacate, support, or counter. Cue 2-3. *Working:* vacate first. *Failing:* support first while the important piece remains exposed. *Irrelevant:* the piece is not on the target square. Node: `DEF.RESOURCES`.
9. **Does the perpetual exist?** Position type: the defender or attacker believes a perpetual check is available. Learner must test the king's escape squares and interposition before relying on it. Cue 2-4. *Working:* a true perpetual. *Failing look-alike:* a check sequence the king escapes (modelled on Lasker's miscalculation, but built from newly generated positions). *Irrelevant:* a position with no checks. Needs engine verification. Nodes: `TAC.PERPETUAL`, `CAL.DEFENSE`.
10. **Points of vantage for a passed pawn.** Position type: king-and-minor-piece ending with a passed pawn. Learner lists the squares/lines the defender must control and decides whether the pawn ties down enough to allow an attack elsewhere. Cue 1-3. *Working:* pawn whose path is covered by strong points. *Failing:* pawn whose path has one square the enemy king can reach in time. *Irrelevant:* position with no passed pawn. Nodes: `POS.PASSED`, `END.SQUARE`.
11. **Who is to move? (exhaustion).** Position type: king-and-pawn endings with locked pawns. Learner states whether the side to move wins, draws, or loses, then chooses the plan (including spending or saving tempi). Cue 1-4; solved by tablebase. *Working:* a true mutual-zugzwang. *Failing look-alike:* nearly identical placement where a spare pawn move exists so the idea does not apply. *Irrelevant:* a position with open play where kings simply race. Nodes: `END.OPPOSITION`, `END.KEYSQ`, `END.TRIANGULATION`.
12. **King out or king home?** Position type: queens off vs queens on with a similar king geometry. Learner decides whether to bring the king to the centre. Cue 1-3. *Working:* queens gone, no mating pieces. *Failing look-alike:* queens still on, with a rook lift available to the opponent. *Irrelevant:* a position where the king cannot reach anything. Node: `END.KINGPOWER`.
13. **Bishop or knight to keep?** Position type: minor-piece ending or middlegame with pawn structure either open or locked. Learner chooses which to keep and states the structural reason. Cue 1-3. *Working:* bishop in open play with pawns on both flanks; knight in a locked structure with a strong outpost. *Failing look-alike:* a nominally open structure where one flank is blocked and the bishop is hemmed. *Irrelevant:* a position where a trade is forced anyway. Nodes: `END.MINOR`, `POS.GOODBAD`.
14. **Count the tempi.** Position type: two plans, one on each wing, with no immediate tactics. Learner counts moves each side needs and says who is faster, then checks for forcing moves that break the count. Cue 2-4. *Working:* a clean count. *Failing:* a count spoiled by a forcing check. *Irrelevant:* a position where a tactic dominates. Nodes: `LOOP.V4`, `CAL.COMPARE`.

## 9. Agreements and tensions with the other sources in this batch and with modern teaching

**Important caveat.** [OUTSIDE] In this task I did not read the Capablanca, Nimzowitsch, Znosko-Borovsky or Edward Lasker texts, nor their sheets. What follows is recalled general knowledge about those books and may be wrong in detail; each item is weak evidence and should be checked against the corresponding sheet when it is written.

- **Capablanca, *Chess Fundamentals*.** [OUTSIDE] Agreement: both prize rapid development and sensible piece placement and treat the king as an active piece in the ending. Difference: Capablanca organises teaching from simple endings upward and gives plain practical rules with minimal theory; Lasker aims at a unifying theory and spends half the book on opening verdicts. Lasker's endgame section is more conceptual (exhaustion, vantage points) than Capablanca's demonstrations [OUTSIDE, weak recollection].
- **Nimzowitsch, *My System*.** [OUTSIDE] Agreement: both treat blockade/obstruction (Lasker even uses the queen as a blockader) and weak-square/strong-point thinking; Lasker's weak point/strong point and Nimzowitsch's outpost/hole cover similar ground, with Lasker's earlier and less systematic. Tension: Lasker's defensive advice to ignore preparations and wait contrasts with Nimzowitsch's prophylaxis, though the difference may be in conditions rather than doctrine; Lasker's dismissal of doubled/isolated pawns early on partly resembles Nimzowitsch's defence of doubled pawns, but I recall that book also cautions about isolated pawns, so treat as unclear. Vocabulary clash: obstruction vs blockade; weakness vs hole.
- **Znosko-Borovsky, *The Middle Game*.** [OUTSIDE, weak] I recall an emphasis on force, space and time and on planning from the position. This partly parallels Lasker's force/time framing; difference: Znosko-Borovsky is organized by middlegame themes rather than by game phase. Unknown in detail.
- **Edward Lasker, *Chess Strategy*.** [OUTSIDE, weak] Edward Lasker (a different person from Emanuel) discusses development, exchanges, pawn structure and the endgame; I do not know the book well enough to compare points. A priori the two Laskers should share some formulations of development; treat as unverified.
- **Steinitz.** [READ] Lasker cites a Steinitz principle (support an advanced centre pawn with the bishop pawn). [OUTSIDE] Lasker's "no attack without superiority" is the Steinitz-Lasker line that modern teaching still repeats.
- **Modern teaching.** [OUTSIDE] (1) Opening principles are taught as defaults (Watson, Silman-style exceptions) which agrees with the best of Lasker's practice and with CD-4. (2) Dynamic evaluation: modern theory does not accept "damaged pawn structure ⇒ inferior position" without counting activity; Lasker's Ruy Lopez verdict is the textbook case of that error. (3) Modern attack teaching (conditions for the attack) restates Lasker's rule (i) in a more granular form. (4) Zugzwang and corresponding squares are taught as concrete techniques with tablebase confirmation; Lasker's principle of exhaustion is a good conceptual introduction but is not enough alone. (5) The "king as a fighting piece" idea is universal. (6) Prophylaxis as a separate named skill (Nimzowitsch/Petrosian/Dvoretsky) is not in Lasker.
