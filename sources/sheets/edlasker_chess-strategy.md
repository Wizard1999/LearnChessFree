## 2. One-paragraph portrait

A 1915 manual for the improving club player who has no access to master teaching, built on one thesis: a short list of strategic principles (quick development with no lost moves, a pawn centre, mobility of the pieces, a sound pawn structure) is enough to judge any move, so the reader should be taught to reason rather than to memorise [READ, preface l. 288-311]. Its method is unusual: it starts the opening from the pawn skeleton, then teaches the endgame *before* the middlegame (because middlegame decisions are made with the ending in view), then closes with 48 master games annotated to show the principles at work [READ, l. 321-364]. The voice is confident, argumentative and sometimes dismissive of opening analysis as a study method; it includes its author's own losses (Games 19 and 46) and speculates freely about time trouble. The reader is assumed to follow long variations in descriptive notation from ASCII diagrams, so the effective audience is an advanced beginner to club player; only about twelve pages are addressed to true beginners. There are no exercises, no solutions and no summaries by node; the teaching is in worked verdicts. Only about 700 of the 13,900 lines are a dedicated middlegame chapter, so the title promises more strategy than the book contains: it is really a pawn-structure-driven opening guide, an endgame primer, and a model-game collection [READ; I].

## 3. Structure of the book

Part I (principles and phases)
- Translator's and Author's Prefaces (l. 213-381): thesis, method, order of chapters; advice to study each critical diagram and form your own judgment *before* reading the continuation.
- **Ch. I Introductory** (l. 389-683): rules; descriptive and algebraic notation compared; piece names in ten languages. Not strategic.
- **Ch. II Hints for Beginners: Elementary Combinations** (l. 686-1136): stages of a beginner's development; the arithmetic of counting attackers and defenders on a square; three kinds of complication; counting what can arrive in the next few moves.
- **Ch. III General Principles of Chess Strategy** (l. 1138-1297): strategy vs tactics, rejection of military analogies, balance of attack and defence, mobility as the measure of piece value; two illustrative diagrams.
- **Ch. IV The Opening** (l. 1299-3286): development and the lost move (with Morphy's opera-house game); pawn play and the pawn skeleton; the centre; King's Pawn games (King's Gambit, Falkbeer, Danish/Centre/Scotch/Bishop's gambits, the three defensive systems against the Ruy Lopez, French and Caro-Kann with pawn-chain diagrams, Scandinavian/Sicilian remarks); Queen's Pawn games (Queen's Gambit systems, Stonewall, irregular replies); irregular openings; a restatement of the two opening principles.
- **Ch. V The End-game** (l. 3288-4983): minimum force to mate; mates with Q, R, two bishops, bishop+knight; two knights cannot force mate; Q, R, B, N against one piece; pawn endings (K+P v K, opposition, rook-pawn exception, majorities, distant passed pawn, equal pawns and king position, doubled pawns); endings with one piece each (bishops, knights, rooks, queens), wrong bishop, advanced pawn vs queen.
- **End-games from Master Play** (l. 4984-5570): six endings (Teichmann-Blackburne 1897; E. Lasker-Rotlewi 1910; Blackburne-Schlechter 1898; Bird-Janowski 1895; Steiner-Forgacz 1907; Charousek-Heinrichsen 1898) with per-move reasoning.
- **Ch. VI The Middle Game** (l. 5573-6298): the ending dictates middlegame pawn policy; choosing an objective that cannot move away; pawn moves create weaknesses (diagrams 90-98); a model open-file game fragment (Lazard-Ed. Lasker 1914); a five-point recapitulation.

## 4. Core ideas

Each entry: (a) name; (b) idea in my words; (c) conditions; (d) exceptions/limits stated or implied; (e) where; (f) example type; (g) likely misconception. Numbering (K1..K30) is for cross-reference only, not node IDs.

### K1. Typical stages of a beginner (diagnostic)
(b) The book describes three stages: chaotic pushing of pawns and wandering queen and knight raids; then perception of one-to-two-move threats and sounder combinations; and, only after years, positional play. It says the first stage is natural and a few dozen games in it are useful, but that learning stalls at stage 2 unless the learner studies how to open a game and how to treat the middlegame with the ending in view. (c) Applies to untutored self-learners. (d) Anecdotal; no evidence offered [READ]. (e) Ch. II, l. 692-755. (f) Descriptive prose, no position. (g) That more games alone produce positional understanding.

### K2. Count attackers and defenders on one square, weighting by value
(b) Before pushing a pawn into a contested square or starting captures there, tally every unit that can capture on that square for each side, then compare the *values* lost; do not play out an imagined sequence move by move, because beginners forget which pieces they used. If the total value the pusher loses is not more than what the opponent loses, the push is acceptable. (c) All counted units are really able to capture (no pins, no blockers); equal head-counts favour the side whose capturers are cheaper; the arithmetic is the default for the elementary capture-and-recapture cases. (d) The book immediately lists spoilers (K3) and says values yield to mobility in non-elementary cases (K5); the one-line rule ignores the fact that either side can stop capturing midway and that cheapest-first order matters [OUTSIDE: modern static exchange evaluation]. One sentence is quotable and central: the book's rule is that "loss of material must be avoided, even if only a pawn" (Ch. II). (e) Ch. II, diagrams 4-5, l. 793-871. (f) A crowded square with six attackers against six defenders; a pawn attacked three times and defended twice with cheaper defenders. (g) "More attackers than defenders always wins material" or "equal counts are always safe".

### K3. Three ways the count lies
(b) (i) A defender can itself be captured by a cheaper piece, so its defence is illusory; (ii) a defender can be forced away by a bigger threat elsewhere, for instance a pawn threatening to promote, or a mate threat that ties down a piece; (iii) a defender can be pinned. Also: a piece's own capture can unmask an attack by another piece, changing the tally. (c) Any time one of the counted defenders is itself loose, pinned, or needed elsewhere. (d) None stated; the author offers the list as a sample of frequent cases, not a complete one. (e) Ch. II, diagrams 6-12, l. 873-1127. (f) Positions where capture-recapture arithmetic favours the capturer but a defender is attackable by a cheaper unit, or is pinned to a rook. (g) That the arithmetic is a proof.

### K4. Count what can arrive, not only what is there
(b) For a target not yet attacked (a pawn you might support or attack), count how many additional attackers and defenders each side can bring in how many moves; a pawn push is safe if the defence can keep pace, dangerous if the other side has more reserves. Again check whether each would-be defender is genuinely effective (it may be chased off or be pinned). (c) The target stays put and reinforcements have access. (d) Same spoilers as K3. (e) Ch. II, diagrams 9-12, l. 970-1127. (f) A pawn on d5 attacked and defended once each, with three more reachable units on each side. (g) Counting only the present attackers.

### K5. Mobility is the value of a piece and the balance of forces; values are conditional
(b) Ch. III: an attack is justified only if more force can be concentrated at the point than the defence can muster; raw numerical equality is not balance, because what counts is how many units can actually act there. The text treats mobility as the primary criterion, in the author's words "mobility is the first and foremost consideration" (Ch. III). Intrinsic ordering: rook above bishop; bishop and knight equal; two bishops above two knights; the whole table fails if the position hinders a piece. The footnote prices: minor pieces equal; rook is a minor piece plus one or two pawns; queen is two rooks or three minors. (c) Ordinary open or semi-open positions; the ordering is for typical arrangements. (d) The book itself shows exceptions: a rook with no open file to enter gives no benefit from winning the exchange (diagram 13); equal material loses when development has left the side with no resources (diagram 14). Bishop vs knight in endgames is called hard to gauge (l. 4534-4538), yet a Game 19 note calls the knight generally superior in endings (l. 9399): internal inconsistency [READ]. (e) Ch. II footnote 2 (l. 844-850), Ch. III (l. 1193-1291), Ch. V (l. 4534-4541). (f) Rook unable to reach a file; a team of undeveloped pieces facing an attack. (g) That piece values are fixed numbers.

### K6. Quick development and the lost move
(b) The aim of the opening is to bring all pieces, in the fewest moves, to squares where they command the most squares, then concentrate on one point. White moves first, so only knights can move at once; e4 and d4 are the pawn moves that free both bishop and queen; two or three pawn moves are enough, and any other pawn move (or any piece move that takes more moves than needed to reach a square) *loses a move*. A lost move tends to cause more lost moves because later moves must repair the damage. Exchanging so that the opponent's bishop has moved twice while your knight moved once gains a move. (The book speaks of losing and gaining a move; it never uses the word tempo [READ, text search confirmed].) (c) Both sides play sensibly; the position is open enough that piece activity decides; the pawn move is not forced by a threat. (d) Book exceptions: the pawn move a6 in the Ruy Lopez is justified, h3 is called one of the few justified pawn moves in one position (Diagram 23, l. 2164-2168), and gambit sacrifices of a pawn for development are accepted. Early precautionary pawn moves are criticised, but a luft is correct once a mate threat shows it is needed (Game 2 note, l. 6528-6532). (e) Ch. IV, l. 1305-1562 (a beginner game, then Morphy's opera game); restated l. 3256-3272; Game notes 6, 36, 39 (l. 7044, 11426, 12063). (f) Two move-by-move comparisons showing the same position reached with an extra pawn move; a mating finish against a side with undeveloped pieces. (g) That moving the same piece many times is activity, or that no pawn move beyond the centre is ever right.

### K7. A development lead licenses direct attack; a development deficit voids it
(b) If the opponent is behind in development, the attack can be started without long analysis and can justify sacrifices to open files and diagonals; conversely, if you are behind, even a tempting attack is unsound and calculating it is wasted effort, which the author says beginners and even stronger players resist. (c) The opponent is behind and cannot castle, or lines can be opened quickly; the attacker has several pieces in play. (d) Sacrifices are not automatically sound: the note to Game 7 says the Max Lange attack had long been out of tournament use, that Marshall's novelty in that game reopened the question, and that afterwards no master defended it [READ]. The book judges sacrifices largely by what happened in the games, not by systematic analysis [I]. (e) Ch. IV l. 1368-1378; Games 3, 10, 14, 20, 31, 35, 39. (f) Open centre with the defender's queenside pieces undeveloped and an uncastled king. (g) "Sacrifice whenever the king is exposed" or "never sacrifice".

### K8. White has the initiative as a birthright; a lost move reverses roles
(b) The first move makes White morally entitled to attack (the author's phrase) and Black the natural defender, until White loses a move, at which point Black acquires the initiative. (c) Opening stage, both sides developing sensibly. (d) The book's own openings show Black equalising by ...d5 and counterattacking (Falkbeer, Riga). Dated as an absolute [OUTSIDE]. (e) l. 1315-1320, 1617-1623. (f) Opening positions. (g) That Black must be passive.

### K9. Pawns cannot go back: the pawn skeleton
(b) Pieces can retreat; pawns cannot. So the pawn formation is the lasting element, usually chosen in the opening and often surviving into the endgame. Plan from it: arrange the pieces round the skeleton, and change the skeleton only after thought. In the middlegame the same lens is used for how the skeleton *transforms*. (c) As long as exchanges and breaks have not transformed the structure. (d) The book concedes transformation is the real middlegame issue (l. 5637-5643) but gives no separate worked section on the evolution of the skeleton despite the contents page listing one. (e) preface l. 331-341; Ch. IV l. 1564-1588; Ch. VI l. 5637-5643. (f) Chain diagrams 29-36. (g) That structure is fixed once chosen.

### K10. The centre: squares, lines, pieces and pawns
(b) The central squares (e4, d4 for each side; lesser c4, f4) matter because pieces there have more mobility and can reach any wing quickest. Lure the opponent's central pawn away by attacking it with a pawn thrust that can be exchanged; do not give up your own central pawn without a gain (open lines for your pieces or a commanding piece in the centre). **But** removing the enemy centre pawn while keeping your own does not automatically give central control: opening the lines toward the centre for your pieces is what counts (Diagram 17 example, where a mechanical capture of the offered pawn backfires). (c) You are not behind in development; you can recapture. (d) The book also advises giving up the centre when it buys a compensating plan (French/Rio de Janeiro cases), but Game 21 (surrendering the centre) and Game 26 (abandoning the centre for the long diagonal) are presented as losing lessons. (e) Ch. IV l. 1590-1706; Games 21, 26, 36 (l. 9680, 10272-10278, 11474). (f) A pawn-exchange offer in the centre; an opened diagonal after exchanges. (g) "Pawns on e4/d4 equal centre control" or "always take the offered centre pawn".

### K11. Keep the option of ...d5 in open games (and general freeing breaks)
(b) In King's Pawn openings after White's d4, Black's central ...d5 is the standard equalising break; do not make early moves (such as covering the pawn with ...c5) that let White prevent it by an early c3 or by the e-file. Compact comparison tables are given (Centre Gambit, King's Bishop's Opening, Scotch Gambit, Scotch Game, l. 1929-1966). (c) The break is supported by development and not met by a pin tactic. (d) The Ruy Lopez is the exception where Black must struggle for it (l. 2057-2062). (e) l. 1759-2056. (f) Opening trees ending in an equalising ...d5. (g) Always playing ...d6.

### K12. Where the rooks will operate follows from the pawn structure
(b) Decide the rook files from the pawn skeleton: after 1.e4 e5 the e- and d-files open by exchange; in French and Caro-Kann chains the central files are closed so each side advances a wing pawn (White the f-pawn, Black the c-pawn and sometimes b-pawn); in closed d-pawn games the c-file is the target, so the queen's knight should not go to c3 before the c-pawn decision. Bishop development is tied to the same choice. (c) That structure type. (d) Kingside rook files (f/g/h) remain possible with a pawn storm (l. 2805-2813). (e) Ch. IV l. 2433-2555, 2793-2820. (f) The pawn-chain diagrams 29-32. (g) That rooks belong on the first open file regardless of structure.

### K13. Prefer simple development to a messy pawn grab (a beginner policy)
(b) When there is a choice, choose clear development over winning a pawn that leads to complicated play; hence the student should decline the King's Gambit and answer gambits by freeing moves rather than trying to hold the pawn; the accepted King's Gambit is called suitable only for experts. (c) Beginner or club learner; an equally good simple option exists. (d) Stated as a principle for learners, not as the best move. (e) l. 1838-1892 (Rice Gambit footnote as an example of difficulty). (f) Gambit positions with a safe declined line. (g) "Always take free material."

### K14. Understand openings rather than memorise; opponent's bad opening can still win
(b) Learn the principles so you can handle irregular openings; if a player wins despite a poor opening, he simply played better afterwards. The author thinks opening analysis is rated far too highly as a study method. (c) The learner can already calculate and the principles are internalised. (d) The author's own book devotes about 2,000 lines to opening variations and a Table of Openings, and admits the middlegame and endgame cannot be wholly deduced from the rules (l. 3274-3284), contradicting the preface claim that the principles are sufficient [READ]. (e) preface l. 307-311; l. 3249-3284; l. 5614-5619. (f) None. (g) "Opening knowledge is irrelevant."

### K15. Choose an objective that cannot move away
(b) An attack should aim at something that cannot evade: a piece that is pinned, or a pawn that is held in place (by occupying the square in front, or controlling it with more units than the opponent can bring). Harassing a movable piece with pawns loses time (the piece retreats to an equally good square while the harasser has spent more moves) and weakens one's own pawn structure; do not drive a piece off a good post if it costs structure or leaves pieces offside. (c) The target really is movable and has equal alternatives. (d) The book hedges the rule with a generally-speaking qualifier; chasing is acceptable when it does not weaken the skeleton or send pieces far afield. (e) Ch. VI l. 5645-5746; recap items 1-2 (l. 6260-6267). (f) A pawn advance to chase a bishop; ...c5 to kick a queen, leaving the d-pawn backward (diagrams 90-91). (g) That kicking a piece with a pawn always gains time.

### K16. A backward pawn as a target: fix it and out-count the defence
(b) Prevent it from advancing (by an enemy pawn on the square in front, or control of that square), then pile attackers on it along the file: double rooks, add the queen and a bishop, and bring a further attacker (possibly a pawn) that the defence cannot match. The defender can add only so many. This is easier when the defender lacks a bishop of the pawn's colour. The book's footnote definition is simply a pawn that cannot move to be covered by another pawn [READ, l. 1977-1978]. (c) The pawn is genuinely stuck, on a semi-open file, and the attacker's pieces have access. (d) A defender may free the game with a pawn break; the stop-square-control nuance is not spelled out. (e) l. 1973-1979, 5769-5782 (diagram 92); Games 8 (l. 7338-7371), 26 (l. 10310-10345), 48 (l. 13340). (f) Rooks and queen massed on a backward pawn on an open file. (g) That every pawn behind its neighbours is backward and weak.

### K17. Every pawn move leaves something behind (especially in front of the castled king)
(b) Ask of each pawn move which squares it used to guard and which pawns it leaves unprotected. In front of the castled king this opens entry squares for hostile pieces (queen + knight + bishop), and the advanced pawn can be a target for a pawn lever with rook support or a piece sacrifice that strips the king's cover. Do not play a luft early as a precaution before it is needed. The Ch. VI recapitulation sums up the consequence: pawn moves always have drawbacks, so, in the author's words, "the middle game is the pieces' own hunting ground" (Ch. VI, recap point 5). (c) The opponent has pieces that can reach the weakened squares (e.g. a bishop of the right colour, a knight) and your own defenders are traded or misplaced. (d) The book shows the move is fine when it forces off the opponent's attackers by exchanges (analysis after diagram 90: ...Be6 against Nd5) and when the pawn move is needed (h3 in Diagram 23). (e) Ch. VI l. 5872-6112 (diagrams 93-96); Games 2, 10, 12, 29, 42 (l. 6507-6531, 7783-7855, 8200-8265, 10733-10737, 12524). (f) The h3 pawn move that weakens g3 before a bishop sacrifice; h4-h5 against ...g6. (g) That pushing pawns near one's own king is safer, or that a luft should always be made early.

### K18. Pawn storms need overwhelming force; opposite-side castling is a race
(b) An attack that depends on pawn advances is justified only if many pieces can be massed behind it before the opponent counterattacks the advanced pawns. If kings castle on opposite wings, pawns are cheap and open lines are the point; speed decides, and the author says the player who gets his attack going first usually wins and passive defence fails. A claim in several notes: the queenside is harder to defend because of its greater expanse. An attack on one wing built on pawn moves often conflicts with an unfinished plan on the other (Games 30, 38). (c) Kings on opposite wings; pieces already aimed; opponent's counterplay slower. (d) The book says such games are hard for beginners and need years of practice (l. 6551-6555). (e) l. 6540-6555 (G2), 7566-7585 (G9), 10817-10850 (G30), 11785-11818 (G38), 9794-9803 (G22). (f) A pawn storm with rooks aimed on a semi-open file; two kings castled opposite. (g) "Always storm the king" or "pawn moves are free."

### K19. The same missing pawn, a different problem in a different phase
(b) Diagrams 97 and 98: with the g-pawn gone, the f3/h3 squares become weak and that is serious in the middlegame, but in endgames the f- and h-pawns remain near the king; with the b-pawn gone, the middlegame is untroubled but the a- and c-pawns lie far from the king and are weak in endgames. The exchange also brings compensation (an open file for rooks, a supported d-pawn break). A weak a-pawn can often be got rid of by advancing it to force an exchange. (c) Where the kings and heavy pieces are; which phase is likely. (d) Compensation depends on file control. (e) l. 6091-6173. (f) Two similar structures differing by one missing pawn. (g) "A weakness is a weakness."

### K20. Open files, the seventh rank, and being first
(b) Once pawn structures are broken, the side that first puts rooks on the open file gets rank 7/8 raids and attacks on pawns from flank and rear; the opponent's pieces are tied to defending and the positional edge is eventually cashed in as a material gain. Cover entry squares (a bishop guarding d7), and give the king a loophole only when the rook must leave the back rank. (c) The file is genuinely open and entry squares are not controlled. (d) A file with no entry square gives no benefit (diagram 13). (e) Ch. VI l. 6174-6256 (Lazard-Ed. Lasker); Ch. III diagram 13; Game 10 l. 7787-7789. (f) Rooks doubling on the b-file against a pawn skeleton of broken pawns. (g) That any open file is good.

### K21. Exchange into a known win; endgame knowledge steers the middlegame
(b) The book says the player must know the endgame to judge exchanges: if ahead, trade into a typical ending that is theoretically won; avoid pawn structures that lose endings; beginners dislike queen exchanges because they think games become dull. Masters of the day simplified at the first hint of a pawn advantage because endgames can be calculated precisely. (c) The resulting ending is known or calculable and the opponent has no counterplay. (d) Rook endings are called the most frequent and most difficult, with extra pawns seldom enough (l. 4816-4820). (e) l. 3294-3320, 4062-4070, 4984-5000, 5583-5600. (f) A material edge with an available exchange sequence into a pawn ending. (g) That exchanging is always safe, or that exchanges kill winning chances.

### K22. Minimum mating force and what cannot be forced
(b) To mate a bare king you must control the four squares around the corner; hence mating forces are queen, rook, two bishops, bishop + knight. Knight + bishop mate only in the corner of the bishop's colour (the book gives the colour-parity argument). Two knights cannot force mate unless the defender has a spare move (a pawn). A queen beats any single piece. A rook generally cannot beat a minor piece. Against a bishop the defender survives by sheltering in a corner of the colour opposite his bishop (the rook then cannot avoid stalemate), but on other edge squares the rook can win (diagrams 46-47); against a knight the rook wins when the knight is cut off from its king, and the defence holds once the king supports the knight (diagram 48 and the lines after it). (c) No pawns (or a defender's spare pawn move). (d) The 50-move rule is not discussed [READ]. (e) Ch. V l. 3306-3626 (diagrams 42-48). (f) Mating-pattern and drawn-ending classification. (g) That two knights mate by force; that a rook beats a bishop.

### K23. King and pawn against king: opposition and the sixth-rank condition
(b) The defender draws if his king commands the queening square and is able to hold or take the opposition at the critical moment. Opposition means kings on the same file or rank with one square between and the *other* side to move. The critical moment is when the pawn reaches its sixth rank; with the pawn further back, back-rank opposition does not save the defender. If the attacking king is more than one rank ahead of the pawn the attacker wins, because a pawn move acts as a spare move that takes the opposition back from the defender. Distant opposition (same-colour squares) is explained. Rook pawn exception: the defender who reaches the corner draws. (c) Pure K+P v K; not a rook pawn. (d) With pieces on board forced-move positions are rare (l. 4480-4488). The book does not state the key-squares formulation or the rule of the square (grep confirmed). (e) l. 3634-3868 (diagrams 49-55), 4143-4184. (f) K+P v K positions. (g) That having the opposition always wins; that opposition means "standing opposite".

### K24. Count each side's moves separately: races, outside passers, majorities
(b) Count moves for each side separately: pawn races, king raids against pawns, whether a king catches a passed pawn. A distant passed pawn wins because it drags the opposing king away, putting the other king two files nearer the pawns; a passed pawn on the opposite wing from the kings does similar work; a one-pawn majority on one wing is converted by exchanging until a passed pawn exists; when all pawns are on one wing, exchanges alone won't do and the opposition matters. A pair of passed pawns on the sixth wins even against a rook. (c) Pawn endings, including checks that force the enemy king to block his own pawn. (d) All pawns on one wing is harder (diagram 57). (e) l. 3891-4070; master endings 1, 3, 6 (l. 5002-5100, 5216-5366, 5504-5568). (f) Race positions with counts like ten moves vs seven. (g) That the first to queen wins.

### K25. Spare moves, forced-move positions and the knight's parity
(b) In tight pawn endings with no spare moves, the side to move loses ground; keeping a spare pawn move in hand decides games (Master ending 1). Diagram 74: a knight reaches any square in only odd or only even numbers of moves, so a knight cannot lose a move, whereas a bishop can (the winning method in diagram 73). (c) Fixed pawns, few pieces. (d) With pieces on the board such positions are infrequent. (e) l. 4079-4140, 4398-4407, 4652-4699, 5026-5100. (f) Mutual-zugzwang type endings (the book says *forced move*; zugzwang is not named). (g) That having the move is good.

### K26. Doubled and isolated pawns are not weak by definition
(b) Doubled pawns are weak when isolated; when supported from an adjacent file they can hold three singles; but if no exchange can create a passed pawn, doubled pawns are a handicap even if not isolated. An isolated pawn ties down a king or piece; a pawn far from its king is weak in endings. Bishop pair or an open file can compensate for doubled pawns in the middlegame (Game 23 note; Game 25 note). (c) Phase and piece activity decide. (d) The book calls the belief that doubled pawns are weak merely by being doubled frequent and erroneous (l. 4252). (e) l. 4243-4373 (diagrams 64-66); Games 4, 23, 25. (f) Pawn-ending pairs differing by one supporting pawn. (g) Doubled pawns are always (or never) bad.

### K27. Bishop and knight endings with pawns; wrong bishop; opposite colours
(b) Rook pawn + bishop of the wrong colour: draw if the defender's king reaches the corner. Same-coloured bishops: an extra pawn usually wins by forcing exchange into a won pawn ending. Opposite-coloured bishops: mostly drawn, even two pawns up, unless two distant passed pawns arise; attacked united pawns should stand on squares of the opponent's bishop's colour. Knight endings: a passed pawn engages both king and knight, leaving one defender (E. Lasker-Rotlewi). (c) Pawns not blockaded; the bishop's colour matters. (d) Wins exist with two connected passers (diagram 77). (e) l. 4531-4807, 5102-5214. (f) Bishop-ending classification. (g) That an extra pawn always wins, or that opposite bishops are always drawn.

### K28. Rook endings: activity over material
(b) Reduce the opposing rook's scope (diagram 78: the stronger rook wins by tying the enemy rook, even a pawn up it takes a specific manoeuvre); the side that places a rook behind the opponent's passed pawn freezes the opposing rook; with one pawn left a defending king commanding the queening square draws; when ahead a pawn, advance pawns on the wing of the extra pawn to create a passed pawn that stretches the opposing rook (Game 40 note, l. 12276-12288). (c) Pawns on both wings, rooks on. (d) Not general theory (no Lucena/Philidor named) [READ; absence]. (e) l. 4816-4893, 12276-12288, 5431-5500. (f) R+P v R positions. (g) That an extra pawn wins rook endings.

### K29. Queen against a far-advanced pawn; stalemate resources
(b) The queen normally beats an advanced pawn even when it is supported by the king; rook and bishop pawns on the seventh can draw if the stronger king is not close, via stalemate; a knight pawn loses; two passed pawns on the sixth beat a rook. (c) The pawn on the seventh with the king near; defender far. (d) Stalemate is the saving device. (e) l. 4894-4981. (f) Q v P positions. (g) That the queen always wins.

### K30. Strategy sets tasks, tactics decides feasibility; no military analogies
(b) The author defines strategy as the problems to be solved and tactics as the means; a sound strategic aim is one tactically possible, so strategy cannot be taught without tactics. Piece-role analogies from war (knights as scouts, rooks as artillery) are rejected as useless. (c) The book's own practice is to justify strategy with concrete variations. (d) None. (e) Ch. III l. 1144-1190. (f) None. (g) "Strategy" and "tactics" as separate subjects.

### K31. Squeeze: restrict the opponent's mobility until he must weaken
(b) In several games the winner leaves the opponent no useful piece moves, so that only pawn moves remain, then a pawn move opens an entry (Games 10, 14, 18). (c) The opponent's pieces are cramped, and the squeezer retains flexible reserve moves. (d) The book never names prophylaxis or restraint and attributes outcomes to mobility. (e) l. 7875-7878, 8526-8531, 9159-9175. (f) A bind with a knight on an advanced square. (g) That only material gain counts as progress.

### K32. Castling timing and the king in the centre
(b) Keep the option of castling rather than committing early, unless the opponent's central plan is known (a point credited to Capablanca in the Game 12 note, l. 8104-8110); do not forfeit castling unless there is no better way to safety; open centre lines punish the uncastled king; *artificial castling* (a king stepping to g2 with pawns advanced) is possible when the wing is dominated (Game 27). (c) Centre open or likely to open. (d) Not a rule; the choice of side matters. (e) Game 12 note; Game 24 note (l. 10050-10058); Game 27 note (l. 10392-10393). (f) Positions with a king stuck in the centre. (g) "Castle as soon as possible, always."

### K33. A plan, even an unsound one, is better than none (contested)
(b) The author says all combinations should serve one leading plan, which is hard to find; an unsound plan carried through is of no value but still better than none; experience builds an instinct to reject hopeless plans. (c) Not stated. (d) [OUTSIDE] Modern coaches would say an unsound plan is worse than an adaptive reply to the opponent's threats. (e) l. 5602-5610. (f) None. (g) Playing plans without checking the opponent's counterplay.

## 5. Claims that are dated, absolute, or contestable

An engine-era reader should treat each of these as hypotheses.

1. **Piece values and the footnote table** (minors equal; R = minor + 1-2 pawns; Q = 2R or 3 minors) [READ, l. 844-850]. Modern working values (about 3/3/5/9) differ at the margins, and the bishop-pair premium may be larger than a bare above-two-knights ordering suggests. [OUTSIDE] Use as a first approximation only.
2. **White morally entitled to attack, Black must defend** (K8). Modern engines show Black equalising routinely; the "entitlement" is only a small first-move edge [OUTSIDE].
3. **Only developing pawn moves; anything else loses a move** (K6). The book's own exceptions (a6 in the Ruy Lopez, h3) show the rule is weaker than stated; modern play uses many non-developing useful moves (prophylactic, space-gaining, waiting) [OUTSIDE]. The unit of "wasted" is hard to define when a piece takes two routes.
4. **Opening verdicts** are 1915 verdicts and several are wrong now [CHECK each]: the King's Gambit accepted as expert-only and the declined line recommended to learners (a teaching judgment, but the accepted gambit is not expert-only); the Danish, Centre and Scotch gambits and the Max Lange treated as out of fashion or refuted (the Max Lange remains a known line); the Rio de Janeiro Defence doubted and the Stonewall said to have practically left tournament play; White's early e5 in the French called premature (the Advance French is a main line); the Caro-Kann Advance judged good for White (the modern Advance Caro-Kann is solid for Black); 1...d5 against 1.e4 advised against on principle (the Scandinavian is playable); the Queen's Gambit Accepted called not commendable (a respected defence); the Ruy Lopez reported as held by many to be White's strongest opening (an opinion, not a finding); and the opening survey omits Nimzo-Indian, King's Indian, Grunfeld, Alekhine's and Slav-type systems entirely [READ: absent from the Table of Openings; OUTSIDE: they exist].
5. **Diagram 17 / Queen's Gambit tactical claim** (a mechanical capture of an offered pawn leads to a bad game because the opponent's bishop-for-knight exchange then brings his queen to a dominating square, winning a pawn) [CHECK]. The idea (opening lines for pieces matters more than removing a pawn) is sound; the specific verdict needs an engine.
6. **Specific variations in the notes** (Rice Gambit footnote; Max Lange attack notes; Ruy Lopez sub-variations; sacrifices in games 7, 9, 13, 20, 22, 29, 43) were analysed by hand in 1914-15; any line used in an item must be engine-verified [CHECK].
7. **Endgame summary claims** [CHECK against tablebases]: rook vs bishop and rook vs knight verdicts (diagrams 46-48), queen vs rook (diagram 45), queen vs advanced pawn (diagram 81 and the A/B/C cases), the distant-opposition sequences (diagrams 62-63), the doubled-pawn endings (diagrams 64-66), the wrong-bishop and bishop-colour cases (diagrams 72-77), and the pawn-ending counts (diagram 59). The principles are standard; the specific diagram claims are only as good as the transcription and the author's analysis.
8. **K+P v K statement** is the opposition rule with a sixth-rank threshold, a simplification of the key-squares method [OUTSIDE]. It is correct for typical cases but leaves out edge cases that a key-squares formulation handles directly.
9. **Bishop vs knight in endings**: the book says in one place that the two are hard to compare, and in a Game 19 note that the knight is generally better in endings (K5). Modern view: depends on structure and where the pawns are [OUTSIDE].
10. **Opposite-side castling**: the claim that the player who attacks first wins, and that the queenside king is harder to defend (K18) is plausible folk-wisdom, not a theorem; it ignores defensive resources and the possibility of timely counterattack [OUTSIDE].
11. **Pawn moves in front of the king always weaken** (K17). True as a caution, absolute as phrased; modern players use h3/a3 and fianchetto structures deliberately [OUTSIDE].
12. **Attack only immovable objects** (K15). Hedged by the author, yet chasing pieces to gain space or tempo is an accepted dynamic idea (e.g. hitting a knight to get it offside) [OUTSIDE].
13. **Preface claim that principles are sufficient** (K14): contradicted at l. 3274-3284 [READ].
14. **An unsound plan is better than none** (K33). Dated coaching advice [OUTSIDE].
15. **Exchanging into the ending when ahead** (K21). Often right; not always (the ending must be checked); 1915 masters' trend is described as a fact without evidence.
16. **The backward pawn definition** (a pawn that cannot move into cover) omits the stop-square control that defines a *weakness*; a backward pawn that can advance safely, or whose stop square the opponent cannot reach, is not a target (K16) [OUTSIDE].
17. **Annotator speculation about time trouble and other causes** (the notes repeatedly guess that a player was short of time) is conjecture, not evidence [READ].
18. **Stage theory of the beginner** (K1) is an unsupported anecdote [OUTSIDE].
19. **Notation and terminology**: descriptive notation; the old open/closed game split (the author notes it has been abandoned, l. 2363-2366); usage of *backward pawn* and *hanging pawn* differs slightly from today.

## 6. Teaching method observed

- **Principles first, then phases, then games** (opening, ending, middlegame, then model games), with the unusual order **endgame before middlegame** justified by the claim that middlegame play must be conducted with the ending in view [READ].
- **Pawn skeleton as the organising device**: each opening is shown through its pawn formation (chains, centre exchanges), then the pieces are placed to suit it (diagrams 29-36) [READ].
- **Diagram-led verdicts**: the author sets a position and gives a verdict with a reason. He names mistakes explicitly (a lost move, a premature advance, an illusory defender). Typical annotation: why the move fits or breaks a stated principle, with the variation that refutes it.
- **Explicit cross-references and recapitulation**: pointers such as compare-Game-12, recapitulation lists (five points at the end of Ch. VI, two principles at the end of Ch. IV).
- **Contrast pairs built into the exposition**: diagrams 56 vs 57 (a majority that wins quickly vs one that needs opposition), 97 vs 98 (same pawn missing, different phase), the Morphy game vs the beginner game, the pinned-knight lines with and without exchanges. These are natural material for contrast families [READ; I].
- **Predict-then-compare**: the translator's preface instructs the student to study each critical diagram, form his own opinion, and then compare with the sequel (l. 257-266). The author himself does not supply exercises or ask questions in the text [READ].
- **Candid use of the author's own games**, including losses (Games 19, 46), and admission of time pressure and oversights [READ].
- **Weak points as pedagogy**: no exercises; no checkpoints; variation-heavy notes in descriptive notation; no summaries of takeaways per game; very long runs of unexplained move lists in the games; opening tables are memory material despite the anti-memorisation stance [READ; I].
- **Suitable reading levels** [I]: Ch. II-III and the closing recap of Ch. IV are accessible to T1-T3 learners; the first half of Ch. V (K+P v K, opposition, counting moves) is directly usable at T2-T3; Ch. VI general remarks are T3-T4; Part II is mostly T4+ except the short development-lead games (e.g. 3, 20, 31, 35) which can serve earlier.

## 8. Candidate exercise and contrast-family ideas

Cue levels follow `content-schema.md`: 1 named, 2 themed set, 3 mixed/unlabeled, 4 in-play. Contrast families give a working case, a failing look-alike and an irrelevant case. No FENs; positions must be built and engine-checked before use.

1. **Square count (cue 2-3).** Position type: a contested square with several attackers and defenders of mixed value. Learner action: decide whether a capture or pawn push loses material, writing both tallies first. Include items where equal head-counts differ in value.
2. **Count lies (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: a count that favours the capturer and holds. Failing look-alike: same count, but one defender is attackable by a cheaper piece. Irrelevant: a square no one can capture on. Learner predicts outcome before moves.
3. **Illusory defender / pinned defender / forced-away defender (cue 2 -> 3).** Three themed sets, then mixed. Learner names which spoiler applies.
4. **Arrival count (cue 3).** Position type: a pawn that could be attacked or supported over the next three moves. Learner lists the units each side can bring and chooses whether to push.
5. **Lost-move audit (cue 2-4).** Position type: two move orders to the same piece placement, one of which wastes a move. Learner picks the efficient order, then plays out against a bot to see the cost. Include items where an extra pawn move *is* justified (a6 in a Ruy Lopez type; a forced luft).
6. **Development lead: sacrifice or not (contrast family, cue 3-4).** Working: opponent uncastled with three or more pieces undeveloped and an open file. Failing look-alike: same sacrifice when the opponent has castled and developed. Irrelevant: closed centre. Learner decides sacrifice / develop.
7. **Offered centre pawn (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: capture and keep a lead because lines open for your pieces. Failing look-alike: the mechanical capture that lets the opponent seize a commanding central square. Irrelevant: an offered wing pawn.
8. **Chase or not (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: a pawn attack on a pinned or trapped piece. Failing look-alike: a pawn kick that lets the piece retreat to an equal square and leaves a backward pawn. Irrelevant: a piece with no good squares anyway.
9. **What does this pawn move leave behind? (cue 2-3).** Position type: a castled king; a pawn move to consider. Learner marks newly weak squares and names which enemy piece can use them. Include positions where the relevant enemy attackers are absent (the move is fine).
10. **Backward pawn (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: fixed backward pawn on a semi-open file attacked by more units than can defend. Failing look-alike: a pawn that can safely advance or is guarded by a same-coloured bishop. Irrelevant: a backward pawn on a file the attacker cannot reach.
11. **Same missing pawn (contrast pair, cue 3).** Two structures differing by one missing pawn (kingside vs queenside). Learner predicts the main danger in the middlegame and in the ending (compare diagrams 97-98).
12. **File with no entry (cue 3).** Working: an open file with a rook entering on the seventh. Failing look-alike: an open file with entry squares covered, so winning the exchange gains nothing. Irrelevant: a closed file.
13. **K+P v K: who holds the opposition? (cue 2 -> 4).** Learner classifies win/draw first, then plays it out against a tablebase bot. Items vary the pawn's rank (pawn on 4th, 5th, 6th with identical king placement) and include the rook-pawn exception and a "distant opposition" item [CHECK all].
14. **Count both races (cue 2-3).** Position type: a pawn race with a king raid; learner counts moves separately for each side, including forced king blocks, then decides.
15. **Outside passed pawn (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: distant passed pawn that drags the enemy king away. Failing look-alike: a passed pawn too close to the kings. Irrelevant: a position with active rooks where the king raid is not available. [CHECK each by tablebase.]
16. **Doubled pawn verdict (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: doubled pawns supported by a neighbour, with bishop pair. Failing look-alike: isolated doubled pawns in an ending with no way to create a passed pawn. Irrelevant: doubled pawns in a position decided by a king attack.
17. **Wrong-bishop fortress (contrast family, cue 3).** Working: rook pawn + wrong-coloured bishop, defender's king reaches the corner. Failing look-alike: same set-up with a right-coloured bishop or a non-rook pawn. Irrelevant: pawn not on the edge. [CHECK.]
18. **Classify the ending (cue 3).** Mixed items: K+B+N (mate in the bishop-colour corner), K+2N (no forced mate), R vs minor (usually draw), Q vs advanced pawn. Learner outputs win/draw and the method. [CHECK with tablebase.]
19. **Predict-then-compare on a model game (GAME.GUESS, cue 4).** Use a short development-lead game (e.g. Game 3, 20, 31 or 35 as a source of move facts). Learner states a plan for each side at three critical positions before seeing the sequel (translator's method).
20. **Rook ending: which side should advance? (cue 3).** Learner chooses which wing to push to create a passed pawn when one pawn up with rooks on.

## 9. Agreements and tensions with other sources and with modern teaching

Only where I know the other work; confidence is stated. I have not read these four sources for this sheet.

- **Capablanca, *Chess Fundamentals* (1921)** [OUTSIDE, moderate confidence]. Agrees: the same development-and-centre principles; teaching the endgame early (I recall that book opens with elementary endings, as Lasker advises); concern about simple, clear play. Likely difference: Capablanca is more economical and avoids long variations; Lasker leans on pawn skeleton and long notes. Neither names key squares in the way modern theory does (Lasker: not named, grep confirmed).
- **Nimzowitsch, *My System*** [OUTSIDE, moderate]. Tension: Nimzowitsch rejects the idea that central control means pawn occupation and develops blockade, restraint and prophylaxis; Lasker's pawn-skeleton/centre material is the pre-hypermodern baseline (Nimzowitsch appears as a *player* in Games 15, 27, 34, 41, 48). Complement: Lasker's French chain diagrams and the idea of which side attacks which wing anticipate Nimzowitsch's pawn-chain teaching; Lasker's backward-pawn technique (fix and pile on) overlaps Nimzowitsch's treatment but lacks the blockade/outpost vocabulary (neither "outpost" nor "prophylaxis" appears in Lasker; grep confirmed).
- **Em. Lasker, *Common Sense in Chess*** [OUTSIDE, low confidence]. Both are principle-based "common sense" manuals; I do not recall details well enough to compare specific claims. Flag as an open question for that sheet.
- **Znosko-Borovsky, *The Middle Game in Chess*** [OUTSIDE, low confidence]. I believe it centres on aims and weaknesses and attacking weak points; if so, K15-K17 (attack the immovable; weaknesses) would agree. Verify when that sheet is done.
- **Modern teaching** [OUTSIDE]. Agrees: development and king safety; endgame competence (opposition, outside passed pawn, wrong bishop); conversion by trading when the ending is won; doubled pawns are not intrinsically bad; pawn structure as the long-term guide. Tensions: engines show many so-called lost-move pawn moves are fine; the first-move initiative is overstated; opening verdicts are dated; modern coaches (Silman, Watson, Dvoretsky) would add an imbalance checklist, stress rule-independence and prophylaxis, and treat dynamic piece play against rules. Within the book the principles are stated in more absolute terms than in our CD-4 conditions-not-rules approach; we should keep the idea and supply the conditions.
- **Council decisions**: CD-4 (contrast families) fits K15-K19, K26; CD-5 (practical vs sound labels) applies to the book's sacrifices; CD-8 (guess-the-move) matches the translator's study advice; CD-6 (endgame spine) matches Ch. V; CD-16 (original text) governs all use.

## 10. Open questions
