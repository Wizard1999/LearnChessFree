# Synthesis sheet: Young, *Chess Generalship*, Vol. I, Grand Reconnaissance (1910)

Labels: [R] recalled from the text I read, [I] my inference, [V] checked here with a tool. Original wording only. Nothing below is true on the book's authority; where Young gives a rule, I say "the author holds".

## 1. Scope and edition

- Franklin K. Young, *Chess Generalship*, Vol. I, Boston, International Publishing Co., 1910, copyright 1910; the file is the Project Gutenberg text (eBook 55278, PGDP transcription of an Internet Archive scan) [R]. First publication 1910 puts the printed work in the US public domain by date [I]; the Gutenberg licence applies to the file, not to our paraphrase.
- Read: the preface, introduction, and the whole chess-bearing body (the opening essay, Grand Reconnaissance, Organization, Topography, Mobility, Numbers, Time, Position, Prime Strategetic Means, Prime Strategetic Proposition). Skimmed, not read closely: the "Military Examples" sections after each chapter (Greek, Roman, Napoleonic, Boer War and American Revolution campaigns) and the long run of quotations from Frederick, Napoleon and others. See section 8.
- Format: no diagrams and no games. The volume contains no chess game, no annotated position and no move sequence that I found [R]. Chess content is definitions, tables and rules. It is Volume I of a series; the "Prime Strategetic Proposition" is announced in three phases and only the first (and part of the second section) appears here [R].

## 2. What the book teaches, in order

1. Chess is war; study the masters, trust fixed principles, distrust "analysis" of variations as unreliable when facts are unknown [R].
2. **Grand Reconnaissance**: before acting, compare the two armies in six respects: time, numbers, position, organization, mobility, topography. The commander must do this personally, not delegate it. Do not act on guesses; judge the position as it is, not as it should be [R].
3. **Organization**: each piece is an equal "corps" but with different build; a table of "potential complements" (king 6 9/16, queen 22 1/4, rook 14, bishop 8 3/4, knight 5 1/4, pawn 1 1/2 units) counts squares reachable. Cancel equal pieces, compare what remains, and pick a battlefield that suits the better-organized piece. It is explicitly not a measure of strength [R].
4. **Topography**: each piece sees the board as a different landscape; a piece's "weakness" is the set of enemy pieces that can hit it without being hit back. Rule on colour: with enemy pawns on one colour and yours on the other, the side with more pieces that act on that colour has the topographical edge [R].
5. **Mobility**: the aim of every move is to cut the enemy's mobility and raise your own; claims about the long central diagonal (36 squares for you, 28 for him); proposed reforms of castling and of check by a pinned piece [R].
6. **Numbers**: the side with more men is stronger; all men count equally (a pawn "counts" as much as a queen) [R].
7. **Time**: absolute time is moving while the opponent is frozen; the initiative is forcing him by threats [R].
8. **Position**: a three-term syllogism of attack (A), support (S) and manoeuvre (M): attack = who holds the better front; support = pawn assets such as passed pawns and a majority far from the enemy king; manoeuvre = who is nearer to queening. Twenty-seven combinations tabulated [R].
9. **Prime means and proposition**: combine the six to choose a policy (concentrate force against a wing, attrit if ahead in numbers, keep material and use ground if behind), then a three-step formula for the first move direction: time says who moves, numbers say the motive (exchange or split the defender), position says the direction [R].

## 3. Ideas with conditions and exceptions

- **Look before you plan.** List the differences between the sides in a fixed set of categories before choosing a plan [R]. Condition: the lists must be of real features; Young warns against optimism about your own position and underrating the enemy [R]. Exception (the book's own): none given. [I] A fixed list is a checklist, not a plan; the book never says how to turn the comparison into a concrete target.
- **Defender reacts to the enemy's movements; the attacker is guided by the enemy's position** [R]. [I] This is a reasonable summary of why to wait for the opponent to commit, but Young also scorns waiting moves, so he contradicts himself [R].
- **Plan around the strongest asset** (best-built piece, more numerous force, better ground, nearer pawn) [R]. Condition: the other factors should be at least equal [R].
- **Policy by material**: the side ahead should trade and offer battle; the side behind should avoid loss, avoid creating indefensible points, and fight only where ground favours it [R]. Exceptions are not stated; [I] "trade when ahead" lacks the usual qualifications (which trades, which pieces).
- **Attack on one wing with the rest held back, rather than frontal assault** [R]. Illustrated only by military cases.
- **Support and manoeuvre as separate jobs** (pawn majority/passed pawns versus the nearest runner) [R]. [I] This is the only part that reads like an endgame/pawn-race plan.
- **Make forcing threats** to limit the opponent's choice [R].

## 4. Examples

None in chess. Positions listed: only the starting array, used to argue that the first move is the "absolute time" advantage [R]. Games listed: none. The military illustrations (Leuctra, Cannae, Metaurus, Austerlitz, Torgau, Yorktown, the Boer War newspaper forecasts) are history, not chess evidence, and are not usable as items.

## 5. Mapping to skill nodes (ids exist in content/skills)

- POS.PLAN: the six-factor comparison is a loose ancestor of "making a plan you can carry out" [I].
- POS.PLAN.DIFF (the key difference between the two sides): the closest match; Young's list of differences in time, numbers, position, organization, mobility, topography is a coarser version of the difference method [I].
- POS.IMBALANCES: same list idea, but with Young's categories rather than the modern ones (minor pieces, structure, king safety, space, initiative) [I].
- POS.PLAN.MODE (mode follows the evaluation): Young's "ahead trades, behind avoids loss" is one half; he has no "equal" mode [I].
- POS.PLAN.RACE (racing plans): the manoeuvre term (nearest pawn runner) and counting moves to promote [I].
- POS.PLAN.PIECES (allocating pieces): the wing-attack and support/manoeuvre column idea [I].
- POS.VALUES: Young counts all pieces as equal in number and offers square-reach units; this is a foil, not support [I].
- POS.MAJORITY and POS.PASSED: the support term (passed pawns, majority away from the enemy king) [R].
- POS.BISHOPS and POS.OUTPOST: only as the colour-topography rule, weakly [I].
- ATK.SUPERIORITY (tangible superiority before attacking): numbers-plus-position idea; Young says concentrate first, strike where the defender is outnumbered [I].
- DEF.TRADES: policy for the side behind; loosely [I].
- SELF.1 (observe): the "do not act until you have looked at everything" habit, loosely [I].

## 7. Outdated or doubtful claims (flag; do not teach as fact)

- **Square-reach "potential complements" as the measure of organization.** Unchecked as a strength measure; also internally uneven. I checked mobility counts on an empty board with python-chess [V]: queen 21 to 27 squares (the text gives 21 as the minimum in one place and 20 for its three-valley class in another [R]); rook always 14 (matches); bishop 7 to 13 (the author gives 9 as the minimum, which is wrong: a corner bishop reaches 7); knight 2 to 8 (matches); a knight needs at most 6 moves to reach any square (matches). Squares reached do not equal value.
- **"All men count equally; a queen is no stronger than a pawn."** Contradicts piece values and the whole of POS.VALUES. Treat as wrong.
- **"White should win with exact play."** Stated as likely by the author [R]. Unproven; not checked, and the project does not claim it.
- **Pinned piece cannot give check.** The author argues at length that it cannot [R]. Under the current rules (FIDE Laws) a pinned piece does give check and the opposing king may not move into its attack; I did not run a test but the rule is standard [I]. Do not teach the book's version.
- **Castling reform** (king to the knight file, rook to the bishop file) and other proposals to change the rules: not part of the game; ignore.
- **"Chessboard is a quadrant of a circle", "three sides equal, so geometry is broken"**: a numerological argument from counting squares; invalid geometry [I]. The derived claim that holding the long central diagonal gives 36 versus 28 squares is unchecked; the numbers are arithmetic on a diagram I cannot see, and the claim as a general rule is an author's view, not tested.
- **Distrust of variation analysis** ("none can be relied on"): contradicted by modern engine verification and the project's own rule 1. Do not adopt.
- **"No waiting moves, no prophylaxis"** [R]: conflicts with DEF.WAIT and POS.PROPHYLAXIS content. Author's view only.
- **Named "ambuscades", "formation by wings", "grand columns"** and the 27 syllogism codes: vocabulary with no evidence that it predicts results. Not engine-tested.
- **Great-man and cultural claims** (only eleven generals understood strategy; talent is innate; Morphy is the model): unsupported rhetoric, and one that cuts against the project's premise that planning can be taught.

## 8. What I did not read, or did not check

- The "Military Examples" blocks after Organization, Topography, Mobility, Numbers, Time and Position, and the Boer War newspaper section, I skimmed for headings and chess mentions only; the chess-bearing paragraphs in them (for example one passage about a conqueror playing chess during a battle) were seen but not studied.
- The first half of the opening essays (the Ruskin, Saxe, Frederick quotations) I read for argument, not for detail.
- I did not engine-check any claim about the central diagonal, about attrition when ahead, or about the "first move wins" idea. No positions are given, so there is nothing to feed validate.py.
- Terms like "Grand Vertex", "Point en Potence", "Corps en Crochet" are defined in Young's earlier books (Minor Tactics, Grand Tactics) which I did not have; I cannot say what they mean precisely. Volume II (Grand Manoeuvres) is not in the file.

## 9. Verdict for the planning nodes

- Value for POS.PLAN.*: low as content, moderate as a contrast. The book is a good exhibit of the failure mode "a framework of categories without a method for choosing a move": the comparison step is rich, the step from comparison to a concrete plan is only a formula with no worked chess example.
- If any lesson draws on it, use it as a historical note or as an unlabeled-comparison exercise (find the difference list yourself), never as a source of claims. Every claim in a lesson still needs its own check.
